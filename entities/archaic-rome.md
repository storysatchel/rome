# Archaic Rome

Archaic Rome was the early city whose defensive growth can be traced, imperfectly, through sixth- and early fifth-century BCE fortifications. Their surviving stretches suggest a community extending across several hills, although neither the precise plan of a continuous circuit nor a single construction date can be established.

## A wall built over time

Even if a complete circuit existed early in Rome’s history, it need not have been the product of one building campaign. The surviving evidence allows for separate initiatives extending over a century or more, from the sixth into the fifth century BCE. Some portions of a proposed archaic circuit could therefore have been completed only during the Republic.

An early wall circuit should consequently not be assigned a single precise date merely because its remains are discussed as one system. The available evidence leaves the chronology as uncertain as the overall plan.

## What the walls establish

Cappellaccio stone walls on the northern Quirinal, eastern Esquiline, and western Capitoline are generally accepted as early urban defences. Their position along outer hill edges, rather than complete circuits around each individual hill, points to protection of a larger community. Proposed Aventine sections remain less secure.

The ritual boundary, or *pomerium*, must not automatically be equated with this defensive perimeter. Burials and sanctuaries follow religious restrictions, so their locations cannot alone settle the route of a military wall.

Even a discontinuous defensive system implies a substantial settlement territory. The source estimates at least 265 hectares within the Quirinal, Esquiline, and Capitoline traces, rising to about 426 hectares if the Caelian and Aventine are included. These are reconstructions of protected area, not measurements of continuous dense habitation.
