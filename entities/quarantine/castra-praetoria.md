# Castra Praetoria

The Castra Praetoria was the praetorian camp at Rome. Its architecture shows both a superficial resemblance to conventional Roman military installations and features that caution against reconstructing it from a standard fort plan.

## Roads and defences

Two principal roads crossed the camp at right angles, bisecting its internal layout in a manner familiar from forts and fortresses. The account in *Praetorian* nevertheless emphasizes that the resemblance did not extend to the plan as a whole.

The north wall preserves evidence of alteration. An original crenellated wall, 3.35 metres high, was raised to 5 metres by filling the gaps between the crenellations and building upward. The surviving wall therefore incorporates more than one phase of defensive construction.

## A camp fitted to the city

The enclosure measured roughly 440 by 380 metres, about 16.72 hectares. Its southern side projected outward where the camp fitted between converging roads, creating an irregular five-sided footprint rather than a symmetrical rectangle. Excavated barracks had at least two storeys, and accommodation cells were built into the walls.

The incomplete plan prevents a reliable total capacity calculation. The source estimates a minimum of ten to fifteen thousand men, while acknowledging uncertainties about units housed there and the location of stables. Claudian coins show what may be a headquarters building behind the walls, but its identification on the ground remains unresolved.

## An unusual defensive perimeter

The brick-faced walls had a concrete core and up to sixteen non-projecting towers. No external ditch has been identified, an unusual absence in a Roman military installation. Incorporation into the Aurelian Walls preserved parts of the enclosure. After Constantine abolished the Guard, its south and west sides were demolished and remaining gates blocked, turning the former camp’s fabric into evidence of an institutional break.
