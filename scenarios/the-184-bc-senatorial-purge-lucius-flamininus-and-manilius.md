# Cato's 184 BC Senate Purge: The Flamininus Execution Scandal and the Manilius Kiss

During his censorship of 184 BC, Cato the Elder and his colleague L. Valerius Flaccus conducted a review of the Senate (*lectio senatus*) and expelled seven men as unworthy. The two most memorable cases were former consul Lucius Quinctius Flamininus, accused in Cato's most vehement censorial speech of having a man executed at a banquet merely to please a paramour, and a senator named "Manilius," expelled — according to Plutarch — for kissing his wife in broad daylight with their daughter watching.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Cato the Elder** ([[entities/cato-the-elder]]) — goal: enforce strict public and private morality as censor, consolidate reputation for *severitas*; resources: censorial authority, oratorical skill, political alliances.
- **Lucius Valerius Flaccus** ([[entities/lucius-valerius-flaccus]]) — goal: support Cato's censorial program; resources: co-equal censorial power, patrician standing.
- **Lucius Quinctius Flamininus** ([[entities/lucius-quinctius-flamininus]]) — goal: retain senatorial status and reputation; resources: consular rank, his brother Titus's political support.
- **Titus Quinctius Flamininus** ([[entities/titus-quinctius-flamininus]]) — goal: defend his brother's honor; resources: immense prestige as "liberator of Greece," network in the Senate.
- **"Manilius"** ([[entities/manilius-expelled-senator]]) — goal: preserve his reputed strong prospects for the consulship; resources: unclear, possibly praetorian rank.

## What Happened

In the most vehement of his censorial speeches, Cato charged that while campaigning in Cisalpine Gaul, Lucius Flamininus had carried out an execution — by his own hand or through a lictor — purely to gratify the whim of a companion, probably a boy named Philippus. Most versions describe the victim as a criminal already condemned to death; Livy, however, says Cato's own speech identified the victim as a Boian chieftain who had come seeking Roman protection. Lucius, backed by his brother Titus, denied the charge, and Cato concluded his oration with a challenge to a formal judicial contest over the facts — a challenge Lucius declined to take up. ^[extracted]

Separately, Plutarch records that another of the seven expelled senators, a man called "Manilius" (possibly a corruption of "Manlius"), was thought to have strong prospects of reaching the consulship, yet was expelled because he had kissed his wife in open daylight with their daughter present. Cato reportedly followed the charge with a joke that he himself never embraced his own wife except during a thunderstorm, and that he counted himself happy whenever Jupiter thundered. ^[extracted] Ancient and modern commentators differ on whether this was a serious charge, a minor item lumped in with graver unrecorded complaints, or essentially a scornful joke misreported as a literal reason for expulsion. ^[ambiguous]

## Outcome

Both men lost their Senate seats in the 184 BC *lectio senatus*. Lucius Flamininus did not successfully clear his name, and the affair helped fuel Titus Flamininus's subsequent retaliation against Cato over the public works contracts later that same censorship (see [[scenarios/the-184-bc-public-contracts-dispute]]). The identity of "Manilius" and the full substance of his offense remain unresolved in the sources.

## Open Questions

- Whether Lucius Flamininus's victim was an already-condemned criminal or an innocent Boian chieftain is disputed between Livy and other ancient accounts. ^[ambiguous]
- "Manilius's" true identity and whether the kissing charge was literal or a retrojected joke are both uncertain. ^[ambiguous]
