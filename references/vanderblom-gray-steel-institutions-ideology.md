# Institutions and Ideology in Republican Rome — Speech, Audience and Decision

Edited by Henriette van der Blom, Christa Gray, and Catherine Steel (Cambridge University Press, 2018), this volume collects sixteen chapters by an international group of Roman historians, originating from a 2014 London conference held under the European Research Council–funded *Fragments of the Roman Republican Orators* (FRRO) project. The book's central thesis is that Roman Republican politics cannot be explained by institutional rules alone, nor by ideology alone, but operated through the constant interaction of argument, speaker, delivery and institutional setting.

## Core Argument / Thesis

The editors argue that the "institutional" (rules and structures — assemblies, magistrates, the Senate, law, religious authority, elections, military command) and the "ideological" (political beliefs about how the world is and should be) dimensions of Roman Republican politics are not separable analytical categories but are "entirely enmeshed": politicians did not first form ideological goals and then execute them within a neutral institutional framework — institutions like the tribunate of the plebs were themselves constituted around an ideological purpose (promoting plebeian interests against the patrician minority). Against the long-dominant Mommsen-derived "institutional" tradition and the Syme/Gelzer/Münzer prosopographical tradition that downplays the role of belief, the volume argues ideology was a genuine, if informally organized (no political "parties" existed), force in Republican politics.

## Key Claims and Evidence

- Part I ("Modes of Political Communication") examines the *contio* as the central but precarious venue of elite-mass political communication: Yakobson argues the *contio* was "always a place of danger" for elite speakers' dignity, even as it generated consensus; Tiersch analyzes *optimates* and *populares* as a "semantic battle" in which conservative orators tried (ultimately unsuccessfully) to exclude *popularis* claims from legitimate political vocabulary; Rosillo-López shows non-elite Romans nonetheless maintained high awareness of senior politicians' public identities despite the absence of party structures; Clark argues Cicero's oratory is the fullest surviving evidence for how speakers invoked the gods in political argument.
- Part II ("Political Alliances") treats *amicitia*-based alliances as informal but consequential political institutions: Pina Polo shows the *contio* format itself encouraged cooperative alliances, since only a magistrate could summon a *contio*, requiring non-magistrates to secure a friendly magistrate's invitation to speak; Santangelo argues Theophanes of Mytilene's importance to Pompey was political as much as cultural, as a discreet confidential agent (paralleled by Balbus for Caesar); Valachova argues a cluster of Epicureans among Caesar's associates shared a view of friendship well-suited to Caesar's use of warm personal loyalty as a political tool; Morrell reinterprets Cato's and Pompey's uneasy 52 BC cooperation after Clodius's murder as a shared commitment to civic order overriding mutual suspicion.
- Part III ("Institutions in Theory and Practice") stresses the gap between nominally rule-governed institutions and considerable practical flexibility: Driediger-Murphy examines how participants in the 55 BC dispute over Crassus's ominous departure invoked tradition while innovating tactically; Clemente traces the censor's *lectio senatus* (selection of senators) as itself a once-radical innovation that helped legitimate the patricio-plebeian nobility; Haimson Lushkov shows Cicero's *Pro Murena* packaged the institution of election itself around a simplified binary (soldier vs. obscurantist lawyer); Frolov distinguishes the terms *contio* and *coetus* as marking a real institutional difference (a magistrate-summoned legitimate meeting vs. other gatherings) that authors could nonetheless blur rhetorically to delegitimize opponents; Flower examines the *consilium* as a magistrate's private advisory body that nonetheless adopted public, Senate-like formal features including set-piece speeches.
- Part IV ("Memory and Reputation") treats how politicians built reputations for posterity: Jewell shows family *exempla* in oratory simplified and foregrounded a distinctive service-trait within a gens, used to argue the speaker could replicate it; Eckert traces Sulla's novel self-presentation as *felix* ("fortunate") becoming both an imitable model (for Caesar) and a later yardstick for political leaders; Stone argues Gaius Verres, though comprehensively vilified by Cicero's prosecution, had a genuine, reconstructable counter-reputation defended by Hortensius, and that his downfall reflected immediate Roman political concerns more than any coherent Roman provincial-governance policy.
- The volume's two recurrent, cross-cutting arguments: (1) a constant tension between an institution's existing format and the ever-present possibility of innovation, legitimated retroactively by audience acceptance rather than strictly by precedent; (2) Republican institutional complexity (forty-plus new magistrates per year, competing venues, multiple paths to authority) resists collapse into a single binary (people vs. Senate, *populares* vs. conservatives). ^[inferred] (synthesized across all four parts by the editors in the Introduction.)

## Structure / Scope

Covers the Roman Republic broadly, with concentration on the late Republic (effectively Gracchi to the Second Triumvirate, 133–43 BC); draws on Cicero's speeches and letters as the single richest body of evidence, supplemented heavily by fragmentary non-Ciceronian oratory (the volume grew directly out of the FRRO project cataloguing these fragments), Sallust, Plutarch, Appian, Cassius Dio, and Asconius. Sixteen chapters organized into four thematic parts (Modes of Political Communication; Political Alliances; Institutions in Theory and Practice; Memory and Reputation), bookended by an editorial Introduction and full bibliography/index.

## Significance

The volume positions itself explicitly against two poles in the historiography of [[concepts/contio|contio]]-based scholarship: Fergus Millar's "democratic" reading of the Republic (in which the *contio*'s centrality demonstrates real popular sovereignty) and the skeptical counter-readings of Hölkeskamp (the *contio* as elite image-building ritual) and Mouritsen (the *contio* crowd as unrepresentative and politically marginal). It cites Robert Morstein-Marx's work on contional "ideological monotony" directly, noting his later work (2013, 2014) has moved from that earlier position — signaling this volume's attempt to find a middle path that treats ideology as real without overstating the *contio*'s democratic credentials. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- The editors themselves flag that applying the term "ideology" — grounded in twentieth-century political debates — to Republican Rome risks implying a more organized, programmatic phenomenon than existed, especially for non-elite political thought; they proceed with the term while acknowledging this tension explicitly rather than resolving it. ^[ambiguous]
- Several chapters (e.g. Frolov on *contio*/*coetus*) note that usage in the sources is not always a reliable guide to objective institutional fact versus rhetorical framing by a hostile or partisan author.
- A re-scour pass surfaced one discrete incident from Flower's Part III chapter on family *consilia*: [[scenarios/servilias-consilium-at-antium]], the June 44 BC family council at which Servilia, Brutus, Cassius, and Cicero debated whether the conspirators should leave Italy after Caesar's assassination.
