# Cato v. Lucius Furius: The Illegal Water Diversion Case

During their censorship of 184 BC, Cato the Elder and L. Valerius Flaccus ordered that buildings erected over the public water system be demolished within thirty days and moved to shut off water illegally diverted from public channels to private houses and land. One target of this crackdown was a man named Lucius Furius, against whom Cato delivered the speech *In L. Furium de aqua* ("Against Lucius Furius, concerning the water").

## Parties & Stakes

- **Cato the Elder** ([[entities/cato-the-elder]]) — goal: enforce public control of the water supply and punish speculative abuse of it; resources: censorial authority to cut off water and levy fines.
- **Lucius Furius** ([[entities/lucius-furius-de-aqua]]) — goal: retain the value of land he had purchased expecting to irrigate it; resources: capital to buy land at a premium, presumed local influence to arrange the diversion.

## What Happened

Cato alleged that Furius had paid an unusually high price for a plot of land in the expectation that he would be able to irrigate it using water illegally diverted from the public supply — essentially land speculation banking on utility theft. The surviving fragments of Cato's speech repeatedly invoke the phrase *de multa* ("concerning the fine"), indicating that Cato did not merely cut off Furius's illegal supply but also had him formally fined. ^[extracted]

## Outcome

Furius's diverted water supply was shut off and he was fined, per the fragments of Cato's speech. No record survives of further retaliation or appeal, unlike the contemporaneous backlash from the public contracts dispute (see [[scenarios/the-184-bc-public-contracts-dispute]]).

## Open Questions

- Furius's precise identity is unresolved; some scholars tentatively link him to L. Furius Purpurio (cos. 196), but Astin cautions against over-hasty identification. ^[ambiguous]
- The exact size of the fine and the location of the land are not recorded in the surviving fragments.
