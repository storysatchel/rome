# Stages of Political Violence in the Fall of the Roman Republic

A four-stage interpretation of Republican collapse traces murder, then intimidation, then civil war, then autocracy, each stage "a generation" apart. The dated episodes give this compressed escalation model specific anchors, but the framework remains an interpretation rather than an inevitable sequence.

## Key Ideas

- **Stage 1 — political killing (133 BC).** The mob killing of Tiberius Gracchus (tribune 133 BC, son of [[entities/quarantine/tiberius-sempronius-gracchus-cos-177-163|Ti. Sempronius Gracchus, cos. 177/163]]) and 200–300 supporters on the Capitoline marks the opening threshold. Plutarch calls it the "first outbreak of civil strife in Rome that resulted in the bloodshed and murder of citizens since the expulsion of the kings." Cicero, writing a lifetime later, recalled that "the whole manner of his tribunate divided one people into two factions." On this interpretation, the killing, not just the land law, broke precedent.
- **Stage 2 — armed intimidation of assemblies and elections ("within a generation").** Gaius Gracchus's death under the first [[concepts/senatus-consultum-ultimum|senatus consultum ultimum]] (121 BC) and [[entities/lucius-appuleius-saturninus|Saturninus]] and Glaucia's armed gangs contesting the consular elections of 100 BC mark a shift from a single mob action to organized, recurring intimidation of voting.
- **Stage 3 — civil war ("within two generations").** Publius Sulpicius Rufus's recruitment of roughly 3,000 armed supporters in 88 BC to force through Marius's command against Mithridates was followed by Sulla's unprecedented march of his legions on Rome to reverse a domestic political defeat. The model connects this rupture with the Marius–Cinna reprisals of the mid-80s, Sulla's return and proscriptions (82–81 BC), and ultimately Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon and civil war against Pompey (49–45 BC). Spartacus's revolt (73–71 BC) belongs to the intervening history of armed disorder, but was a slave war rather than the same kind of contest between Roman commanders.
- **Stage 4 — autocracy ("two generations after that").** The proscriptions of 43 BC under the second triumvirate, Philippi (42 BC), Actium (31 BC), and Octavian's assumption of the name Augustus (27 BC) mark the final phase. One explanation holds that accumulated exhaustion with unpunished disorder converted into active demand for one-man rule rather than passive acceptance of it.
- Autocracy did not arrive through a single clean transition. During a grain-shortage riot under Augustus, the Roman population demanded that he accept the title of dictator outright. Cassius Dio's remark that order held "because they were afraid" can be read as evidence that even autocratic Rome continued to contest how much self-rule to surrender.
- This four-stage structure is a rhetorical and explanatory device rather than an independently established neutral periodization. An alternative institutional chronology centres on the dictatorship's lapse after 217 BC rather than a generational count from 133 BC. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- The "generation" intervals (133→~100s→~88–44→27 BC) are rhetorically clean but not uniform in actual years (roughly 33, then up to 44, then 17–23 years). Whether "generation" functions as a literal demographic unit or a narrative device remains uncertain. ^[ambiguous]
- The stage-3 threshold could be dated either to Sulpicius's armed supporters or Sulla's march on Rome in the same year, 88 BC: domestic intimidation escalating to organized force and military force entering the city were two connected but distinct developments. ^[ambiguous]
