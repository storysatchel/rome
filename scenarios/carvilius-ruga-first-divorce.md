# The Divorce of Spurius Carvilius Ruga — Rome's First No-Fault Divorce

Roman jurists remembered the divorce of Spurius Carvilius, surnamed Ruga, as a landmark case: according to Aulus Gellius (citing the jurist Servius Sulpicius's treatise on dowries), for roughly five hundred years after Rome's founding there had been no actions or formal guarantees (*cautiones*) concerning dowry restitution in Rome or Latium, "because none were needed, since in those days no marriages were ever dissolved." The jurists dated the first exception to Spurius Carvilius Ruga, who divorced his wife because she could not bear him children due to a physical defect (*corporis vitio*) — sources disagree on the exact year, with ancient authorities variously placing it between 234 and 227 BCE.

## Parties & Stakes

- **[[entities/spurius-carvilius-ruga|Spurius Carvilius Ruga]]** — divorced his wife on grounds of her apparent infertility, despite her having committed no fault; needed a formal mechanism to return her dowry since Roman law otherwise let a husband retain a guilty wife's dowry.
- **His unnamed wife** — divorced through no fault of her own; her case established the legal necessity of a dowry-restitution guarantee (*cautio rei uxoriae*) for faultless divorces.

## What Happened

Because the wife had done nothing wrong, Roman custom (which allowed a husband to keep the dowry of a wife divorced for misconduct) could not justify withholding her property; Carvilius's divorce therefore forced jurists to formalize a procedure and guarantee for returning a blameless wife's dowry — the origin, per the jurist Servius Sulpicius, of the *cautiones rei uxoriae*. The formula associated with the proceeding — *tuas res tibi habeto* ("take your things for yourself") — became the standard Roman verbal formula for divorce.

## Outcome

The case became the textbook example cited by Roman jurists (via Aulus Gellius, *Noctes Atticae* IV.3) of the first "blameless" divorce and the practical origin of dowry-restitution law, even though Marquardt notes ancient sources could not agree on its exact date (proposed years range from 234 to 227 BCE, with some authors incorrectly dating it even earlier).

## Open Questions

- The precise year of the divorce is inconsistently reported by the ancient sources Marquardt collates (Plutarch, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Gellius, Valerius Maximus, Tertullian each differ). ^[ambiguous]
- Whether this was genuinely Rome's very first divorce, or merely the first to require a dowry-restitution procedure due to being fault-free, is left unresolved. ^[ambiguous]
