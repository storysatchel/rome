# Mark Antony's Election Feud With Dolabella

Julius Caesar wished P. Cornelius Dolabella to be elected consul for 44 BC alongside Mark Antony, intending to leave both in charge while he campaigned against Parthia. Antony, however, had personal reasons to resent Dolabella: he had divorced his wife Antonia on grounds of her adultery with Dolabella. Unable to voice this grievance openly, Antony resorted to his religious office of augur to obstruct his rival's political advancement.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Mark Antony** ([[entities/mark-antony]]) — goal: block or discredit Dolabella's election out of personal animus while appearing to act on religious grounds; resources: consular authority over elections, the augurate.
- **P. Cornelius Dolabella** ([[entities/publius-cornelius-dolabella]]) — goal: secure the consulship Caesar had promised him; resources: Caesar's backing.

## What Happened

When the senate met at [[entities/rome|Rome]] on 1 January 45 BC to discuss Caesar's wish to have Dolabella elected, Dolabella spoke first, after which Antony threatened to invalidate the election by claiming unfavorable auspices in his capacity as augur — a tactic Cicero later mocked as self-defeating, since Antony as consul could have obstructed the election in that capacity too, without needing the augural pretext. When the electoral assembly for Dolabella's candidacy convened at the [[entities/campus-martius|Campus Martius]] shortly after, Antony, presiding as consul in charge of elections, let the voting proceed but then adjourned the meeting ("*alio die*" — "another day") at the end, casting doubt on the election's validity without outright blocking it. The standoff stretched over several months. Cicero records that Antony's stated grounds — constitutional concern — rang hollow, since Antony's own path to the consulship had been very similar to Dolabella's; the real motive, Cicero implies, was personal: Dolabella's affair with the divorced Antonia.

## Outcome

After Caesar's assassination in March 44 BC, both Antony and the anti-Caesarian faction in the senate approved Dolabella's election, since both sides needed stability and Dolabella's cooperation in the chaotic aftermath. Antony's earlier augural obstruction was thus abandoned once it no longer served his interests — exactly the opportunistic use of religious authority for political ends that Cicero accused him of throughout.

## Open Questions

- Antony's personal grudge is Cicero's own explanation (in the hostile *Philippics*) and may be an oversimplification of a more complex political calculation.
