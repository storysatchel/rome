# Roman Commercial Credit and Household Partnership

Bang's Chapter 5 situates Roman commercial finance within a broader comparative pattern of "bazaar" credit (drawing on Mughal India, medieval Italy, and the Islamic and Jewish Mediterranean) in which the extended household, not the bank or the independent firm, is the basic unit of trading capital.

## Key Ideas

- **The household (*oikos*), not the bank, was the foundation of trading capital.** Deposit banks existed and offered real financial services — money-changing, assaying, giro transfers, short-term loans — but operated on a small scale and were chronically vulnerable to insolvency; they did not constitute the base of commercial capital. That base came from devolving a share of extended-household wealth onto junior relatives, freedmen, or slaves who supplied labor and risk in exchange for a share of profit — directly paralleled by the Mughal Jain merchant Banarasidas's autobiography, which repeatedly shows him raising trading capital from family rather than lenders.
- **Societas was a flexible, non-corporate partnership form.** Roman *societas* could cover a single venture or a merchant's whole business, but legally it was purely consensual — it dissolved automatically if any partner withdrew consent or died, and (except in unusual cases) it was not treated as an entity independent of its partners, only as a set of internal obligations between them. Bang reads this not as a deficiency relative to the "modern firm" but as a feature suited to bazaar uncertainty: a merchant could be sole trader one year and in partnership the next, diversifying across different lines of trade or household capital pools simultaneously. The Monte Testaccio amphora-stamp evidence (e.g. the "DD. Caeciliorum," documented trading individually in AD 149 and jointly by AD 154) shows exactly this kind of fluid, mixed-status (freeborn/freedman/slave) partnership switching in practice.
- **Roman jurists recognized a true commenda-equivalent inside societas law**, without giving it a separate legal name: a partnership where one party supplies capital and the other supplies labor, profits are shared by agreement, but losses fall entirely on the capital-providing partner. Bang treats this as functionally identical to the medieval Italian *commenda* (typically a 3:1 or 2:1 capital/profit split) and to equivalent asymmetrical finance instruments independently attested in Islamic, Jewish, Byzantine, and even older Mesopotamian trade — evidence, following van Leur, Udovitch, and Goitein, that this is a generic household-economy adaptation to risk and mobility, not a specifically "proto-capitalist" Italian innovation (contra Max Weber's reading of commenda as a first step toward the separation of firm from household).
- **The *peculium* was a delegation tool, not a limited-liability device.** A *peculium* was a fund a *paterfamilias* granted to a dependant (son or slave) to trade with — structurally similar to a commenda in effect (Plautus' *Mercator* shows a father outfitting his son's trading voyage this way). Bang explicitly corrects a common modern misreading: the Digest shows the master was liable *in full* (*in solidum*), not capped at the peculium's value, whenever he had authorized the transaction or placed the dependant in charge of a shop or business (via the *actio institoria*/*exercitoria*). Liability was capped at the peculium's size only when the dependant acted purely on his own initiative, without authorization — and even then, a documented ruling (Dig., Paul, 14.5.8) shows a master held fully liable because his broader pattern of underwriting the slave's various dealings made the emperor conclude he had "in everything" treated the slave as acting in his name. The real function of the peculium was protecting masters from *unauthorized* acts, not shielding them from authorized business risk. ^[extracted]
- **Bottomry loans (*fenus nauticum*) transferred voyage risk to the lender in exchange for very high interest.** If the ship was lost, the loan was void; surviving voyages typically carried interest of 15–30%. Lenders commonly sent their own representative (often a slave) to accompany the borrower and monitor compliance with the loan's terms — documented in a case from the jurist Scaevola (a slave, Eros, sent from Berytus to supervise a borrower's round trip to Brundisium) and, at a vastly larger scale, in a papyrus recording a ~7-million-sesterce bottomry loan financing a round-trip to Muziris in India, with the lender's agents stationed at warehouses in Coptos and Alexandria to seal the cargo at each stage. A mid-range example — a joint loan of roughly 47,160 drachmas to four merchants and shipowners from Askalon — shows the same instrument scaling down to ordinary Mediterranean grain-and-goods trade.
- **The Sulpicii archive (Puteoli) documents real short-term, high-security lending.** Freedmen bankers recorded loans to grain traders secured by goods worth several times the loan's face value (e.g. 13,000 sesterces secured against goods worth 3–4x that sum), repayable on demand or within a couple of months — mirroring the short-duration, high-collateral pattern Bang also documents for early modern Indian bazaar credit. The bank's own business network ran through freedman ties to former masters (trader Caius Novius Eunus continued doing business with his former master Caius Novius Cypaerus, who ran the warehouse storing Eunus's goods).
- **Egypt's *epitheke* functioned as a rudimentary letter of credit.** Papyri document a non-negotiable bill-of-exchange instrument used to transfer money between cities without physically moving coin, illustrated by a 2nd/3rd-century business letter from a dyer, Ammon, asking his trading partner Dionysius whether an *epitheke* would be available locally before committing to travel for a wool purchase. Bang is careful not to overstate this into a developed bill-of-exchange system comparable to the Indian *hundi*, but treats it as evidence the Roman world was not wholly without paper-credit tools for intercity trade.

## Open Questions

- Bang's own footnotes flag an active scholarly dispute (Rathbone and Harris vs. de Ligt) over how much of Roman commerce actually ran on non-cash credit transfers versus physical coin movement — this page follows Bang's own cautious middle position but the underlying debate is not resolved in the sampled text.
- The extent to which *societates publicanorum* (tax-farming companies, covered on [[concepts/roman-portorium-customs-system]]) should be read as a structural exception to the household-based credit pattern described here, rather than folded into it, is asserted by Bang (against Temin and Kessler) but the comparative evidence is treated briefly. ^[ambiguous]
