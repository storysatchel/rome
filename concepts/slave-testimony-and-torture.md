# Interrogation under Torture: Slaves, Citizens, and Legal Exceptions

Roman criminal procedure distinguished sharply between the evidentiary value and bodily sanctity of free citizens and enslaved persons. In statutory jury courts (*quaestiones perpetuae*) and imperial tribunals, the judicial examination of slaves was governed by a mandatory requirement of interrogation under torture (*quaestio per tormenta*).

## Rationale and Evidentiary Function

Under Roman jurisprudence, enslaved individuals possessed no independent civil personhood and were assumed to be incapable of giving reliable voluntary testimony. Because slaves were subject to their master's domestic power (*potestas*), jurists operated on the premise that an uncoerced slave would invariably lie out of fear of their owner or desire to curry favor. Consequently, a slave's witness testimony was legally admissible in a criminal court *only* when extracted under judicial torture.

Paradoxically, Roman courts regarded household slaves as the most critical category of eyewitnesses because their intimate proximity to the domestic sphere gave them direct knowledge of poisonings, forged wills, adulteries, and political conspiracies that free citizens could never observe. Judicial torture was thus not viewed as an extraordinary punishment for a crime, but as a standard procedural instrument to compel truth from an evidentiary source.

## The Rule Against Testimony Against Masters (*Servus in Dominum*)

To prevent the total subversion of domestic authority, archaic and Republican civil law established a strict constitutional prohibition:
> *Servus in dominum quaeri non debet.*  
> ("A slave ought not to be interrogated under torture against his master.")

Romans recognized that if slaves could bring capital accusations against their own owners, no household head could sleep safely in his bed (*"so many slaves, so many enemies"*). An owner's enemies would easily bribe or threaten domestic slaves into inventing incriminating testimony.

### Statutory Exceptions to the Prohibition
Over the course of the late Republic and early Empire, specific statutory exceptions were carved out where state or religious emergencies overrode the master's private immunity:
1. ***Crimen Maiestatis* (High Treason):** Any conspiracy against the life of the magistrate, the safety of the state, or (under the Principate) the person of the emperor permitted the immediate torture of an accused citizen's slaves.
2. ***Incestum* of Vestal Virgins:** In trials concerning the unchastity of Vestals—viewed as a cataclysmic rupture of the *pax deorum* threatening Rome's survival—slaves of both the Vestal and the alleged paramour were routinely interrogated under torture (as in the 216 BC and 114–113 BC Vestal trials).
3. ***Adultery (*Lex Julia de adulteriis coercendis*):** Augustus's adultery statutes allowed a husband to demand the judicial torture of his wife's household slaves to establish marital infidelity.
4. ***Publicani* and Tax Fraud:** Slaves could be examined regarding fraudulent evasion of state customs dues and public revenue declarations.

### Legal Evasions and Countermeasures
Because a freed citizen could not be subjected to judicial torture in ordinary criminal trials, masters frequently manumitted their slaves immediately following a crime to prevent prosecutors from examining them. In 52 BC, following the roadside slaying of Publius Clodius on the Appian Way, Titus Annius Milo promptly emancipated all his travelling slaves. When the prosecution demanded their torture, Cicero defended the act in *Pro Milone*, arguing that Milo was merely rewarding loyal servants who had defended their master's life.

To counter such evasions in treason cases, Augustus introduced a legal fiction in AD 8 allowing the state treasury to compulsorily purchase an accused master's slaves at market value, thereby technically transferring their ownership to the Roman state so that they could be tortured without violating the *servus in dominum* rule.

## Citizen Bodily Protections and Imperial Erosion

Under the Republic, statutes including the *Lex Valeria* (509 BC) and *Lex Porcia* (early 2nd century BC) granted Roman citizens absolute immunity from judicial torture, summary execution, and scourging (*verberatio*), enshrining the right of appeal (*provocatio*) to the popular assembly. The famous cry *"Civis Romanus sum"* ("I am a Roman citizen") legally halted summary magisterial violence (e.g. Acts 22:25).

Under the Principate, however, this egalitarian citizen immunity gradually eroded. The judicial system split into a two-tier hierarchy:
- ***Honestiores* (Privileged classes):** Senators, equestrians, municipal decurions, and military officers retained immunity from torture and degrading capital penalties (such as crucifixion, being thrown to wild beasts, or forced labor in the mines).
- ***Humiliores* (Lower-class citizens):** Poorer citizens gradually lost their procedural shields, becoming liable to judicial torture in serious criminal investigations and facing the same brutal penalties historically reserved for slaves and non-citizens.
