# Decimus Junius Brutus Albinus

## Lineage and Political Background
Decimus Junius Brutus Albinus (*c.* 81–43 BC) was one of the most prominent yet complex aristocratic figures of the late Roman Republic. Long overshadowed in historical memory by his distant cousin Marcus Junius Brutus, Decimus was Caesar's most trusted military subordinate, a named secondary heir in Caesar's will, and the indispensable operative who ensured Caesar went to the Senate House on the Ides of March 44 BC.

Prosopographical research by Olga Liubimova demonstrates that Decimus's parentage bridged radical popular and conservative optimate traditions:
- **Paternal Line:** Son of Decimus Junius Brutus (consul 77 BC), belonging to the illustrious plebeian stirps of the Junii Bruti.
- **Maternal Line:** His mother was Sempronia, famously depicted by Sallust (*Cat.* 25) as an exceptionally cultivated and radical aristocratic woman implicated in Catiline's conspiracy. Liubimova proves Sempronia was the daughter of the reforming tribune Gaius Sempronius Gracchus and Licinia, making Decimus the direct biological grandson of Gaius Gracchus.
- **Patrician Adoption:** Adopted by testamentary adoption (*adoptio per testamentum*) by the patrician Aulus Postumius Albinus, appending Albinus to his nomenclature while retaining his natural praenomen and gentilicium.

## Military Career under Caesar
Decimus served Caesar with outstanding distinction throughout the Gallic and Civil Wars:
- **The Venetian Naval Campaign (56 BC):** Given command of Caesar's newly constructed fleet on the Loire, Decimus defeated the formidable sailing fleet of the Veneti in Quiberon Bay by deploying long falces (sickles on poles) to slice the rigging and halyards of the leather-sailed Gallic vessels, crippling them for boarding.
- **The Siege of Massilia (49 BC):** Appointed naval commander during the siege of Massilia, Decimus constructed a scratch fleet that defeated the veteran Massiliot and Pompeian squadrons under L. Domitius Ahenobarbus in two hard-fought naval engagements, securing the city's capitulation.
- **Governor of Transalpine and Cisalpine Gaul (48–44 BC):** Caesar entrusted Decimus with the strategic governorship of Transalpine Gaul, where he suppressed revolts among the Bellovaci, and subsequently nominated him to govern Cisalpine Gaul and designated him for the consulship of 42 BC.

## The Ides of March Conspiracy
Despite receiving immense honors, offices, and affection from Caesar—who named him a secondary heir in his testamentary disposition—Decimus joined the conspiracy led by Marcus Brutus and Gaius Cassius:
- **Role on the Ides of March:** On the morning of March 15, 44 BC, when Caesar hesitated to attend the Senate due to alarming omens and Calpurnia's nightmares, Decimus went personally to Caesar's domus, mocked the seers, took Caesar by the hand, and persuaded him that the assembled senators were ready to confer royal honors abroad.
- During the assassination, Decimus's role was to detain Mark Antony outside the Curia of Pompey in earnest conversation, preventing the powerful consul from coming to Caesar's defense.

## The Mutina Campaign and Death (44–43 BC)
Following the assassination, Decimus immediately took possession of his assigned province of Cisalpine Gaul:
- **The Siege of Mutina:** Mark Antony marched north with his legions to dislodge Decimus and seize Cisalpine Gaul. Decimus withdrew into the fortified city of Mutina (modern Modena), withstanding a grueling winter siege (44–43 BC).
- **Relief by the Consuls and Octavian:** Encouraged by Cicero's *Philippics*, the Senate declared Antony a public enemy and sent the consuls Hirtius and Pansa alongside the young Octavian (propraetor) to relieve Mutina. Antony was defeated at Forum Gallorum and Mutina in April 43 BC, though both consuls perished.
- **Betrayal and Death:** The Senate awarded Decimus a triumph and supreme command, but Octavian refused to cooperate with his adoptive father's murderer. As Octavian marched on Rome to seize the consulship and Antony united forces with Lepidus in Gaul, Decimus's veteran legions deserted him. Attempting to flee disguised through Gaul to join Marcus Brutus in Macedonia, Decimus was captured near the Rhine and executed by a Gallic chieftain on Mark Antony's orders in autumn 43 BC.
