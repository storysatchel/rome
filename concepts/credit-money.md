# credit-money

Credit-money raises a question that coinage alone cannot answer: how far did Roman lending and recoverable debt enlarge the resources available for payments? The absence of modern banking institutions does not by itself rule out a credit multiplier, but its size cannot be estimated confidently.

## Credit beyond coin reserves

The relevant issue is whether lenders held more recoverable debt than state-issued coin in reserve. One reconstruction holds that they generally did. A Roman clearing bank was not a necessary condition for this relationship; insisting on such an institution risks making a modern organizational form the test of an ancient economic capacity.

## Why the scale remains uncertain

Neither Roman GDP nor the stock of coinage can be established well enough to provide a firm denominator. Estimates of the coin stock range from roughly 21 billion sesterces to 6–8 billion, figures too insecure to settle the importance of credit.

Coin hoards also pose a sampling problem. If hoarders preferred older, purer coins, hoards need not represent a cross-section of circulating money. Reminting and debasement further complicate reconstruction. These uncertainties prevent a secure calculation of credit-money as a fraction of the total.

The conclusion is consequently qualitative: even a limited addition to monetary resources could have had strategic importance for the economy. Uncertainty about the quantity is not evidence that credit was negligible.
