# The Theatricality of the Deaths of C. Gracchus and Friends

## Coverage Note
Detailed historiographical study by J. Lea Beness and T. W. Hillard (*The Classical Quarterly* 51.1, 2001, pp. 135–140) evaluating how late Republican oral tradition, tragic drama (*fabula praetexta*), and popular stage performance shaped the literary narratives of the deaths of Gaius Gracchus, Marcus Fulvius Flaccus, and their followers on the Aventine Hill and in the Grove of Furrina in 121 BC.

## Sampled Claims and Historiographical Significance
- **Theatrical Staging Hypothesis:** Builds upon T. P. Wiseman's proposal that the vivid narrative architecture in Plutarch's *Life of Gaius Gracchus* derives from a lost stage drama featuring the standard three-door Roman theatrical backdrop: the Temple of Diana on the Aventine, the house of Gracchus, and the house of Fulvius Flaccus.
- **Mutability of the Dramatis Personae:** Documents the striking fluidity of characters assisting Gracchus's attempted suicide or flight across ancient accounts. While Plutarch names two devoted equestrian friends—Pomponius and Licinius—who sacrificed themselves at the Pons Sublicius to enable Gracchus's flight, other traditions (Valerius Maximus, Velleius Paterculus, Pliny) substitute a Greek teacher (Philocrates), an enslaved attendant (Euporus), or an anonymous slave.
- **Themes of Betrayal and Irony:** Contrasts heroic loyalty (*fides*) with tragic betrayal in the person of Septimuleius, a former companion of Gracchus who severed the fallen tribune's head, drained the brain matter, filled the cavity with molten lead, and presented it to consul Lucius Opimius to collect the bounty of its exact weight in gold.
- **Tragic Peripeteia of Fulvius Flaccus:** Analyzes the death of consular Marcus Fulvius Flaccus and his younger son in an abandoned bathhouse or cellar as a classic tragic reversal, illustrating the psychological collapse of the Gracchan leadership under the first deployment of the *senatus consultum ultimum*.
