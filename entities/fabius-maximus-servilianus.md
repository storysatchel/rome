# Quintus Fabius Maximus Servilianus

Biological son of (likely) Gnaeus Servilius Caepio, consul of 169 BC, and brother of the consul of 140 BC, Quintus Servilius Caepio. Adopted into the [[entities/clan-fabius|Fabii Maximi]] as a second rescue-adoption of the family's near-extinct line in the 180s BC, making him the adoptive — not biological — brother of [[entities/fabius-maximus-aemilianus]]. See [[concepts/roman-elite-adoption-practices]].

## Biography / Role

- McCall treats his adoption as evidence that "the older generation of the Fabius family had adopted not just one son in Aemilianus, but at least two" to save the line — a hedging strategy spreading succession risk across multiple adoptees from different allied gentes.
- Won the consulship for 142 BC; made proconsul in 141 BC and sent to relieve the Roman commander Quinctius in the Spanish war against the Lusitanian leader Viriathus, bringing two additional legions (totaling roughly 18,000 infantry and 1,600 cavalry) plus ten war elephants requested from Rome's Numidian ally King Micipsa.
- Survived a near-disastrous ambush in which his army, after initially routing Viriathus's forces, was broken by a Spanish counterattack and fled in panic to camp; Servilianus personally rallied his tribunes and soldiers to hold the camp until darkness saved them.
- Earned a reputation for brutality against Rome's enemies: he had the hands severed from captured Roman deserters to deter further desertion, and — per the fourth-century writer Orosius — lured roughly 500 Lusitanian leaders in under a pledge of alliance before severing their hands as well; McCall notes this cruelty drew no recorded repercussions, since it was "fair" conduct against Rome's enemies at the time.
- Ultimately failed to defeat Viriathus outright: while the historian Appian gives the most favorable account (an orderly withdrawal to winter quarters), Diodorus and Livy's summarizer instead describe Servilianus conceding a peace with Viriathus "on equal terms" that they regard as a disgraceful outcome rather than a victory. ^[ambiguous]
- Separately achieved note as a historian, reportedly authoring a now-lost history of Rome beginning with Aeneas, cited approvingly by Dionysius of Halicarnassus as a source Romans themselves respected.
- His son, Fabius Maximus Eburnus (consul 116 BC, censor 108 BC), later executed his own son during his censorship for an unspecified "grievous lapse in sexual mores," was convicted in court for it himself, and was subsequently exiled — effectively ending the Servilianus branch of the Fabii Maximi, per McCall.

## Open Questions

- McCall cannot determine from the conflicting sources (Appian versus Diodorus/Livy) whether Servilianus's Spanish command ended in a creditable withdrawal or an embarrassing capitulation to Viriathus. ^[ambiguous]
- The exact offense behind Eburnus's execution of his own son is, per McCall, "lost to us," leaving the episode's severity and the public reaction to Eburnus's subsequent exile only partially explained.
