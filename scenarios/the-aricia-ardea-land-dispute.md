# The Aricia–Ardea Land Dispute: How Two Cities Asked Rome to Arbitrate and Both Lost

In 446 BC a conflict arose between the Latin cities of Aricia and Ardea over possession of a tract of land. Unable to settle the matter themselves, the two cities asked Rome — then merely a leading member of the Latin League rather than a hegemon — to arbitrate.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Aricia** ([[entities/aricia]]) — goal: secure Roman ruling in its favor over the disputed land; resources: status as a fellow Latin city petitioning for arbitration.
- **Ardea** ([[entities/ardea]]) — goal: secure Roman ruling in its favor over the disputed land; resources: status as a fellow Latin city petitioning for arbitration.
- **Rome** — goal: nominally, to render fair judgment as arbiter; actual outcome: acquired the disputed land for itself; resources: its accepted role as a trusted neutral arbiter among Latin communities.

## What Happened

Livy and Dionysius of Halicarnassus record that when Rome, asked to judge the dispute between Aricia and Ardea, could not reach a decision, it resolved the impasse by declaring the contested land to belong to Rome itself as public land (*ager publicus*) — a pattern Roselaar notes recurred later with a similar dispute between Nola and Naples arbitrated by the jurist Labeo (see [[scenarios/labeos-arbitration-of-the-nola-naples-boundary-dispute]]). ^[extracted] Both petitioning cities ended up losing the land to the very arbiter they had asked to settle their quarrel.

## Outcome

Neither Aricia nor Ardea received the disputed territory; it instead became Roman public land, marking one of the relatively few additions to Roman territory during this period beyond the small acquisitions at Labici (418 BC) and Bola (415 BC).

## Open Questions

- The precise location and size of the disputed tract are not specified in the surviving sources.
- Whether Rome's "inability to decide" was a genuine arbitration failure or a pretext for opportunistic annexation is not addressed directly by the ancient sources, though the recurrence of the pattern (cf. Nola–Naples) invites suspicion. ^[ambiguous]
