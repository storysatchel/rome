# Gaius Octavius / Imperator Caesar Divi Filius Augustus

Both citing narrative histories converge on Augustus — not Sulla, not Caesar, not the Gracchi — as the decisive endpoint of the Republic's collapse, though they frame the mechanism of that collapse somewhat differently.

## Key Ideas

- Marin's explicit conclusion rejects single-cause explanations for the Republic's fall (blaming Caesar, his assassins, or Cato and the Senate for failing to compromise), arguing instead that the Republic survived multiple earlier dictatorial crises — Sulla's dictatorship, even Pompey's sole consulship of 52 BC — because those men "stepped down... and allowed the *res publica* to resume its path." The decisive break, for Marin, was Augustus's gradual erosion of Republican tradition after his victory, not any prior individual crisis.
- Marin identifies the roughly two-year gap between Caesar's assassination (44 BC) and Octavian's securing the legal condemnation of Brutus and Cassius (43 BC), followed by Philippi (42 BC), as evidence that contemporaries did not believe the Republic was already dead at the moment of Caesar's death — reinforcing her view that Augustus's own later consolidation, not Caesar's assassination, was the true endpoint.
- Marin's reading of Octavian/Augustus's consolidation after Actium (31 BC) emphasizes a "carefully orchestrated programme of reconciliation with Republican traditions" whose stability "clouded the issue of monarchy," making the transition to one-man rule gradual and partly concealed rather than an abrupt rupture. ^[inferred]
- Watts similarly treats Augustus as the terminus of his narrative arc (through "The Republic of Octavian" and "Choosing Augustan Liberty"), but frames the transition not as an elite program of concealment so much as a popular choice: he opens his book in 22 BC showing that even under Augustus's own rule, the Roman population periodically panicked and *demanded* more autocratic power — up to and including offering Augustus the title of dictator — illustrating his central thesis that republics die because citizens come to prefer the security of autocracy over the chaos of unpunished political norm-breaking.
- Taken together, Marin's and Watts's framings of Augustus diverge subtly: Marin's emphasis falls on Augustus's own deliberate, calculated political programme of disguised monarchy, while Watts's emphasis falls on the public's active complicity — demanding autocratic protection — in normalizing his rule. Both agree the endpoint is Augustus, but they locate the primary causal agency differently (elite design vs. popular demand). ^[ambiguous]

## Open Questions

- Neither citing page details the specific constitutional mechanisms (titles, powers, settlements of 27 and 23 BC) by which Augustus formalized his position; both treat the broader narrative arc rather than the institutional specifics of the Augustan settlement.
