# Lucius Mummius's Sack of Corinth and Personal Enrichment

In 146 BC, the same year Rome destroyed Carthage, the Roman general Lucius Mummius destroyed the leading Greek city of Corinth at the conclusion of the Achaean War, in which Rome crushed a Greek confederacy that had risen against Roman domination. The destruction of Corinth sent shock waves through the Greek world; the historian Pausanias later recorded that most of the male population was put to the sword while women and children were sold into slavery. Mummius personally profited enormously from the plunder and used the proceeds to build a temple to Hercules at Rome.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Lucius Mummius** ([[entities/lucius-mummius]]) — Roman consul and general; goal: crush the Achaean League's resistance to Roman authority and extract maximum material gain from victory; resources: a Roman army and, after victory, unrestricted access to the wealth of one of the richest cities in Greece.
- **Corinth and the Achaean League** — stakes: their political independence, their population's freedom and lives, and the city's accumulated art and wealth, all destroyed or seized.

## What Happened

Rome's intervention in Greek affairs culminated in war against the Achaean League, a confederacy of Greek city-states that had defied Roman directives. After defeating the Achaean forces, Mummius took and destroyed Corinth — one of the wealthiest and most celebrated cities of the Greek world — razing its buildings, slaughtering much of the male population, and selling the women and children into slavery, according to Pausanias's later account. Mummius looted Corinth's famous artworks and treasures (some shipped to Rome, reportedly with so little understanding of their value that Mummius warned the shippers that if any pieces were lost they would have to replace them with new ones — a detail suggesting Roman unfamiliarity with Greek high art at this stage).^[inferred] Mummius personally became wealthy from the proceeds of the sack.

## Outcome

Mummius used his share of the plunder to fund a temple to Hercules at Rome, and Greece was reorganized under direct Roman oversight (the region was not immediately made a province, but Corinth's destruction symbolized the end of independent Greek political action). The sack became proverbial in the Greek world as a demonstration of Roman ruthlessness, paralleling and reinforcing the destruction of Carthage in the same year.

## Open Questions

- The famous anecdote about Mummius's ignorance of the value of Greek art (via Velleius Paterculus and other sources) may be later rhetorical exaggeration rather than strict historical fact.
