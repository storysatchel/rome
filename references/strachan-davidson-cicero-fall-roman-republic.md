# Cicero and the Fall of the Roman Republic

## Coverage Note
Authoritative constitutional and biographical study by J. L. Strachan-Davidson (Heroes of the Nations series, G. P. Putnam's Sons, 1894; 446 pp.) examining the political career, legal defenses, constitutional philosophy, and martyrdom of Marcus Tullius Cicero during the collapse of the Roman Republic.

## Sampled Claims and Constitutional Analysis
- **Forensic Advocacy as a Political Pathway:** Reconstructs Cicero's rise as a *novus homo* from Arpinum, establishing his preeminence through fearless defense advocacy: securing the acquittal of Sextus Roscius of Ameria (80 BC) against Sulla's all-powerful favorite Chrysogonus, and the prosecution of the extortionate Sicilian governor Gaius Verres (70 BC).
- **The Catilinarian Crisis and the Emergency Decree:** Reconstructs Cicero's consulship in 63 BC. Strachan-Davidson provides a rigorous constitutional defense of Cicero's invocation of the *senatus consultum ultimum* to arrest and execute the conspirators Lentulus and Cethegus in the Tullianum, demonstrating that the Senate acted within its customary emergency prerogative to neutralize armed traitors in an active state of insurrection (*perduellio*).
- **Exile and the Clodian Legislation:** Details the constitutional injustice of Cicero's exile in 58 BC under Clodius's retroactive enactment (*Lex Clodia de capite civis Romani*), analyzing the subsequent struggle of Pompey, Milo, and the Senate to achieve Cicero's triumphant recall in 57 BC.
- **The Cilician Proconsulship (51–50 BC):** Highlights Cicero's upright, incorruptible provincial administration in Cilicia, demonstrating his efforts to restrain equestrian usurers (such as Marcus Brutus's agents lending at predatory 48% interest to the city of Salamis in Cyprus) and enforce administrative equity.
- **The Final Stand against Mark Antony:** Chronicles Cicero's leadership of the Senate in 44–43 BC following Caesar's assassination, delivering the fierce *Philippics* against Antony, attempting to steer the young Octavian into defending the commonwealth, and meeting his death with stoic dignity in the triumviral proscriptions.
