# The Trial of Gaius Lucretius for Cruelty Toward the Chalcidians

C. Lucretius, praetor in 171 BC, was accused in the senate by ambassadors from [[entities/chalcis|Chalcis]] of merciless cruelties and robberies perpetrated against their community — almost certainly connected to his naval command during the opening phase of the Third Macedonian War. Rather than letting the matter rest with a senatorial rebuke, two tribunes of the plebs brought formal charges against him before the people.

## Parties & Stakes

- **C. Lucretius** ([[entities/gaius-lucretius-praetor171]]) — praetor of 171 BC accused of provincial misconduct; goal: avoid conviction and the accompanying fine; resources: his prior magisterial standing and whatever defense he could mount before the popular tribunal.
- **Chalcidian ambassadors** — goal: obtain redress from Rome for Lucretius's alleged cruelty and robbery against their community; resources: the right to lay a formal complaint before the Roman senate.
- **M'. Juventius Thalna and Cn. Aufidius, tribunes of the plebs** ([[entities/juventius-thalna-and-aufidius]]) — goal: prosecute Lucretius before the comitia tributa on behalf of the aggrieved allies; resources: tribunician right to bring a popular prosecution.

## What Happened

After the Chalcidians' complaint reached the senate, tribunes Thalna and Aufidius took up the case and prosecuted Lucretius before the people, technically on a charge of *furtum* (theft) and *iniuria* (wrongful injury) rather than any specialized extortion statute, since the standing quaestio for provincial misgovernment (*quaestio repetundarum*) was not established until 149 BC. ^[extracted] The case went to a vote of all the tribes, which convicted Lucretius and imposed a fine of one million asses — an enormous sum reflecting the gravity with which the assembly treated the mistreatment of Rome's Greek allies. ^[extracted]

## Outcome

Lucretius was convicted and fined a million asses; the case stands as an example of the comitia tributa exercising direct judicial authority over a magistrate's provincial conduct in the decades before a permanent extortion court existed, relying instead on ad hoc tribunician prosecution.

## Open Questions

- Whether Lucretius ever paid the fine in full, or what became of his later career, is not addressed in the source. ^[ambiguous]
