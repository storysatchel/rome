# Roman Magistracies

Roman public life was organized around an expanding set of annual magistracies — from the two consuls down through praetors, aediles, tribunes of the plebs, quaestors, and censors — whose holders alone could summon citizens to *contiones* or *comitia*, and whose offices (per Sandberg) were not interchangeable with respect to which assembly they typically used to legislate.

## Key Ideas

- By the late third century BC, Rome's magistracies had grown considerably beyond the two [[concepts/consulship|consuls]]: a growing number of praetors (one from the start, two by c. 244, four by c. 227, six by 197), curule and plebeian aediles, ten tribunes of the plebs, quaestors, and irregular boards (*duumviri*, *triumviri coloniae deducendae*, *decemviri agris dandis*).
- Only officials holding the *ius agendi cum populo* could summon citizens — either to *contiones* (speeches/announcements) or to *comitia*/*concilia* (voting assemblies) — making the right to convene the citizenry itself a defined magisterial privilege rather than a general one.
- Tenure in a magistracy depended on the census, conducted every five years by censors, who assigned citizens to centuries by wealth and to tribes by residence — linking eligibility for office (and voting weight in the [[concepts/popular-assemblies-rome|assemblies]]) to the same census machinery.
- Sandberg's central argument is that specific magistracies had discernible, non-random preferences for specific legislative assemblies, challenging the "commonly assumed" consensus (Ernst Meyer, Jochen Bleicken) that comitial competence was legally undifferentiated after the *lex Hortensia* of 287 BC — since it is well established which assembly elected which magistrates, Sandberg argues it is reasonable to expect, and sets out to demonstrate, that the same magistracies showed comparable patterns in which assembly they used to legislate.
- Sandberg traces a rising dominance of tribunician legislation over consular legislation across the period his study covers (367–88 BC), part of the broader institutional evolution he reads from his evidence of magistrate-assembly pairing. ^[inferred]
- The *patrum auctoritas* — the senate's formal assent once required for comitial decisions a magistrate had put to vote — became a mere formality over time, part of a broader trajectory from aristocratic to more genuinely popular constitutional practice; see [[concepts/roman-senate]] for the senate's own institutional role. ^[inferred]
- Earlier scholarship (Mommsen's *Römisches Staatsrecht*, Bleicken, Lintott) consistently treated magistrates collectively, alongside the senate and the assemblies, as the other "elements" of a tripartite constitution — a framing descending from Polybius himself — without probing why specific magistracies might have distinct institutional preferences among the several assemblies they could use; Sandberg identifies this collective treatment as a gap his study closes.
- Gargola frames the magistracies not purely in formal-constitutional terms but spatially: magistrates organized official activity differently depending on whether it took place *domi* (at home, within the city) or *militiae* (on campaign, outside the *pomerium*) — a distinction bearing on everything from the type of *imperium* a magistrate exercised to which assembly could legitimately be convened where. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- Sandberg concedes the evidentiary base for magistrate-assembly correlation is "extremely limited" and that "many other interpretations than those that will be offered here are perfectly possible" — his stated goal is to show the existing consensus is problematic rather than to prove a definitive alternative pattern. ^[ambiguous]
- Gargola leaves open, as an inherited problem, how far Polybius's and Cicero's king-by-king narratives of individual magistracies' origins (e.g. attributing the censorship and centuriate structure to Servius Tullius) reflect genuine constitutional memory versus retrospective systematization. ^[ambiguous]
