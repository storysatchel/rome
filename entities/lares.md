# Lares

The Lares were Roman protective divinities associated with particular places and communities. The household Lar, or Lares, guarded the *familia* and its property; Lares also had cults at crossroads and in the city. They were worshipped alongside other domestic powers, including the [[entities/penates|Penates]], but the membership and arrangements of household cult varied. Their earliest origin is uncertain: ancestral spirits and spirits of fields are competing explanations, not established facts.

## Household protection and obligation

In Plautus' *Pot of Gold*, a household Lar speaks of guarding a family's buried treasure across generations. The Lar contrasts the neglect of the men of the house with the daughter's daily offerings of incense, wine and garlands. This is a dramatic representation, not a census of ordinary practice, but it depicts protection and offerings as reciprocal obligations. Other literary evidence extends the Lar's concern beyond the house: Tibullus prays for protection on military service, while Cato tells an estate owner to greet the Lar on arrival.

The gods also appeared at transitions in family life. Offerings could mark a boy's coming of age or a wedding; one reported wedding custom assigns coins to the groom, his household Lar and his crossroads Lar. Images could travel: finds of statuettes at Pompeii and Herculaneum have been interpreted as possessions carried by people escaping the eruption of AD 79. Lar figures could be represented as youthful, standing or dancing, and holding a cornucopia.

Household worship was not uniform. Some homes had more than one shrine and worshipped a different combination of deities at each. Garlands, incense, grain, fruit and wine were among offerings in domestic rites; animal sacrifice also occurred. Evidence from preserved shrines and literary depictions should not be flattened into a single compulsory daily ritual.

Among the other powers honoured in domestic settings were the Genius of the *pater familias* and the Juno of the *mater familias*. The Genius was associated with a man's life and celebrated on his birthday; images could show a toga-clad Genius sacrificing to the Lares. Literary evidence for worship of the householder's Genius precedes that for the householder's Juno, so their parallel roles should not be assumed to be equally documented in every period.

## Crossroads and the city

The *Lares Compitales* were associated with crossroads and neighborhoods rather than simply with one household. The movable Compitalia festival took place around the end of December or beginning of January. In Rome under Augustus, neighborhood cult was reorganized: shrines or images of the crossroads Lares were replaced or renamed as Lares Augusti, and Augustus distributed images to at least some neighborhoods. The degree of imperial direction and the chronology of the change remain debated.

Ovid's *Fasti* gives the Lares a mythic parentage in Lara, the silenced mother of twins. His treatment also brings together attributes associated with the crossroads, household and city-guarding Lares Praestites. The distinction between these types was not necessarily fixed in all contexts, so his poetic account should not be treated as a definitive taxonomy.
