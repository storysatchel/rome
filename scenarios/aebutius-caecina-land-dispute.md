# Aebutius v. Caecina: An Armed Eviction and Cicero's Pro Caecina (69/68 BC)

A property dispute between Sex. Aebutius and A. Caecina of [[entities/volaterrae|Volaterrae]], in [[entities/etruria]], escalated to the point that Aebutius allegedly used an armed gang to physically drive Caecina off disputed land — producing a civil suit so contested it required three separate hearings and two deadlocked ("not clear," *non liquet*) jury votes before a verdict emerged.

## Parties & Stakes

- **A. Caecina** ([[entities/aulus-caecina]]), of Volaterrae — plaintiff, suing under the formula for violent dispossession by an armed gang (*unde vi hominibus coactis armatisve*); represented by Cicero in his first recorded civil (as opposed to criminal) oration.
- **Sex. Aebutius** ([[entities/sextus-aebutius]]) — defendant, accused of orchestrating the armed eviction; represented by C. Calpurnius Piso (cos. 67).
- Two jurisconsults — C. Aquillius Gallus (pr. 66) for the plaintiff and possibly Ser. Sulpicius Rufus (cos. 51) for the defendant — supplied competing technical legal opinions on the precise scope of the *interdictum* governing violent dispossession.

## What Happened

The underlying facts, as reconstructed from Cicero's surviving *Pro Caecina*, involved a disputed inheritance or purchase of land near Volaterrae; when Caecina attempted to take possession, Aebutius's men used force to keep him off the property. Caecina sued for violent dispossession, a legal remedy (the *interdictum de vi armata*) that did not require proving who had the better underlying claim to the land — only that force had been used to exclude the plaintiff. The case turned on a highly technical legal question of whether the interdict applied only to a plaintiff forcibly removed after taking possession, or also to one (like Caecina) who was prevented from ever taking possession in the first place — a distinction the two sides' jurisconsults disputed at length. The jury (*recuperatores*) proved unable to reach a verdict twice, returning *non liquet* ("not clear") before finally deciding in a third hearing.

## Outcome

The verdict may have favored Caecina, the plaintiff, but that outcome is inferred from the speech's rhetorical stance rather than directly attested.

## Open Questions

- The precise date (69 vs. 68 BC) depends on dating the praetorship of the presiding magistrate P. Cornelius Dolabella, which is itself uncertain. ^[ambiguous]
- Whether Ser. Sulpicius Rufus or another jurisconsult supplied the defense's technical opinion is a modern scholarly conjecture, not directly stated in the surviving speech. ^[ambiguous]
