# Engraved Gems and Propaganda in the Roman Republic and under Augustus

Pawel Golyzniak's *Engraved Gems and Propaganda in the Roman Republic and under Augustus* (Archaeopress Roman Archaeology 65, 2020) is a monograph-length archaeological study arguing that engraved gemstones — intaglios (carved seal-stones) and cameos — were a significant, under-examined medium of Roman self-presentation and political propaganda from the 3rd century BC through Augustus. It rests on a catalogue of 2,913 gem objects the author compiled and analyzed for political/propagandistic content.

## Core Argument / Thesis

The proposed importance of glyptics is captured by the formulation "glyptics indeed played a significant role in propaganda machinery," serving to popularize images of political figures, function as tokens of factional allegiance (especially among soldiers and followers who commissioned cheaper glass-gem copies), commemorate military and career milestones, and — crucially — permit "bolder propaganda messages than any other branch of Roman art" because of gems' private, personal character, which evaded the public accountability constraints on state-sponsored sculpture or coinage. [[concepts/art-and-political-propaganda]]

## Key Claims and Evidence

- Gems are treated as a class of evidence distinct from but interlocking with coinage, sculpture, relief, oil lamps, pottery (especially Arretine bowls), and toreutics (metalwork) — their "strictly private character" makes them uniquely informative about individual and factional self-presentation rather than only official state messaging.
- The book identifies fourteen anticipated "areas of propaganda" on gems: use in triumphs, collecting, employment of engravers, seals, personal branding, induction/manifestation of loyalty, use of heritage, promotion of family via *origo*, promotion of faction, commemoration, promotion of abstract ideas (*ordo rerum*, *Pax Augusta*, *aurea aetas*), religious/mythological references, political symbols, and luxury objects (State Cameos, carved vessels, works in the round).
- Family symbols and references to familial legends on gems are shown to parallel the much better-studied practice on Republican coinage issued by the *triumviri monetales*, where moneyers promoted their *gens* and ancestors to raise their own authority; gems might show the same or an even stronger phenomenon given their personal-use character, though positively identifying specific family-propaganda gems (as opposed to generic mythological or historical scenes) proves difficult and is prone to scholarly overinterpretation. ^[ambiguous]
- The earliest clear applications of gems for family/*origo* promotion date to the second half of the 2nd century BC, chronologically and iconographically correlating with numismatists' findings about when Roman Republican coinage began performing similar family-promotion functions — some motifs appear on both media at apparently the same period, suggesting shared workshop practices or shared propagandistic strategies across object classes. [[concepts/land-and-agrarian-conflict]]
- Before the late 2nd century BC, promotion of specific individuals through gems relied mainly on portraits of famous ancestors or deliberately crafted images, as later seen with [[entities/lucius-licinius-lucullus|Lucullus]], Pompey the Great, Julius Caesar, Sextus Pompey, and Augustus — rather than abstract family symbols or rebus-like devices, which are much harder to verify as deliberately "family" propaganda versus generic iconography. ^[inferred]
- Case study: Sulla (early 1st century BC) used portrait seals, is linked to possible personal patronage of gem engravers, and employed mythological/divine references and political symbols on his gems; several surviving intaglios and a cameo depicting mythological or triumphal scenes associated with Sulla's circle are presented in the catalogue's plates (e.g., Figures 102–108), including portrait intaglios in carnelian and sardonyx and a scene of winged victories flanking a bust on an altar, read as commemorating military triumph.
- By the Augustan period, cameos in particular "guaranteed social distinction and created a powerful image of the Julio-Claudian dynasty," with top politicians using mythological comparison and even full identification with divine patrons to elevate themselves and their ancestors — a propaganda strategy whose success is inferred from the fact that followers independently reproduced official imagery on their own commissioned or purchased gems (especially cheap glass intaglios), providing a measurable "barometer" of a propaganda campaign's actual public reach, unlike most other media. [[concepts/roman-identity-and-cultural-memory]]
- Pliny the Elder (*Natural History*) is cited as literary evidence that Romans used signet rings bearing family references, though he does not specify when the practice began — a gap the archaeological gem evidence partially fills by dating the clearest examples to the later 2nd century BC. ^[ambiguous]

## Structure / Scope

The book is organized in five parts: (I) Introduction — preface, state of research, aims/methodology; (II) Theory — definitions of self-presentation and propaganda, problems of interpreting ancient propaganda via glyptic evidence; (III) Evidence — chronological chapters from "Beginnings" (3rd–2nd century BC) through early-1st-century figures (Sulla, Marius, Lucullus), the late Republic's major and minor politicians and women, to Augustus (27 BC–AD 14); (IV) Summary and conclusions, including statistical analysis of provenance/production/distribution across the full catalogue; (V) the catalogue itself (2,913 objects), figures, bibliography, and indices. Geographically the evidence spans the Mediterranean collecting world (major museums including the Kunsthistorisches Museum Vienna and the State Hermitage), with findspot/provenance data analyzed separately in the statistics chapters.

## Significance

Golyzniak positions the book as the first comprehensive synthesis incorporating engraved gems into the well-established scholarly field of Roman propaganda studies (previously dominated by coinage, sculpture, and literary sources), explicitly framing prior glyptic scholarship as either narrowly art-historical or insufficiently connected to propaganda theory. The family-symbol findings intersect with the broader elite self-presentation strategies documented in McCall's *Clan Fabius* (funeral eulogies, ancestor masks) and Gruen's *Culture and National Identity* (art as spoils and civic ideology) — all three studies converge on the idea that Republican elite families used whatever media were available (gems, coins, portraiture, oratory, historiography) to construct and transmit distinguished lineage claims. ## Open Questions

- Distinguishing genuine family-propaganda gems from generic mythological/historical scenes with no political intent is methodologically fraught: "overinterpretations are common" in prior scholarship — a caution the book applies to its own catalogue attributions as well, marking only confirmed cases with a "P." designation. ^[ambiguous]
- The relative propagandistic "effectiveness" of gems compared to coinage and sculpture is asserted (via the glass-gem-reproduction argument) but the book acknowledges this impact "seems weaker if compared to coinage or sculpture at the first glance," leaving open exactly how to weigh a medium that is simultaneously highly private and (via copies) potentially widely replicated.
