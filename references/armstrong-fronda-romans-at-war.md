# Romans at War: Soldiers, Citizens, and Society in the Roman Republic

Edited by Jeremy Armstrong (University of Auckland) and Michael P. Fronda (McGill University), published by Routledge in 2020 as a Festschrift for Nathan Rosenstein on his retirement. The volume grew out of a 2017 Celtic Conference in Classics panel ("New Directions in Roman Military Studies") and collects 17 chapters by Rosenstein's former students and collaborators, dedicated to surveying the modern "war and society" approach to the Republican army rather than presenting a single unifying thesis.

## Core Argument / Thesis

The editors explicitly disclaim an overarching argument: "the volume does [not] promote a coherent grand narrative or argue an overarching thesis. Each contributor's work stands on its own." Instead, the book's organizing move is historiographical — it frames itself as a direct descendant of Polybius, "arguably the father of the discipline" of Roman war-and-society studies, and aims to demonstrate how far the field has moved from Polybius's "nuts and bolts" praxis-and-organization approach toward treating warfare as a lens on Roman social, political, religious, and economic structure. [[concepts/roman-military-organization-republic]]

## Key Claims and Evidence

- Polybius is credited as the first (extant) historian to treat an army as a systemic, socio-political institution rather than a backdrop for individual heroism, "bookending" his military digression (Histories 6.19–42) with discussions of the Roman constitution — an approach the editors trace partly to lost Hellenistic antecedents (Hieronymus of Cardia) but credit Polybius with transplanting to a Roman context.
- The editors survey the "imperialism debate" lineage in detail: Mommsen's "defensive imperialism" (following Livy) → W.V. Harris's *War and Imperialism in Republican Rome* (1979), arguing Rome was a uniquely war-built, profit-seeking aristocratic society → A.M. Eckstein's "IR Realist" rebuttal that all ancient states were equally bellicose in an anarchic interstate system, with Roman success explained by superior alliance-management rather than exceptional militarism.
- Nathan Rosenstein's own scholarship is credited with demonstrating that plunder from most Roman wars did not cover their cost — warfare before 167 BC was financed chiefly by citizen *tributum*, with indemnities, not booty, the larger long-run revenue source. [[concepts/roman-provincial-administration]]
- Fred Drogula's chapter (Ch. 2) argues that early Roman military command was not a singular regal prerogative transferred intact to the consulship in 509 BC; instead, individual *gentes* (clans) commanded private warbands and conducted independent raiding/defense throughout the regal period and early Republic, with the *imperium* concept only gradually crystallizing. Evidence includes the Fabii's private war against Veii, Attus Clausus's retinue of 5,000, and the *Lapis Satricanus* inscription recording a *sodales* war-band dedication to Mars.
- James Tan's and Jeremy Armstrong's chapters (on the 4th-century *dilectus*-*tributum* system and "organized chaos" in the manipular army c. 300 BC, respectively) extend the volume's state-formation theme: military obligation and tribal/economic contribution systems are treated as mechanisms binding central Italian communities into the Roman state. ^[inferred]
- The volume notes a live methodological debate (Gauthier vs. Cadiou) over whether the "Marian reforms" constituted genuine institutional change or merely ad hoc wartime measures, reflecting a broader recent trend of questioning inherited narratives (also applied to the supposed early Roman hoplite phalanx, per Rosenstein's own "Phalanges in Rome?").
- The editors note the volume's self-acknowledged gap: minimal engagement with archaeological evidence (beyond scattered Spanish camp references) and no dedicated treatment of the Roman navy or the imperialism debate as a topic in its own right.

## Structure / Scope

Spans the full Republic chronologically, from early Rome (Drogula on command, Tan on *dilectus-tributum*, Armstrong on the manipular army c. 300, Helm on allied integration) through the middle Republic (Roselaar on *ager publicus*, Fronda on Flamininus's "Italian triumph," Barber on senatorial demography after Cannae) to the late Republic (Brice on indiscipline, Wells on Servius Tullius as exemplum, Gauthier on the army's late-Republic transformation), closing with Rosenstein's own epilogue. Contributors include Drogula, Tan, VanDerPuy, Armstrong, Helm, Serrati, Milne, Barber, Fronda, Roselaar, McCall, Clark, Brice, Wells, and Gauthier — largely Rosenstein's doctoral students and mentees at Ohio State.

## Significance

The editors explicitly situate the book against a historiographical backdrop running from Mommsen through Harris's 1979 *War and Imperialism* (called "perhaps the most important work on war and society in the Roman Republic in the last 40 years") to Eckstein, Burton, and Terrenato's more recent interstate-relations and network-based models. They note that diachronic surveys of the Roman military (Keppie, Le Bohec, Goldsworthy) have traditionally privileged the late Republic and early Principate because evidence is denser there, and argue this volume helps redress the Republic's comparative neglect — citing Dobson's *The Army of the Roman Republic* (2008) and Sage's *The Republican Roman Army: A Sourcebook* (2008, this batch's companion volume) as rare exceptions. ## Open Questions

- The editors themselves list significant omissions: archaeological material, the Roman navy, and no chapter directly engaging the imperialism debate despite its centrality to the field's historiography — readers seeking a comprehensive single-volume treatment will not find one here. ^[ambiguous]
- Because the volume disclaims a unifying thesis, cross-chapter claims (e.g., on the pace of the "professionalization" of the army, or on whether late-Republican armies were truly "client armies") remain genuinely contested within the book itself (see the noted Gauthier/Cadiou dispute).
