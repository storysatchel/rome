# Pompeia Plotina

Pompeia Plotina was the wife of the emperor Trajan and bore the honorific title *Augusta*. Her position gave her access to imperial power, although the title itself carried no political office. Little is securely known of her early life; a connection with Nemausus in southern Gaul is a scholarly reconstruction, not a documented birthplace.[^background]

## Public image and influence

Plotina was married to Trajan before he became emperor in AD 98. Pliny the Younger praised her modesty and wifely virtue, presenting an ideal of imperial womanhood rather than an unfiltered portrait. Ancient accounts also describe her intervening with Trajan over provincial abuses and other matters; those reports are evidence for how her influence was remembered, not independent proof of every episode.[^image]

## Sabina, Hadrian, and succession

The later *Historia Augusta* reports that Plotina favored the marriage in AD 100 of [[entities/vibia-sabina|Vibia Sabina]], Trajan's great-niece, to [[entities/hadrian|Hadrian]]. It adds, citing the biographer Marius Maximus, that Trajan was less enthusiastic. The match connected Hadrian more closely to the imperial household, but the account of private negotiations needs caution.[^marriage]

After Trajan's death in 117, Hadrian was announced as his adopted son and successor. Cassius Dio reports that the death was concealed for several days and points to Plotina's signature on letters to the Senate. The *Historia Augusta* preserves a more dramatic rumor: that someone imitated the dying Trajan's voice to arrange the adoption. These are ancient allegations, not established facts; hostile portrayals of Hadrian could exploit stories of a woman's secret machinations.[^succession]

## The Epicurean school at Athens

A fragmentary bilingual inscription dated to 121 preserves correspondence concerning the leadership of the Epicurean school at Athens. Plotina asked Hadrian to relax a citizenship restriction affecting the appointment of its head; Hadrian granted the request, and Plotina conveyed the decision to the school in Greek. This provides more direct evidence of a specific intervention than the later stories about the succession.[^epicureans]
