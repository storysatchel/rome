# Precedent-Escalation as a Mechanism in Marin's Narrative

Marin's book repeatedly flags individual acts as "constitutionally unprecedented," "against tradition," or setting "a dangerous precedent" — a recurring analytical vocabulary, not just incidental description. Read across the whole book rather than at the level of her conclusion's single sentence about Augustus, this vocabulary traces a specific, named chain in which each precedent-breaker cites (or is measured against) the one before.

## Key Ideas

- The chain's first link, in Marin's telling, is the senate's response to Tiberius Gracchus's death in 133 BC: the following year (132 BC) "the anti-Gracchan base" obtained a measure, which Marin calls "another constitutionally unprecedented" act, giving the consuls power to exile any Roman citizen for one year without trial — a measure Gaius Gracchus later made retroactively illegal by law, "fundamentally overturning" it, only for exile-without-trial to reappear in the 80s and 40s civil wars regardless of the statute.
- The second link is Saturninus's election to a third consecutive tribunate in 100 BC, which Marin flags as "elected... for an unprecedented third time" — paralleling and escalating Marius's own run of five consecutive consulships (104–100 BC) in the same years, both read by Marin as violations of the normal one-year, non-repeating cursus that the mid-Republic had relied on.
- The third and most consequential link is Sulla's 88 BC march on Rome: Marin states flatly that "this action was unprecedented, forbidden by law and by tradition," and that the senate's praetors sent to plead with him were humiliated by his soldiers — concluding the episode with the bare sentence "a dangerous precedent had been set." The precedent set was specifically that a Roman army could be marched against Rome itself to reverse a political defeat, independent of any particular ideology.
- The fourth link is Sulla's proscriptions as dictator: Marin credits Sulla as the one "who set the precedent for proscription," explicitly wishing (quoting her source) "that he had been the last" — a precedent that nonetheless recurred in 43 BC under the second triumvirate against a different generation entirely.
- The final link, central to how the chain bears on Caesar, is the precedent of *stepping down*. Marin frames Caesar's refusal to relinquish the dictatorship as a breach measured against two living precedents: Sulla, who held the dictatorship roughly two years before retiring to private life, and Pompey, who held a sole consulship functioning as a dictatorship for only six months in 52 BC before resuming shared consular government. Marin's explicit comparison — "the only precedent in living memory of another dictator was that of Sulla... Pompey had set down his dictatorship/sole consulship in 52 after six months" — makes clear that what made Caesar's dictatorship different in kind was not the office itself (already twice normalized) but his refusal to exit it, a choice with no precedent at all among Marin's own cited chain.
- Marin's conclusion compresses this entire chain into a single line about Augustus "gradually eroding" Republican "traditions," but the book's substantive demonstration of precedent-breaking is concentrated in Chapters 2 and 8 (Sulla's rise, and Caesar's dictatorship) rather than in any sustained treatment of Augustus's own reign, which her narrative does not cover past Actium (31 BC) and the proclamation of Augustus (27 BC) noted only in her chronology table. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- Marin's chain treats each precedent as a discrete, nameable event with a clear originator (Saturninus, Sulla, Caesar), but does not explicitly theorize whether precedent-breaking itself became easier over time (declining cost of violating tradition) or whether each instance required comparably strong individual will — leaving the causal mechanism connecting the links implicit. ^[ambiguous]
- Because Marin's narrative stops at Actium/27 BC and does not detail Augustus's subsequent consolidation (the 27 BC and 23 BC settlements), her often-cited claim that Augustus "gradually erod[ed]" Republican traditions is asserted in her conclusion but not demonstrated with the same step-by-step precedent evidence she supplies for Sulla and Caesar — a gap this deep read surfaces but cannot close from her text alone. ^[ambiguous]
