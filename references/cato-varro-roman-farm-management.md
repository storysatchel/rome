# Roman Farm Management: The Treatises of Cato and Varro

This is a 1918 English translation by Fairfax Harrison ("A Virginia Farmer") of two ancient Latin agronomical works bound together: Marcus Porcius Cato's *De Agri Cultura* (written mid-2nd century BCE, one of the earliest surviving works of Latin prose) and Marcus Terentius Varro's *Rerum Rusticarum Libri Tres* (written in Varro's eightieth year, circa mid-1st century BCE). Both are primary sources, not modern scholarship: practical, didactic handbooks written by Roman landowners for other landowners on how to operate a profitable estate — covering buying land, laying out a farm, stocking it with labor and livestock, and the yearly round of planting, harvesting, and storage.

## Core Argument / Thesis (Didactic Purpose, not Thesis)

Neither text argues a thesis in the scholarly sense; both are manuals. Cato's purpose is to set down, as "a mere collection of random notes," the shrewd common-sense practice of running a mixed farm (vineyard, olive yard, grain, pasture) profitably, with an explicit moral subtext that agriculture is the most respectable and "manly" way to make money, superior to commerce. Varro's purpose is more systematic and consciously scholarly: structured as three dialogues (I. field husbandry, II. livestock husbandry, III. the steading/villa including aviaries, game parks, bees, fishponds), it aims to teach the "profit and pleasure" (*fructus* and *voluptas*) of agriculture to a Roman elite audience that, by Varro's day, increasingly lived in the city and risked losing touch with [[concepts/roman-domestic-life]] rooted in the countryside.

## Key Claims and Evidence

- Cato recommends inspecting a prospective farm repeatedly before buying, prioritizing good climate, strong soil, proximity to a town/river/road, and buildings already in good repair over the sheer number of tools or press-vats present (*De Agri Cultura* I).
- The *pater familias*'s periodic personal inspection tours of the estate, cross-examining the overseer (*vilicus*) on work completed versus excuses offered (bad weather, sick slaves), are described as the core mechanism of estate management and accountability (*De Agri Cultura* II).
- Specific staffing ratios are given for different farm types: an olive farm of 240 *jugera* (~160 acres) is to be stocked with thirteen hands (overseer, housekeeper, five laborers, three ox-drivers, a swineherd, an ass-driver, a shepherd) plus draft animals (*De Agri Cultura* X).
- The overseer's duties are laid out as a quasi-moral code: maintain discipline, settle disputes, prevent theft by ensuring no one goes hungry, keep no hangers-on, consult no soothsayers or astrologers, and work alongside the hands rather than merely supervise (*De Agri Cultura* V).
- The housekeeper (*vilica*), paired with the overseer, is responsible for household religious observance on behalf of the familia, keeping the hearth clean, making preserves, and NOT gossiping, receiving visitors, or attending parties — reflecting gendered and status-bound expectations of domestic order on a working estate (*De Agri Cultura* CXLIII). This is a key primary-source data point for [[concepts/roman-household-staff]].
- Detailed rationing schedules for the familia rustica specify meal, bread, wine, and salt allowances by role and season, illustrating the material economy of a slave-staffed farm (*De Agri Cultura* LVI–LIX).
- Varro's structure divides agricultural knowledge into field crops, livestock, and the steading, reflecting a self-consciously systematic (almost encyclopedic) approach contrasted with Cato's looser notebook style ^[inferred].
- Varro frames agriculture's purpose as twofold — profit (*fructus*) and pleasure (*voluptas*) — and devotes Book III substantially to pleasure-oriented installations (his own elaborate aviary at Casinum, fishponds, game preserves), suggesting that by the late Republic elite villa life had absorbed agricultural production into broader aristocratic leisure and display ^[inferred].

## Structure / Scope

Cato's treatise (mid-2nd c. BCE) is organized not by table of contents originally but has been editorially arranged by topic (buying a farm, duties of owner/overseer/housekeeper, draining, manure, planting, livestock care, recipes for cakes and cured hams). Varro's three books (dated to his old age, roughly 37 BCE) are each cast as a dialogue among learned Roman interlocutors: Book I on field husbandry including an elaborate calendar of agricultural seasons tied to solar and lunar observation; Book II on livestock (sheep, goats, swine, cattle, asses, horses, mules, herd dogs, shepherds); Book III on the steading, covering aviaries, peacocks, pigeons, poultry, rabbits, game preserves, snails, dormice, bees, and fishponds. Geographically the texts describe estates in Italy (Cato writes from a Sabine/Campanian perspective; Varro from his own Sabine and Casinum properties).

## Significance

The 1918 translator explicitly positions the work as of practical, not merely antiquarian, interest to modern (Virginian) farmers, arguing the Roman virtues of "thoroughness and patience" — e.g. fallowing, green manuring with legumes, seed selection, composting — anticipate scientific farming practices rediscovered in the early 20th century ^[inferred, translator's framing rather than the ancient authors' own claim]. Within Roman literary history, Columella (writing under Tiberius) credited Cato with having "taught Agriculture to speak Latin," and Varro's work was itself a known source for Virgil's *Georgics*, establishing both texts as foundational to the later Latin agronomic tradition (Columella, Pliny, Palladius).

## Open Questions

- The extent to which Cato's and Varro's idealized estate organization (ratios of slaves to acreage, overseer/housekeeper duties) reflects universal practice versus the authors' own particular estates is not addressed within the text itself and remains debated among agrarian historians. ^[ambiguous]
- Neither treatise directly addresses the lived experience or interiority of the enslaved *familia rustica* whose labor the system depends on; their presence is visible only through management instructions and ration tables.
