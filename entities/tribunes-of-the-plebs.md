# Tribune of the Plebs

The tribunate of the plebs (*tribunus plebis*) was a Roman magistracy — ten officeholders strong by the late third century BC — that presided over the *concilium plebis* and became a dominant channel of Roman legislation. Unlike the consulship or praetorship, it was not an imperium-holding magistracy, but it carried the power to summon plebeian citizens and bring binding measures to a vote.

## Key Ideas

- By the late third century BC, Rome's magistracies included two consuls, a growing number of praetors (one at first, rising to six by 197 BC), curule and plebeian aediles, quaestors, irregular boards, and ten tribunes of the plebs. Officials with the relevant right to convene citizens could summon *contiones* for speeches and announcements or *comitia*/*concilia* for voting. Tribal assemblies — convened as the *concilium plebis* under a tribune — typically handled legislation from the mid-Republic onward.
- The tribunate presided specifically over the *concilium plebis*, traditionally established after the first *secessio* of 494 BC. Its precise relationship with the broader *comitia tributa*, the full citizen body voting by tribe, remains disputed: they may have been distinct bodies or effectively the same assembly under different procedural names. ^[ambiguous]
- A decision of the full *populus* was technically a *lex*, while a decision of the *concilium plebis* under a tribune was a *plebiscitum* — a distinct and originally narrower category. After the *lex Hortensia* of 287 BC gave plebiscites the same binding force on all citizens (the *exaequatio legibus*), the two terms became interchangeable in common usage, though some inscriptions preserve the older technical distinction.
- Between 367 and 88 BC, tribunician legislation appears to have become increasingly prominent relative to consular legislation. On this reconstruction, tribunes increasingly brought binding measures (*rogationes*) before voters, but the thin evidence makes the chronology provisional. ^[inferred]
- The formal assent of the senate (*patrum auctoritas*), apparently once necessary for assembly decisions to take legal effect, became a formality over time. Interpreting this as a movement from aristocratic toward more genuinely popular constitutional practice is one possible reading of the change. ^[inferred]
- A bill (*rogatio*) underwent formal *promulgatio* (public posting, typically weeks ahead) and public debate through *contiones* before a binary up-or-down vote (*uti rogas* or *antiquo*) in a voting assembly. This procedural reconstruction applies to tribunician proposals as well as those of other magistrates.

## Open Questions

- Whether the *concilium plebis* and *comitia tributa* were institutionally distinct bodies or effectively the same assembly under different procedural names remains disputed and is not resolved by either citing source. ^[ambiguous]
- The evidence for the mechanics of tribunician legislation is "extremely limited." Challenges to conventional reconstructions do not establish a definitive alternative chronology of the tribunate's rise to legislative dominance. ^[ambiguous]
