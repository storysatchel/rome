# A Young Caesar's Failed Prosecution of Cn. Cornelius Dolabella (77 BC)

In 77 BC, a 23-year-old [[entities/julius-caesar]] — not yet a senator, let alone a general or dictator — made his debut as a prosecutor in [[entities/rome|Rome]]'s extortion court, charging the former consul Cn. Cornelius Dolabella with plundering [[entities/macedonia|Macedonia]] during his governorship (Alexander, case #140). The episode predates by decades everything the wiki's existing Caesar material covers and shows Caesar's earliest recorded public act as a courtroom loss.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Cn. Cornelius Dolabella** ([[entities/gnaeus-cornelius-dolabella-cos-81]]), cos. 81 — defendant, accused of extortion as governor of Macedonia (80–77 BC); defended by two of the era's most formidable advocates.
- **C. Iulius Caesar** ([[entities/julius-caesar]]), then a young noble with no major office yet to his name — prosecutor, in what was likely his first major public legal and oratorical appearance.
- **C. Aurelius Cotta** ([[entities/gaius-aurelius-cotta-cos-75]]), cos. 75, and **Q. Hortensius Hortalus** ([[entities/quintus-hortensius-hortalus]]), cos. 69 — co-advocates for Dolabella's defense; Hortensius was already established as Rome's leading courtroom orator.
- Witnesses "from Greek cities" — the Macedonian and Greek provincial communities Dolabella allegedly extorted, whose testimony formed the backbone of Caesar's case.

## What Happened

Caesar brought the extortion charge under the *lex Cornelia de repetundis*, with the trial running to a formal *divinatio* and two separate hearings (*actiones*) — the full-dress procedure used for serious, contested extortion cases. Dolabella's defense team, Cotta and Hortensius, represented the top tier of Roman advocacy available at the time; Caesar, by contrast, had no prior forensic reputation to draw on. Ancient sources (Suetonius, Plutarch, Tacitus's *Dialogus*) preserve the episode chiefly as evidence of Caesar's early oratorical talent — Tacitus cites his speech in this case as proof Caesar could have rivaled the era's great orators had he pursued that career instead of a political and military one — even though the prosecution failed.

## Outcome

Dolabella was acquitted. The loss did not damage Caesar's reputation; ancient sources instead treat his performance as a prosecutor in a losing cause as evidence of exceptional promise, and he went on the following year to prosecute (again unsuccessfully) C. Antonius for provincial misconduct in [[entities/achaea-greece|Greece]].

## Open Questions

- The specific evidence and witness testimony presented are lost; only the identities of the advocates, the procedural structure (divinatio, two actiones), and the outcome survive.
