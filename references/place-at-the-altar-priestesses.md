# A Place at the Altar: Priestesses in Republican Rome

Meghan J. DiLuzio's *A Place at the Altar: Priestesses in Republican Rome* (published via Project MUSE, Princeton University Press) surveys female religious office in the Republic: the [[entities/vestal-virgins]], *flaminica Dialis* (wife of the flamen Dialis), priestly couples, Salian virgins and other *sacerdotes*/*ministrae*. Publicly visible religious authority, rather than merely domestic or symbolic roles, is a probable organizing theme, inferred from the title, contents and recovered chapters rather than a directly recovered statement of the overall thesis.^[inferred from title, table of contents, and recovered chapter content]

## Core Argument / Thesis

Republican priestesses may be understood as holders of genuine public religious offices integrated into Roman state religion, rather than segregated from it; at least the Vestals could also act politically. A wider trend toward increasing political agency remains an inferred framing, not a securely recovered statement of the book’s overall thesis. The evidence available here consists of the contents and surviving Chapters 6–7; the Introduction and Conclusion could not be read. ^[inferred]

## Key Claims and Evidence

- Vestals played a prominent ritual role in state *lustratio* (purification) ceremonies, e.g. Tacitus's account of the AD 70 lustration of the Capitol after Vespasian's forces destroyed the Temple of Jupiter, in which the Vestals sprinkled the site with water drawn from fountains and streams before a praetor and pontifex performed a *suovetaurilia* sacrifice.
- Religious knowledge within the Vestal order was transmitted through direct apprenticeship rather than formal institutionalized education: Valerius Maximus describes a young Vestal as the "pupil" (*discipula*) of the chief Vestal Aemilia, and Dionysius of Halicarnassus reports a thirty-year career divided into learning, performing and teaching duties. This three-stage structure can be compared with Seneca’s *De Otio* 2.1–2 on the division of responsibility by seniority.
- The Vestals were assigned ritual duties connected to the Augustan regime, including incense offerings marking the anniversary of Augustus's election as pontifex maximus, and an annual sacrifice at the altar of Fortuna Redux (dedicated 19 BC) on the Augustalia (October 12); the Vestals were later placed in charge of the cult of the deified Livia under Claudius.
- Political interventions by individual Vestals recur as a theme in Ch.7: Tarpeia (legendary, Regal-period) is read as the archetypal failure of the Vestal's required loyalty to the Roman community over personal/family ties; Claudia (daughter of the consul of 143 BC Ap. Claudius Pulcher) used her person as a Vestal to physically block a tribune from obstructing her father's unsanctioned triumph over the Salassi, an episode ancient authors treat as legitimate largely because of her status; Claudia the Vestal is also invoked by Cicero in the *Pro Caelio* as a model of filial *pietas* contrasted with Clodia.
- Licinia, daughter of a popularis-aligned figure, dedicated a shrine to Bona Dea on the Aventine without the people’s authorization. The urban praetor and senate challenged it, and the pontifical college ruled, as quoted in Cicero’s *De Domo Sua* 136, that an unauthorized dedication on public land was not valid *sacrum*. The Aventine location, associated with Remus’s auspices in a plebeian-cause tradition, may signal deliberate popularis sympathy; that political intent is an interpretation, not an established motive.
- Licinia (likely the same or a related Vestal) also gave her seat at the gladiatorial games to M. Licinius Murena. Cicero’s *Pro Murena* frames this as *pietas* toward a relative rather than an improper political favor; the same gesture could nevertheless have had political significance.
- In the narrative of the Catiline conspiracy (63 BC), the Vestals (including Fabia, cf. [[references/wildfang-romes-vestal-virgins]]) interpreted a dramatic flame omen during a sacrifice and sent word through Terentia urging Cicero to act against the conspirators, before the senate’s vote to execute them. One reconstruction treats the portent as actively orchestrated to influence the crisis through religious prestige; this does not establish that the Vestals fabricated or deliberately produced the omen.
- The Vestals joined other advocates in successfully appealing to Sulla to spare Julius Caesar, who had been appointed *flamen Dialis* by Marius and Cinna in late 87 or early 86 BC. Suetonius gives their appeal an emphatic position. The episode supports a reading of collective Vestal political action; a wider trend toward political "sophistication" is a distinct interpretation, not demonstrated by the intercession alone (compare [[references/wildfang-romes-vestal-virgins]]). ^[ambiguous]
- During the triumviral period (40s BC), the Vestals safeguarded legal documents, valuables and money, including Mark Antony’s will according to Plutarch and Cassius Dio. Octavian’s seizure of the will can be viewed alongside other violations of the temple’s sanctity; the Vestals also removed sacred objects from the Temple of Vesta amid the proscriptions’ instability.

## Structure / Scope

Covers the full Republican period into the early Principate (explicitly extending at least through events of the AD 70s, per the Tacitus lustration example), organized into eight chapters plus introduction and conclusion moving from the flamen/flaminica Dialis and priestly couples generally (Ch.1–3) to a sustained focus on the Vestals specifically (Ch.4–7: order, costume, ritual activity, and politics). Only Chapters 6–7 and the Bibliography were directly readable in this ingest; the book's treatment of the flaminica Dialis, priestly couples, and Salian virgins (Ch.1–3) is known only by chapter title. ^[ambiguous — due to file corruption, not stated in the source]

## Significance

Named Vestals — Tarpeia, Claudia, Licinia and Fabia — can be examined as political agents rather than passive religious symbols. The recovered case studies of the 63 BC Catilinarian omen and the Licinia/Bona Dea shrine dispute complement the 73 BC Fabia/Catiline and Licinia/Crassus *incestum* acquittals emphasized in [[references/wildfang-romes-vestal-virgins]]. These different episodes involve some of the same individuals, but the shared interest in political agency does not establish that both books make the same argument about a historical increase in assertiveness or "sophistication." ^[inferred]
