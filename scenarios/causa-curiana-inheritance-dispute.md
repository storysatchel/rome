# The Causa Curiana: M'. Curius's Inheritance Dispute (94/93 BC)

A will-interpretation lawsuit before the *centumviri* (the Roman court for inheritance disputes) pitted two of the era's leading orators, L. Licinius Crassus and Q. Mucius Scaevola, against each other over a single clause of testamentary Latin (Alexander, case #93). The case became a touchstone in later Roman legal and rhetorical education for the clash it staged between literal and purposive interpretation of legal texts.

## Parties & Stakes

- **M'. Curius** ([[entities/manius-curius]]) — named in the will as residuary heir, contingent on the testator's son dying before reaching legal majority; stood to inherit the whole estate.
- **M. Coponius** ([[entities/marcus-coponius]]) — the testator's nearer relative by blood, who argued the will's wording only covered the case of a son being born and later dying, not a son never being born at all; sued to overturn Curius's claim.
- **L. Licinius Crassus** ([[entities/lucius-licinius-crassus-cos-95]]), cos. 95 — advocate for Curius, arguing for the testator's evident intent over the letter of the clause.
- **Q. Mucius Scaevola** ([[entities/quintus-mucius-scaevola-pontifex]]), cos. 95 (the "Pontifex," not to be confused with his cousin the "Augur" of the same name) — advocate for Coponius, arguing strict construction of the written terms.

## What Happened

The testator's will instructed that if his wife bore a son and that son died before reaching his majority (*ante tutelam*), the estate should pass to Curius as residuary heir (*hereditatis petitio*). In the event, the testator's wife bore no son at all — the condition as literally written (a son being born and then dying) never technically occurred. Coponius, the nearer heir by blood, argued that since the precise wording's condition was never fulfilled, the will's residuary clause could not be activated and the inheritance should pass to him by default. Crassus, for Curius, argued that the testator's manifest intent was to provide for who should inherit *if he died without an adult son* — a condition plainly satisfied by having no son at all — and that courts should enforce intent, not pedantic literalism.

## Outcome

The court found for Curius, endorsing Crassus's argument for interpreting the testator's intent over Coponius's and Scaevola's insistence on the letter of the clause. The case was argued before both men held the consulship (94 or 93 BC), and both were already leading figures of the Roman bar.

## Open Questions

- The exact year (94 vs. 93 BC) depends on dating Scaevola's proconsulship in Asia, which is itself disputed in the sources Alexander cites.
