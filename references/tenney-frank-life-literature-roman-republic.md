# Life and Literature in the Roman Republic

## Coverage Note
Celebrated literary, social, and cultural synthesis by Tenney Frank (*Sather Classical Lectures*, Vol. 7, University of California Press, 1930; 256 pp.) exploring the organic relationship between Roman socioeconomic realities and the development of Republican Latin literature.

## Sampled Claims and Literary Sociology
- **Social Forces and National Drama:** Demonstrates that Latin literature did not emerge from detached academic imitation of Hellenistic models, but was deeply rooted in aristocratic civic competition and popular public festivals (*ludi scaenici*).
- **Early Tragedy and Epic (Naevius and Ennius):** Explains how early poets adapted Homeric and Greek tragic forms to celebrate Roman national identity and *gens* military glory, fostering an aristocratic civic religion centered on ancestral virtue (*mos maiorum*).
- **Popular Comedy vs. Elite Refinement:** Contrasts Titus Maccius Plautus, who composed exuberant, colloquial comedies tailored to the energetic, post-Hannibalic veteran populace, with Publius Terentius Afer (Terence), whose polished, psychologically nuanced scripts reflected the elite philosophical tastes of the Scipionic Circle.
- **The Evolution of Political Oratory:** Traces Roman forensic and political prose from Cato the Elder's blunt, native rustic eloquence (*rem tene, verba sequentur*) through the passionate, populist rhetoric of Gaius Gracchus to the philosophical synthesis and stylistic maturity of Marcus Tullius Cicero.
- **Historiography and Epicurean Philosophy:** Analyzes how Roman annalistic historiography (Fabius Pictor, Cato, Coelius Antipater, Livy) developed as an instrument of aristocratic self-justification, and how Lucretius's *De Rerum Natura* offered an intellectual refuge of scientific rationalism amid late Republican civil strife.
