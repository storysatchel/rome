# The Fabius–Tullius Land Dispute: Cicero's Pro Tullio (71 BC)

A property dispute between two otherwise obscure landowners, P. Fabius and M. Tullius (no relation to Cicero despite the shared name), escalated into an armed confrontation near [[entities/thurii|Thurii]] in southern Italy — then still recovering from its occupation by Spartacus's slave army the previous year — and produced one of Cicero's early civil-law speeches.

## Parties & Stakes

- **M. Tullius** ([[entities/marcus-tullius-plaintiff]]) — plaintiff, suing under the formula for property damage done "by armed men" (*damnum datum vi hominibus armatis*), claiming Fabius's men had attacked his slaves and property; represented by Cicero.
- **P. Fabius** ([[entities/publius-fabius-litigant]]) — defendant, accused of orchestrating or permitting the armed attack; represented by the tribune-to-be L. Quinctius.
- **L. Quinctius** ([[entities/lucius-quinctius-tr-74]]), tr. pl. 74, later pr. 68 — advocate for Fabius.
- **M. Tullius Cicero** (cos. 63) — advocate for the plaintiff Tullius; the resulting speech, *Pro Tullio*, survives only in fragments.

## What Happened

The two men's properties bordered each other in the region of [[entities/thurii|Thurii]], an area still unsettled in the immediate aftermath of Spartacus's occupation (72 BC) — Cicero's surviving fragments specifically date the dispute to after Thurii had ceased to be held by Spartacus's forces, i.e., 71 BC rather than 72. Tullius alleged that Fabius sent armed men (*hominibus armatis*) who used force against Tullius's slaves and property, grounds for a specific *recuperatores* action for violent property damage rather than an ordinary boundary or trespass suit. The case required two separate hearings (*actiones*), suggesting a seriously contested evidentiary record on both sides.

## Outcome

No definite verdict survives. A successful outcome for Cicero is possible, but inferring it from the speech's rhetorical confidence cannot substitute for a recorded judgment.

## Open Questions

- The outcome of the suit is genuinely unknown — only the fragmentary speech and the identities of the parties survive. ^[ambiguous]
- Which praetor (L. Caecilius Metellus or Q. Caecilius Metellus Creticus) presided is uncertain given overlapping possible praetorships in 71 BC.
