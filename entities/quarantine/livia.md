# Livia

Livia was the wife of Augustus and the mother of his successor Tiberius, placing her at the center of the Julio-Claudian imperial family. Born in 59 or 58 BCE, she married Octavian, the future Augustus, in 38 BCE and lived until 29 CE. After Augustus’ death in 14 CE, she was adopted into the Julian family and took the name Julia Augusta. Her position connected marriage, succession, religious honors, and public benefaction without giving her an ordinary Roman magistracy.

Her public image varied across the empire and between her husband’s and son’s reigns. Coinage, temple restorations, and literary accounts illuminate different aspects of her prominence; none supplies a simple measure of how much power she exercised behind the scenes.

## Rome and the eastern coinages

Livia did not appear on coins made at Rome during Augustus’ lifetime, but cities in the Greek-speaking East included her on their issues. A bronze coinage produced in Thrace between 11 BCE and 12 CE placed her bust beside Augustus’ as conjoined portraits. The source relates such imagery to eastern traditions of monarchy and female monarchs.

The other face of the Thracian issue paired the client king Rhoemetalkes I with his queen Pythodoris. In both pairs, the woman’s portrait was the same size as her husband’s but placed behind it. This parallel supplied a locally intelligible image of a ruling couple, while preserving a visual ordering within the pair. Neither woman was named on the issue.

These joint portraits may have been symbolic. The suggestion that they reproduced appearances made together on imperial tours remains conditional on Livia actually accompanying Augustus. The secure conclusion is the regional adaptation of her public image, not that a coin proves a particular journey or a formal sharing of sovereignty.

## Virtue, benefaction, and later prominence

Livia’s public identity also drew on the values of chastity and proper matronly conduct associated with the Augustan regime. Her restorations of the temples of Bona Dea Subsaxana and Fortuna Muliebris linked her activities with religious renewal. The account also describes assistance with children’s upbringing and daughters’ dowries.

Under Tiberius, Livia became the subject of more coin issues and acquired a greater presence in surviving literary narratives. Those narratives require care: *Domina* itself notes that stories of her dominance could diminish Tiberius by portraying him as unable to escape his mother’s influence. Increased visibility does not make every claim about her control equally reliable.

An episode involving the conspiracy of Cornelius Cinna illustrates the distance between influence and recoverable speech. Dio presents Livia advising Augustus to prefer clemency to terror. *Domina* treats their conversation as a literary construction, perhaps derived from Seneca, through which Dio explored imperial rule. Its language of shared sovereignty cannot safely be quoted as Livia’s own account of her position.
