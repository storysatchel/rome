# civic archives

Civic archives were functional spaces for collecting and retaining documents that exercised power within and between communities. Their value extended beyond the storage of official decisions: they could preserve the records through which a community remembered its past and its inhabitants substantiated social and legal relationships.

## Documents and civic memory

Archived material recorded political acts, dealings with neighbouring communities, and interactions among inhabitants. Together, these records could form a repository of civic memory rather than simply an administrative accumulation.

Access affected how that memory could be used. Where archives allowed interested parties to consult deposited documents, motivated individuals could construct their own accounts of the community. Such openness was qualified: the passage refers to access for certain interested parties, not universal or unrestricted public consultation.

## What was kept, and where

The chapter defines an archive as documents deliberately gathered and stored for future consultation. A civic collection could contain decrees of an assembly or council, correspondence from kings or Roman authorities, and private wills, contracts, or property deeds. The inclusion of personal documents distinguishes this broad functional definition from one limited to papers produced by the state.

Archives need not have occupied a single specialized building. Before and during the Hellenistic period, documents could be deposited with magistrates or in public buildings associated with their responsibilities. A decree from Ephesos in 299 BCE ordered copies of debt judgments kept both in the Artemision and by an official charged with facilitating access.

## Consultation and curated display

Access was more often restricted than freely open. Even so, civic institutions could draw on deposited records to inscribe selected texts in public spaces. Storage, consultation, and monumental display were distinct practices: keeping records enabled authorities to curate a public history without necessarily exposing the entire collection to every reader.
