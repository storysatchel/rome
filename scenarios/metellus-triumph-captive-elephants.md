# Metellus's Triumph with 120 Captive Elephants

This is a historical-anecdotal episode from Seneca's *On the Shortness of Life* (ch. 13), mentioned not to celebrate the event but as Seneca's prime example of trivial antiquarian learning — information he considers useless compared to philosophy.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Lucius Caecilius Metellus** ([[entities/lucius-caecilius-metellus]]) — Roman general celebrating a triumph at [[entities/rome|Rome]] ^[inferred: triumphal processions conventionally entered the city of Rome, though Seneca's text does not name it] after defeating Carthaginian forces in [[entities/sicilia|Sicily]]; stakes: the spectacle and prestige of his triumphal procession
- **The defeated Carthaginian army in Sicily** (collective counterpart, not individually named in this source) — supplier of the captured elephants

## What Happened

Quoting an unnamed antiquarian writer, Seneca reports: "Metellus, in celebrating his triumph over the Carthaginians defeated in Sicily, was the only Roman to have 120 captive elephants walk in procession before his chariot" ^[inferred — almost certainly the historical Lucius Caecilius Metellus's triumph following his victory at Panormus in the First Punic War, though Seneca's text supplies neither date nor battle name]. Seneca pairs this with a second piece of trivia from the same antiquarian source (that Sulla was the last Roman to enlarge the city's sacred boundary) specifically to illustrate what he considers worthless scholarly pedantry — his friend Fabianus, Seneca notes, "used to say that he was sometimes in doubt whether it would not be better to leave off studying altogether than to become involved in such subjects as these."

## Outcome

The triumph itself is reported as an undisputed, unmatched Roman spectacle — the only time a Roman general paraded so many captured elephants in a single procession — but within Seneca's own essay the "outcome" that matters is rhetorical: the anecdote is deployed to discredit, not praise, the pursuit of such facts, in service of his broader argument that most people waste their short lives on concerns (including idle erudition) with no bearing on how to live well.

## Open Questions

- Other ancient sources (outside this text) describe some of the captured Carthaginian elephants later being killed as a public spectacle in Rome; this detail is not present in Seneca's account and is not asserted here as established by this source. ^[ambiguous]
- Seneca gives no date, battle name, or opposing general for the triumph; the First Punic War / Panormus identification is supplied from outside knowledge, not from this text. ^[inferred]
