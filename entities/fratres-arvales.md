# Fratres Arvales

The *Fratres Arvales* (Arval Brethren, literally "Field-Brother Priests") was an ancient Roman sodality (*sodalitas*) of twelve elite priests. According to myth, the sodality was founded by Romulus and his foster-mother Acca Larentia to ensure the fertility of the plowed fields (*arva*). Having lapsed into near-total obscurity during the late Republic, the brotherhood was resurrected in 29–28 BCE by [[entities/augustus|Augustus]] as a premier instrument of religious restoration and dynastic legitimation. Operating at the sacred grove of the enigmatic agricultural goddess *Dea Dia* down the Tiber, the Arval Brethren carved the minutes of their proceedings on marble slabs (*Acta Arvalia*), creating one of the richest surviving epigraphic records of imperial religion and senatorial loyalty.

## Origins and Augustan Revival

Roman antiquarian tradition (preserved by Pliny the Elder and Varro) attributed the origin of the Arval Brethren to Acca Larentia, the nurse of Romulus. When one of her twelve sons died, Romulus stepped into his place, calling himself and the remaining eleven "Arval Brothers" (*fratres arvales*). The archaic sodality wore wreaths of braided wheat ears (*corona spicea*) tied with white wool fillets (*infulae*), a badge of office held for life.

By the first century BCE, the sodality had virtually ceased active operations amid the civil wars. As part of his comprehensive program to restore ancestral religion (*mos maiorum*) and revive ancient patrician sodalities, Augustus re-founded the Arval Brethren in 29–28 BCE. Augustus enrolled himself as an active brother alongside eleven of the most illustrious patrician and consular aristocrats in Rome. From that moment, the reigning princeps (and frequently heirs-apparent such as Germanicus, Nero, or Marcus Aurelius) was always a member of the brotherhood.

## The Cult of Dea Dia and the May Festival

The operational heart of the Arval Brethren was the sanctuary of *Dea Dia*, a goddess of growth, clear skies, and agricultural fertility, located five miles southwest of Rome at the fifth milestone of the Via Campana (modern La Magliana, on the right bank of the Tiber).

Annually in January, the brethren gathered in Rome (often at the house of the *magister* of the college or on the Palatine) to officially proclaim the dates for the movable three-day festival of Dea Dia in May (*indictio feriarum*).

The three-day May ritual followed an unvarying ceremonial pattern:
1. **Day One (in Rome):** The brethren convened at dawn at the private home of the *magister*. Clad in the *toga praetexta*, they offered incense, wine, and consecrated grains to Dea Dia, followed by a ceremonial breakfast and the blessing of ears of young green wheat.
2. **Day Two (in the Lucus Dea Dia):** The central ceremonies took place within the sacred grove (*lucus*) at the Via Campana sanctuary. 
   - At dawn, the magister sacrificed two young female pigs (*porciliae piaculares*) to purify the grove, followed by a white heifer (*vacca honoraria*) to Dea Dia herself.
   - Within the temple (*tetrastylum*), the priests touched ears of dry grain, green grain, and loaves of bread.
   - **The *Carmen Arvale*:** The temple doors were closed, the attendant slaves (*calatores*) and public were excluded, and the twelve brethren, tucking up their togas (*succincti*), performed a three-step sacred stamping dance (*tripudium*) while chanting from archaic written scripts the venerable *Carmen Arvale*. This chant, preserved on an inscribed slab from 218 CE, is written in archaic Latin so ancient that by the imperial period the priests themselves could barely comprehend its literal words, invoking the Lares (*Enos Lases iuvate*), Mars (*neve lue rue Marmar sins incurrere in pleoris*), and crying three times *Triumpe!*.
   - Following the dance, the brethren held a chariot race in the adjacent circus before returning to Rome.
3. **Day Three (in Rome):** The festival concluded back at the home of the magister with a final banquet and distributions of sweetmeats and commemorative gifts (*sportulae*).

## Ritual Purifications and Taboos

The grove of Dea Dia was subject to exceptionally strict ritual taboos. Iron was an absolute religious pollutant; if an iron chisel or saw was brought into the grove to trim a fallen tree or carve an inscription, elaborate expiatory sacrifices (*piacula*) of pigs, sheep, and oxen had to be performed before and after the iron touched the sacred soil. Similarly, if lightning struck a tree or a branch fell within the grove, the brethren convened to sacrifice victims to restore ritual equilibrium.

## The Imperial Dynastic Cult: *Vota Pro Salute*

While the agricultural rituals of Dea Dia maintained traditional rural fertility, the primary practical function of the imperial Arval Brethren was the sacral legitimation of the emperor and the Julio-Claudian, Flavian, Antonine, and Severan dynasties.

The *Acta Arvalia* document that the vast majority of the sodality's meetings throughout the year were dedicated to the imperial family:
- **Annual Imperial Vows (January 3):** The brethren assembled on the Capitoline Hill in front of the Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus to fulfill the vows (*vota*) made the previous year and utter solemn new vows for the safety, health, and preservation of the princeps (*vota pro salute imperatoris*). They sacrificed gilded oxen to Jupiter, cows to Juno and Minerva, and offerings to *Salus Publica*.
- **Imperial Birthdays and Accessions:** The Arvales performed sacrifices on the emperor's birthday (*dies natalis*), his day of accession to imperium (*dies imperii*), his assumption of the tribunician power (*tribunicia potestas*), and his victories over foreign enemies.
- **Dynastic Crises:** During political conspiracies, the brethren convened to offer thanksgiving sacrifices. Following the Pisonian conspiracy against Nero in 65 CE, the Arvales sacrificed numerous victims to the gods for preserving the emperor's life; after Nero's suicide in 68 CE, the brethren quickly assembled under Galba to perform sacrifices for the new ruler, demonstrating the sodality’s function as a barometer of senatorial submission to the reigning military victor.

## The *Acta Arvalia* (Inscribed Records)

Between 28 BCE and 304 CE, the brethren meticulously engraved the minutes of their proceedings on the marble columns, revetments, and walls of the temple and round pavilion (*caesareum*) within the grove of Dea Dia. These *Acta* recorded:
- The exact calendar dates and names of the presiding *magister*, *flamen*, and attending brothers.
- Precise locations of meetings (e.g., in the Pronaos of the Temple of Concord, on the Palatine in the imperial library, or within the grove).
- Minute inventories of animal victims (age, sex, color, gilded horns).
- Verbatim transcriptions of oaths, prayers, and imperial letters addressed to the sodality.

Archaeological excavations at the site since the sixteenth century (most systematically in the nineteenth century by Wilhelm Henzen) have recovered hundreds of inscribed fragments, providing an unparalleled prosopographical register of the Roman senatorial aristocracy across three centuries. The sodality finally dissolved in the early fourth century CE as Constantine and his successors redirected imperial patronage away from traditional polytheistic sodalities toward the Christian Church.
