# The Trial of Lucius Opimius for Killing Citizens Without Trial

A year after serving as the consul who crushed Gaius Gracchus's movement under the Senate's first senatus consultum ultimum, Lucius Opimius was brought to trial in 120 BC on the question of whether he had acted lawfully in putting citizens to death without a formal hearing. The prosecutor, Gaius Decius, framed the issue starkly in a line preserved by Cicero: that not even the most wicked citizen could lawfully be killed without trial. Opimius's defense, by contrast, rested on the claim that he had acted "rightly, for the sake of the safety of all and the preservation of the Republic."

## Parties & Stakes

- **Lucius Opimius** ([[entities/lucius-opimius]]) — goal: avoid conviction for the killings carried out under his emergency decree; stakes: his life and the legal legitimacy of the senatus consultum ultimum itself
- **Gaius Decius** ([[entities/gaius-decius]]) — goal: establish that no decree could override a citizen's right to trial; stakes: the principle of due process
- **The Roman people (as jury/assembly)** — stakes: whether to ratify a new tool of emergency rule that bypassed ordinary legal protections

## What Happened

The trial turned on a genuinely novel constitutional question: could the Senate's declaration that "the consul should see that the State took no harm" lawfully authorize killing citizens without trial? Decius argued no; Opimius argued that necessity justified his actions. The case was argued before the Roman people rather than purely a jury of senators.^[ambiguous]

## Outcome

Opimius was acquitted. The verdict effectively validated the senatus consultum ultimum as a legitimate (if controversial) emergency power, a precedent invoked repeatedly in later Roman political crises, including against the Catilinarian conspirators decades later. Opimius went on to build a [[entities/temple-of-concordia|temple to Concordia]] in [[entities/rome|Rome]], provoking a bitter graffito suggesting the "doctor" of civil strife might have been madder than the "patient."

## Open Questions

- The exact composition of the court (full popular assembly versus a special tribunal) is unclear from the fragmentary rhetorical sources Greenidge cites.
- The outcome of a related, less-detailed prosecution and condemnation of the radical politician Carbo the following year (119 BC) is mentioned only in passing and not detailed enough here to stand as its own scenario.
