# Lex Sempronia Agraria and the Gracchan Land Commission

The *lex Sempronia agraria* of 133 BC, proposed by the tribune Tiberius Gracchus, is more than the single famous bill summarized in [[concepts/land-and-agrarian-conflict]]: it created a standing three-man commission, the *triumviri agris iudicandis adsignandis* ("three men for judging and assigning land"), charged with an enormous administrative task — and its machinery, judicial powers, and eventual dismantling over the following twenty-two years can be reconstructed from Appian, the *Epitome* of Livy, and the epigraphic *Lex agraria* of 111 BC.

## Key Ideas

- **Terms of the cap:** Appian records the holding limit on *ager publicus* for existing possessors (*veteres possessores*) as 500 *iugera*, plus 250 *iugera* per child. In contrast, Livy's *Epitome* and *De viris illustribus* give a flat 1,000-*iugera* figure. Reconstructions either reconcile these sources by assuming an allowance capped at two children (500 plus 250 per child up to 1,000), or explain the 1,000 figure as a scribal corruption of Roman numerals (D versus M), reading the statute as imposing no arbitrary ceiling on qualifying children. ^[extracted]
- **Scope: public land only.** The epigraphic *Lex agraria* of 111 BC confirms that the Gracchan law targeted *ager publicus* exclusively: excess holdings above the statutory limit were reclaimed for redistribution, while private landholdings remained entirely exempt. ^[extracted]
- **The commission's real function was adjudication, not just distribution.** Appian reports the single hardest problem the *tresviri* faced was simply determining which land was public and which private, since decades of informal buying, selling, and inheriting of occupied *ager publicus* had erased the paper trail: "not all owners had kept their contracts of sale or titles of allotment, and such as were actually discovered were inconclusive." The commission's founding grant of *iudicandis* (judging) power, not just *adsignandis* (assigning) power, reflects that this adjudication — cataloguing possession and ownership across Italy — was the primary obstacle, not a side issue. ^[extracted]
- **Allotment size:** The common claim that settlers received 30 *iugera* each is not directly documented for the 133 BC law. That figure derives from a clause in the 111 BC statute (lines 13–14) setting a maximum ceiling for a later, separate privatization measure. Since earlier Roman colonial allotments rarely exceeded 10 *iugera*, the actual Gracchan plot size remains unproven. ^[extracted]
- **Suspension of judicial powers (129 BC):** Following complaints from Italian allies who held public land without formal title deeds, the Senate revoked the commission's authority to adjudicate contested boundaries (*iudicandis* power) in 129 BC, leaving it able only to assign undisputed parcels. Despite this curtailment, boundary stones (*termini*) show that substantial land across southern Italy had already been surveyed and redistributed. ^[extracted]
- **Dismantling the system:** Appian's compressed narrative (*BC* 1.27) reflects three successive legislative steps over twenty-two years: (1) a measure permitting the sale of previously inalienable holdings; (2) M. Livius Drusus's abolition (c. 122 BC) of rent (*vectigal*) on distributed Gracchan allotments; and (3) the *Lex Thoria* (c. 119/118 BC), which halted distributions and converted remaining possessed lands into private property subject to rent. ^[extracted]
- **Abolition of rent (111 BC):** The epigraphic *Lex agraria* of 111 BC completed this reversal by abolishing the rent established under the *Lex Thoria*, conferring full, untaxed private ownership on possessors of former *ager publicus*. In Appian's assessment, ending this rent eliminated the final revenue source earmarked for the poor, effectively extinguishing the Gracchan agrarian program. ^[extracted]

## Open Questions

- Whether the initial post-Gracchan law permitting sale applied to the original possessors or to the new Gracchan allotment-holders remains an open textual and legal dispute in interpreting Appian. ^[ambiguous]
- The statutory date of the *Lex Thoria* remains contested in surviving evidence, with proposed dates ranging from c. 121 to 112 BC.
