# Laudatio Funebris (Roman Funeral Eulogy)

The *laudatio funebris* was a eulogy delivered at an aristocratic Roman's funeral and explicitly understood by ancient authors as a piece of family archive-keeping, not a private tribute: families "kept [these speeches] as a sort of honour and a record, in order to preserve the memory of the achievements of the family and document its nobility," as Polybius observed. Because the genre supplied nearly the only detailed narrative evidence (beyond the bare consular *fasti* and the terse *Annales Maximi*) available to later annalists reconstructing the early-to-middle Republic, its reliability problem is foundational to how confidently any family history, including the [[entities/clan-fabius|Fabii Maximi]]'s, can be told.

## Key Ideas

- Polybius's charge against the genre names three concrete categories of fabrication ancient critics identified: "invented triumphs, additional consulships, false claims to patrician status" — military honors never won, magistracies never held, and genealogical/legal rank never actually possessed.
- Livy levels a closely related but distinct charge, not against the spoken eulogies alone but against the inscriptions (*tituli*) placed beneath the wax ancestor portrait busts (*imagines*) displayed in aristocratic homes: "funeral eulogies and fictitious inscriptions on portrait busts" let "families try to appropriate to themselves the tradition of exploits and titles of office," confusing both individual achievement records and public records of events.
Funerary memorials could distort official records: ancient sources record that family eulogies and tituli inflated Q. Fabius Maximus Cunctator's command in 217 BC into a full constitutional dictatorship.
- A second concrete, checkable example: Livy complains that competing family traditions left it unclear whether the dictator Aulus Cornelius Arvina or the consuls Fabius Rullianus and Fulvius actually earned a 322 BC Samnite-war triumph, but the *Fasti Triumphales* — a later, independently engraved public inscription listing all Roman triumphs — confirms Rullianus and Fulvius did triumph, showing the fasti could occasionally arbitrate between a family's embellished claim and a more neutral record. ^[inferred]
Aristocratic eulogies and ancestral portrait traditions (*imagines*) functioned as instruments of family prestige, making them unreliable for verifying historical events without independent epigraphic check.

## Open Questions

Ancient historians explicitly acknowledged that funeral eulogies corrupted public records, though establishing criteria to separate historical truth from family embellishment remains difficult.
- Whether a family's laudatio tradition was altered by direct retouching of the written/inscribed record (as with the Cunctator "dictator" title) versus simple repeated recitation of an already-corrupted oral account is not detailed beyond the single Cunctator example. ^[ambiguous]
