# Diocletian’s Palace at Split

Diocletian’s palace at Split combines a monumental residence with conspicuous features borrowed from military architecture. Its fortress-like appearance has strongly influenced interpretation, but Chen argues that military resemblance should not be confused with an overt military function.

## The language of a fortress

A curtain wall encircles the complex, with octagonal towers at gates and rectangular towers between them. Square chambers immediately inside the gates, termed *propugnaculi*, resemble devices intended in fortress design to delay attackers after a breach. Inside, principal north–south and east–west roads organize space symmetrically, recalling Roman camps.

These features helped establish an early scholarly habit of treating the palace primarily through military parallels. Diocletian’s rise from the army and the military backgrounds of his tetrarchic colleagues reinforced that interpretation.

## A residence, not a combat position

The siting complicates a strictly defensive explanation. The palace occupied low ground in a peaceful coastal area despite the availability of more defensible heights. Its rectangular corner towers departed from the rounded forms preferred by contemporary military engineers. Excavation in the northern sector also failed to reveal the expected barracks.

The combination of defensive forms and spaces graded from public to private is therefore usually understood as a hybrid of military and villa architecture. Comparisons with Galerius’s fortified palace at Romuliana and the unfinished complex at Šarkamen suggest a broader tetrarchic architectural trend: imposing walls and towers could communicate imperial grandeur as well as protection.
