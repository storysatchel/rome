# Roman Household

The Roman *familia* was a social and legal unit — not merely a physical residence — comprising free, freed, and enslaved members bound together under the authority of the [[entities/pater-familias]] (and, in healthcare matters, often the [[entities/mater-familias]]). This page treats the household as an institution; for its physical setting see [[concepts/roman-house]], and for specific farm-labor staffing roles see [[concepts/roman-household-staff]]. ^[inferred]

## Key Ideas

- **Draycott's definition of the household.** Draycott explicitly frames the household as comprising "the house, the garden, and its free, freed, and enslaved members," arguing it functioned as an active, under-recognized site within the Roman "medical marketplace" — a "medicine from below" existing alongside, not subordinate to, professional physicians. Her book's third thematic chapter treats the household's human members specifically — [[entities/pater-familias]], [[entities/mater-familias]], the estate manager, housekeeper, slaves, and freedpersons — and their respective healthcare roles.
- **Internal healthcare staff as evidence of household structure.** Epigraphic evidence from elite *columbaria* shows large numbers of household staff with specialized healthcare roles: the *familia urbana* of Livia Drusilla (Julia Augusta) included fifteen individuals with healthcare-related epitaphs — eight *medici* (including a "chief physician," an eye doctor, and a surgeon), two midwives, two infirmary attendants, an anointer, and two makers of oils/unguents. Comparable patterns in other elite households (Statilii, Volusii, Iunii) suggest aristocratic households routinely maintained internal healthcare staff as part of the household's normal composition, rather than relying solely on external physicians — and owners sometimes lent or hired out their household physicians to friends or even the public.
- **The household as an estate management unit.** Cato's and Varro's farm manuals describe the *pater familias*'s authority as exercised concretely through periodic personal inspection tours, cross-examining the overseer (*vilicus*) on work completed — described as the core mechanism of estate accountability — and through detailed rationing schedules for the *familia rustica* (meal, bread, wine, and salt allowances by role and season), illustrating the material economy binding together a slave-staffed household. See [[concepts/roman-household-staff]] for the specific staffing roles this describes.
- Both sources converge on treating the household as a functioning economic and social unit whose free and unfree members had defined roles (overseer, housekeeper, physician, midwife) rather than an undifferentiated mass of "slaves" — though Cato/Varro's lens is a working farm's labor discipline while Draycott's is an urban elite household's healthcare provision. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- Neither Cato/Varro nor Draycott directly addresses the lived experience or interiority of enslaved household members; Cato/Varro's own text explicitly notes this gap, visible only through management instructions and ration tables.
- How far do Draycott's findings on specialized medical staffing in elite urban *familiae* (Julia Augusta, Statilii) extend to the kind of working rural *familia rustica* Cato and Varro describe? Neither source bridges these two household types directly.
