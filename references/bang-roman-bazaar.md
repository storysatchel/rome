# The Roman Bazaar

Peter Fibiger Bang (2008, Cambridge Classical Studies). A major comparative-economic-history reassessment of trade and markets in the Roman Empire, written against the backdrop of the decades-long "ancient economy" debate kicked off by Moses Finley's *The Ancient Economy* (1973).

## Core Argument / Thesis

Bang rejects both poles of the standard debate: the **"primitivist"** view (Finley) which sees the Roman economy as fundamentally exploitative/predatory and economically undeveloped, and the **"modernist"** view (Rostovtzeff and successors) which imagines something close to an integrated, laissez-faire market economy with a cosmopolitan bourgeoisie. Both images appear in tension in Aelius Aristeides’ 2nd-century panegyric *To Rome*, which depicts Rome simultaneously as a liberal magnet drawing in the world’s goods and as an all-commanding tributary power. An alternative comparative framework reads the Roman economy through **Mughal India** (17th-century) rather than taking early modern Europe as the benchmark for a "modernization narrative." ^[extracted]

## Key Claims and Evidence

- **The "tributary empire" model:** In this model, Rome and Mughal India, despite enormous cultural differences, shared a fundamentally similar political economy — large pre-industrial agrarian empires that extracted surplus from peasant populations via tribute/taxation, as opposed to the developing competitive state-system of early modern Europe. This shared structural condition, not cultural resemblance, licenses the comparison. ^[extracted]
- **The "bazaar" model of exchange:** In the bazaar model, Roman (and Mughal) markets were **opaque, volatile, and discontinuously connected** rather than integrated into anything resembling a single transparent price system. Merchants responded to this endemic uncertainty not by seeking impersonal market mechanisms but through **clientelization** — cultivating personal, trusted, lasting relationships with specific business partners — because a generalized, anonymous "market" sphere was largely unavailable. ^[extracted]
- **Elite wealth stayed agrarian, not mercantile:** unlike the Dutch Republic (where merchant capital could rival and even fund the landed/political elite), Roman landowners kept the upper hand over commercial middlemen, shifting most market risk onto merchants via contract and legal convention. Elite social mobility in the empire tracked access to large agricultural estates, not commercial fortune — exemplified by the rise of Lepcis Magna's landed elite (culminating in the Severan emperors), whose rise is explained in this reconstruction by their agricultural hinterland, not their port's trade volume. ^[extracted]
- **No early-modern-style mercantilism:** Within this comparison, it would be anachronistic to expect the Roman state to wage wars primarily to protect or expand commercial interests (as 17th-century Dutch/Spanish conflicts did) — Roman war-and-peace calculations only "on very rare occasions" factored in trader safety, a sharp contrast with the mercantilist European pattern Adam Smith later criticized in *The Wealth of Nations*. ^[extracted]
- **Structure of the book:** Part I lays out the comparative-historical method and the agrarian-empire framework (with Roman GDP/surplus estimates and comparison tables against Mughal data); Part II ("Imperial Bazaar") examines actual market conditions — price volatility (using detailed Roman Egyptian grain/wine/barley price series), the *portorium* customs-tax system and its relationship to "protection" of trade routes, and the communal infrastructure (cult, courts, credit) that let the bazaar function despite its structural uncertainty. ^[extracted]

## Structure / Scope

Prolegomena (methodology) + Part I: "The Roman Empire and the Comparative Study of Pre-Industrial Society" (ch. 1 critiques prior trade/comparative-history approaches; ch. 2 situates interregional trade within the agrarian-tributary framework) + Part II: "Imperial Bazaar" (ch. 3 market conditions; ch. 4 customs/protection/predation; ch. 5 the social infrastructure of exchange) + Epilegomena (synthesis). Heavy use of Roman Egyptian papyrological price data and extensive comparison with Mughal Indian economic historiography throughout.

## Significance

The landmark "third way" in the primitivist/modernist ancient-economy debate; directly relevant to [[concepts/roman-provincial-administration]] (tribute/taxation as the empire's basic economic logic) and to [[references/goodman-roman-city-periphery|Goodman's]] parallel debate over whether Roman cities were primarily political or economic entities — Bang's answer (tribute-driven, not market-integrated, but not merely predatory either) offers a specific economic mechanism underlying that broader urbanism debate. ^[inferred]
