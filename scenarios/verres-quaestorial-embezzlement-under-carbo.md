# Verres's Quaestorial Embezzlement Under Carbo

Long before Gaius Verres became notorious as the extortionate governor of Sicily, he served as quaestor to the consul Cn. Papirius Carbo on campaign in the early 80s BC. Cicero, prosecuting Verres years later for his conduct in Sicily, dredged up this earlier episode as further evidence of character: as Carbo's quaestor, Verres embezzled from the army's funds and concealed the theft by keeping two separate sets of account books — one genuine, one he was nominally keeping "on the consul's behalf."

## Parties & Stakes

- **Gaius Verres** ([[entities/gaius-verres]]) — goal: personal enrichment from funds under his quaestorial control; resources: his position of trust as quaestor, which gave him legitimate custody of the army's accounts and the opportunity to falsify them.
- **Cn. Papirius Carbo** (unnamed entity, consul) — stakes: the integrity of his campaign's finances and his own reputation, compromised by his quaestor's theft; the personal bond of trust between a general and his quaestor — assigned by lot and treated by Roman custom as near-sacred — was itself violated.

## What Happened

Roman quaestors held a formal, lot-assigned relationship with the commanders they served, described by Cicero as bound by a kind of sacred obligation (*sortis necessitudinem religionemque*) comparable to the bond tying a soldier to the army as a whole. As Carbo's quaestor, Verres was entrusted with managing military funds — pay, grain, and disbursements to legates. Instead, he embezzled from these funds, and was able to conceal the theft because he was keeping two sets of books: his own official quaestorial accounts, and a duplicate set he was nominally maintaining on the consul's behalf, which let him manipulate the figures reported upward. ^[extracted]

The episode only survives because Cicero, prosecuting Verres decades later for extortion during his governorship of Sicily, cited it as a pattern of prior misconduct — part of a broader argument that Verres's later Sicilian crimes were not an aberration but a continuation of established habits. Cicero framed the earlier offense as much a violation of the quaestor-general personal bond as a simple financial crime, arguing that abandoning that bond was "tantamount to deserting the army as a whole."

## Outcome

Verres evidently escaped formal consequences for the Carbo-era embezzlement at the time it occurred; it resurfaced only as supporting evidence in his later, separate prosecution for the Sicilian extortion case. The episode illustrates how the quaestor's position — trusted with real financial authority but formally subordinate to his general — created a structural opportunity for concealment that an unscrupulous quaestor could exploit undetected for years.

## Open Questions

- The precise date and campaign during which Verres served as Carbo's quaestor, and the exact sums involved, are not specified beyond Cicero's later, selectively framed account. ^[ambiguous]
- Whether Carbo himself was aware of or complicit in the fraud (since one set of books was nominally kept "on his behalf") is left ambiguous by the source. ^[ambiguous]
