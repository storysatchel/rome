# The Voting-Tribes Feud: Rullianus Undoes Appius Claudius's Censorship (304 BC)

Appius Claudius's censorship of 312 BC was, in Livy's telling, highly controversial — among other things, it reassigned low-born voters across the Roman voting tribes in a way that, the sources imply, boosted the political power of a rising "forum faction" of urban, lower-status citizens. That faction was reportedly responsible for electing Gnaeus Flavius — son of a freed slave — to the curule aedileship, where he broke aristocratic and priestly privilege by publicizing the previously secret rules of civil lawsuits and the religious calendar.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Appius Claudius Caecus** ([[entities/appius-claudius-caecus]]) — goal: expand the political weight of lower-status voters (and, per his critics, his own faction's influence) through tribal reassignment as censor; resources: censorial authority in 312 BC, consular rank in 307 and 296 BC.
- **Quintus Fabius Rullianus (Maximus)** ([[entities/quintus-fabius-rullianus]]) — goal: reverse Claudius's tribal reassignments and restore the prior aristocratic balance of voting power; resources: censorial authority alongside Publius Decius Mus in 304 BC.
- **Gnaeus Flavius** ([[entities/gnaeus-flavius-aedile]]) — role: beneficiary of the political shift enabled by Claudius's censorship; not a direct combatant in the feud but its most visible symptom.

## What Happened

When Rullianus and Decius Mus held the censorship of 304 BC, Rullianus took deliberate steps to unravel measures Claudius had put in place eight years earlier. According to a source quoted by McCall, "Fabius, for the sake of concord and at the same time to prevent elections remaining in the hands of the lowest-born, sifted out all the Forum crowd, throwing them into four tribes that he called 'urban.'" The move was reportedly so well received among the aristocracy that it earned Rullianus the cognomen "Maximus" — a title, McCall notes dryly, that his many military victories alone had not secured for him. ^[extracted] McCall suggests the underlying rivalry likely began with Claudius's opposition to Rullianus receiving a proconsulship in 307 BC, and speculates this friction originated in or was compounded by disagreements over the earlier 312 BC censorship itself. ^[inferred]

## Outcome

Rullianus's reorganization confined the "forum crowd" (urban, likely freedman-descended voters) into four designated "urban" tribes, diluting their influence compared to the broader distribution Claudius had arranged — effectively reversing his rival's reform. Rullianus gained enormous prestige from the move, acquiring the cognomen "Maximus."

## Open Questions

- Livy's hostile sources may have exaggerated or distorted both Claudius's original reform and its motives; McCall explicitly flags this ancient hostility toward Claudius as a potential source of bias. ^[ambiguous]
- The precise mechanics of how voters were reassigned under either censorship are, per McCall, "impenetrable" from the surviving evidence.
