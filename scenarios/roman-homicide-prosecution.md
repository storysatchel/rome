# Roman Homicide Prosecution

The response to the killing of Tiberius Gracchus reveals a deep division over the use of lethal force against Roman citizens. Participants fought with rocks, sticks, and broken furniture rather than exclusively military weapons. The killing may not have constituted a crime under an existing Roman statute; this is a disputed interpretation of its legal treatment, not an assertion that Romans unanimously approved it.

## The absence of recorded prosecutions

The men identified as having beaten Tiberius to death, Publius Satyreius and Lucius Rufus, are not recorded as having faced trial. Rufus was even remembered as boasting of his role. Satyreius, himself a tribune of the plebs, enjoyed immunity during his term, but no subsequent prosecution is recorded either.

The absence of recorded prosecutions is significant because the incident and its consequences appear in several accounts. It remains an argument from the surviving record, not direct evidence of a formal acquittal or a judicial ruling that the killing was lawful.

## A divided city

Appian describes Rome as divided between sorrow and joy. Some mourned Gracchus and believed constitutional government had given way to violence; others welcomed what had happened. The lack of a recorded trial thus coexisted with profound disagreement over the political legitimacy of the killing.

## Refusal before the killing, endorsement afterward

Before the attack, Nasica urged the consul Publius Mucius Scaevola to suppress what he called tyranny. In Plutarch’s account, Scaevola refused to use violence or put a citizen to death without trial, while saying that he would not uphold an unlawful popular vote. His position separated opposition to Tiberius’s measures from acceptance of summary killing.

Cicero later claimed that Scaevola defended and honoured Nasica’s deed through senatorial decrees after the event. An extraordinary commission investigated Tiberius’s surviving associates. Their prosecution may have served both as a response to perceived danger and as a retrospective justification of the leader’s death. Blossius of Cumae, questioned over his willingness to obey Tiberius even if ordered to burn Jupiter’s temple, fled into exile. Ancient accounts disagree over whether Nasica or Laelius conducted that questioning. The aftermath therefore involved active proceedings against the dead man’s followers, not simply judicial silence about political violence as a whole.
