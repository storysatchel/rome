# Curia/Theatre of Pompey

Julius Caesar was assassinated on 15 March 44 BCE in the Curia attached to the Theatre of Pompey. The choice of a Senate meeting in this complex gave the conspirators an opportunity to attack him before a political audience while separating him from his armed escort.

## Why the meeting mattered

The conspirators had considered attacks during elections, on the Sacred Way, and at the theatre entrance. These alternatives risked a fight with Caesar’s armed companions; an attack near his official residence on the Sacred Way also carried an especially troubling religious significance.

According to *Rome’s Enemies Within*, a dispute over Caesar’s proposal to have Dolabella replace him as consul led to the Senate meeting scheduled for the Ides of March. Armed equites, freedmen, and slaves could not enter the chamber. Caesar’s senatorial supporters would sit at a distance, although Antony, as consul, needed to be delayed because his place was beside Caesar.

## A political stage

A large statue of Pompey overlooked the room, giving Caesar’s death beneath his former rival’s image a symbolism the assassins could associate with restoring the Republic. The entire Senate could witness the act. Gladiatorial games in the theatre complex also meant that Decimus Brutus’s gladiators were nearby if assistance became necessary. These advantages explain the setting’s role in the plot without implying that its aftermath fulfilled the conspirators’ expectations.
