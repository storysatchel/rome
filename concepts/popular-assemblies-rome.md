# Popular Assemblies of Rome

The Roman Republic's popular will was expressed through several distinct voting assemblies — the *comitia curiata*, *comitia centuriata*, *comitia tributa*, and the plebeian *concilium plebis* — each with its own voting-unit basis, jurisdiction, and typical presiding magistrate, together standing institutionally apart from the non-voting *contio*.

## Key Ideas

- Sandberg identifies three distinguishable assembly types by their voting-unit basis: the *comitia curiata* (by *curiae*, the oldest, reflecting primitive clan society and by the late Republic reduced to 30 lictors representing the curiae rather than actual citizen attendance), the *comitia centuriata* (by *centuriae*, traditionally instituted by Servius Tullius, a military-derived body that could only meet *extra pomerium* and became chiefly an electoral body for the chief magistracies), and the *comitia tributa* (by the 35 tribes).
- Botsford draws a sharp, foundational terminological distinction — later treated as basic by Sandberg and other scholars — between the *contio* (a meeting called for debate or announcement, reaching no formal decision) and the *comitia* proper (a voting assembly reaching a binding decision); see [[concepts/contio]] for the deliberative counterpart to the voting bodies described here.
- The curiate assembly survived into the Republic chiefly to pass the *lex curiata de imperio* confirming the *imperium* of newly elected magistrates. Botsford argues — reading Cicero's *De lege agraria* against the ancient sources — that the centuries or tribes actually *conferred* imperium or *potestas* on magistrates, while the curiate assembly's role was merely to confirm or formally sanction a grant already made; this bears directly on how the [[concepts/consulship]] derived its legal authority.
- Gargola notes that officials holding the *ius agendi cum populo* could summon citizens either to *contiones* or to *comitia*/*concilia*; the *comitia centuriata* was presided over by a consul and elected consuls, praetors, and censors, while tribal assemblies typically approved legislation under a tribune from the mid-Republic onward — tying assembly structure directly to the [[concepts/roman-magistracies|magistracy]] presiding over it.
- Pina Polo's reading of Polybius frames consuls as summoning and presiding over the popular assemblies as part of their civil headship of Rome, applying laws the assemblies had already passed — the consulship's relationship to the assemblies is treated at length in [[concepts/consulship]] rather than duplicated here.
- Sandberg's central thesis, against the "commonly assumed" scholarly consensus (Ernst Meyer, Jochen Bleicken) that after the *lex Hortensia* of 287 BC assembly choice was essentially indifferent, is that specific magistracies had discernible, non-random preferences for specific legislative assemblies — since it is well established which assembly *elected* which magistrates, Sandberg argues the same differentiation likely held for legislation. ^[inferred] (synthesis of Sandberg's demonstrated correlation, drawn across his evidence of magistrate-assembly pairing)
- A bill (*rogatio*) underwent formal *promulgatio* (public posting, typically weeks ahead) followed by debate in a series of *contiones* before the *contio* was dissolved and reconstituted as a *comitia* for voting; citizens could only answer *uti rogas* ("as you propose") or *antiquo* ("I prefer the old state of things"), never amend the text — illustrating the two-stage contio-then-comitia procedure common to the Republic's legislative process.
- The *patrum auctoritas* — formal senatorial assent once required for comitial decisions to take legal effect — became a mere formality over time, part of a broader trajectory from aristocratic to more genuinely popular constitutional practice; see [[concepts/roman-senate]] for the senate's side of this relationship. ^[inferred]
- The *comitia tributa* and *concilium plebis* grew in political importance from 449 BC onward, with jurisdiction and legislative functions expanding markedly after the *lex Hortensia* of 287 BC gave plebiscites full force of law, eventually making *lex* (a decision of the full *populus*) and *plebiscitum* (a decision of the *concilium plebis*) interchangeable in common usage.

## Open Questions

- The precise institutional boundary between the *concilium plebis* and the *comitia tributa* is disputed: some scholars treat them as wholly distinct bodies, others as effectively identical. Both Botsford and Sandberg flag this as unresolved — Sandberg explicitly reserves it for extended treatment as a problem of "greatest relevance" to assembly studies. ^[ambiguous]
- Whether the differentiated magistrate-assembly pairing Sandberg demonstrates for elections also held cleanly for every category of legislation, or was a looser tendency rather than a strict rule, is the subject of his book's broader (not fully closed) argument. ^[ambiguous]
