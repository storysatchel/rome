# Lex Sempronia Agraria and the Gracchan Land Commission

The *lex Sempronia agraria* of 133 BC, proposed by the tribune Tiberius Gracchus, is more than the single famous bill summarized in [[concepts/land-and-agrarian-conflict]]: it created a standing three-man commission, the *triumviri agris iudicandis adsignandis* ("three men for judging and assigning land"), charged with an enormous administrative task — and its machinery, powers, and eventual dismantling over the following twenty-two years are reconstructed in detail by Roselaar (see [[references/roselaar-public-land-roman-republic]]) from Appian, the *Epitome* of Livy, and the epigraphic Lex agraria of 111 BC.

## Key Ideas

- **The law's actual terms, disputed down to the number:** Appian gives the cap on *ager publicus* held by existing possessors (*veteres possessores*) as 500 *iugera*, with half that (250 *iugera*) per child; the *Epitome* of Livy and *De viris illustribus* instead give a flat 1,000-*iugera* cap, leading many scholars to assume "500 plus 250 per two children" to reconcile the figures. Roselaar rejects the two-children cap as un-evidenced invention and argues the confusion is simply a manuscript corruption of similar-looking Roman numerals (D versus M); she reads the law as 500 *iugera* for the holder plus 250 *iugera* per child, with **no limit on the number of children** — consistent with the law's explicit aim of reversing population decline, which a two-child cap would have undercut. ^[extracted]
- **Scope: public land only, unlike its model.** Where the earlier [[entities/lex-licinia-de-modo-agrorum|Lex Licinia of 367 BC]] may (per Roselaar) have capped combined public-and-private holdings, the Lex agraria of 111 BC's references back to "whatever public land of the Roman people there was... in the consulship of P. Mucius and L. Calpurnius" (133 BC) confirm the Gracchan law targeted *ager publicus* exclusively — excess holdings above the cap were confiscated and redistributed, but a possessor's private land was untouched. ^[extracted]
- **The commission's real function was adjudication, not just distribution.** Appian reports the single hardest problem the *tresviri* faced was simply determining which land was public and which private, since decades of informal buying, selling, and inheriting of occupied *ager publicus* had erased the paper trail: "not all owners had kept their contracts of sale or titles of allotment, and such as were actually discovered were inconclusive." The commission's founding grant of *iudicandis* (judging) power, not just *adsignandis* (assigning) power, reflects that this adjudication — cataloguing possession and ownership across Italy — was the primary obstacle, not a side issue. ^[extracted]
- **Allotment size is not securely known.** The frequently cited figure of 30 *iugera* per settler rests on a single surviving clause of the Lex agraria of 111 BC (lines 13-14) specifying that plots up to 30 *iugera* "shall be private" — a maximum threshold for a *later, unrelated* privatization provision, not documented evidence of the actual Gracchan allotment size. Roselaar notes this figure is far larger than earlier Roman colonial allotments (never over 10 *iugera*) and treats the 30-*iugera* figure as an unproven inference that "many scholars" make without justification. ^[extracted]
- **129 BC: judicial powers stripped.** The commission's authority to adjudicate disputed land titles (its *iudicandis* function) was abrogated in 129 BC, likely at Scipio Aemilianus's instigation after allied complaints, leaving it only able to *assign* already-settled land. Roselaar stresses that measuring and assigning some 1.3 million *iugera* in the commission's first four years (133-129 BC), before this setback, was "certainly an impressive achievement" given the scale of the title-sorting problem described above. ^[extracted]
- **Three distinct post-Gracchan laws unwound the system over 22 years, not one.** Appian's compressed narrative (*BC* 1.27) describes a sequence Roselaar disentangles into three separate measures: (1) a law — datable to the 120s, likely applying to *veteres possessores* rather than new Gracchan settlers — permitting sale of previously inalienable public-land holdings, distinct from (2) M. Livius Drusus's separate abolition (c. 122 BC) of the *vectigal* specifically on land already distributed to Gaius Gracchus's own settlers, which was in turn distinct from (3) the *Lex Thoria* (c. 119/118 BC, proposed by the tribune Spurius Thorius), which ended all further land distributions and made the *veteres possessores*' remaining holdings private property — but burdened with a new *vectigal* paid to the people. ^[extracted]
- **111 BC: the final step.** Roselaar identifies the epigraphic Lex agraria of 111 BC as the fourth and last post-Gracchan measure, abolishing the *vectigal* the Lex Thoria had just imposed — completing full, untaxed private ownership of former *ager publicus* for the *veteres possessores* within 22 years of Tiberius Gracchus's original law. Appian's own verdict, writing centuries later, was that the *vectigal*'s abolition "deprived the people of absolutely everything," since the rent revenue had at least funded further public distributions to the poor; its loss, combined with the end of distributions, is what Appian treats as the Gracchan program's effective death, roughly fifteen years after Tiberius's tribunate. ^[extracted]

## Open Questions

- Whether the first post-Gracchan "sale" law (Appian's opening clause) applied to *veteres possessores* or to Gracchan settlers themselves remains genuinely disputed; Roselaar argues for *veteres possessores* on textual grounds (Appian's "this too" phrasing implies a second, distinct prohibition from the one already discussed) but acknowledges most modern translators and editors still read it the other way. ^[ambiguous]
- The precise date of the Lex Thoria is itself contested in the scholarship Roselaar surveys (ranging from c. 121 to 113/112 BC), which in turn affects how many years actually separated Tiberius Gracchus's original cap-and-redistribute law from its full reversal into untaxed private property — this page gives Roselaar's preferred reconstruction (c. 119/118 BC) without treating the chronology as settled.
