# Clan Fabius, Defenders of Rome: A History of the Republic's Most Illustrious Family

Jeremiah McCall's *Clan Fabius, Defenders of Rome* (Pen & Sword History, 2018) is a narrative history tracing the Fabius Maximus family — later styled the gens Fabia's most prominent branch — from its legendary origins and the disaster at the Cremera (479 BC, per tradition) through its political eclipse under the Empire. The book doubles as a case study in how Roman historical evidence (fasti, the *Annales Maximi*, aristocratic funeral eulogies, oral tradition) was constructed and potentially distorted over centuries before reaching the surviving literary sources (Livy, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Plutarch, Polybius).

## Core Argument / Thesis

The Fabii Maximi's trajectory offers a possible microcosm of the Republic itself: "the rise and fall of the Fabius Maximus family, in a real sense, parallels the rise and fall of the Roman Republic." [[entities/clan-fabius]] The family depended on both inherited pedigree (the Cremera legend, descent claimed from Hercules) and continuous political achievement across generations to maintain aristocratic standing — when political success faltered, pedigree alone could not sustain the family's prominence, and by the Empire the Fabii Maximi survived only as moral exemplars invoked (often mockingly) against their unworthy descendants. ^[inferred]

## Key Claims and Evidence

- Tradition held that three Fabian brothers (Kaeso, Quintus, Marcus) monopolized the consulship from 485–479 BC, and that the clan subsequently mustered a private force of 306 men (or with clients, ~4,000) to garrison the Cremera River against Veii at their own expense — all but one youth were wiped out in a Veientine ambush; this is a "tale" whose historicity cannot be confirmed from the *fasti* alone.
- Roman historical reconstruction of the early Republic relied on the *Fasti Consulares* (bare lists of consuls, relatively reliable for transmission), the *Annales Maximi* kept by the pontifex maximus (per Cicero's description, simple yearly records on a whitened board), and — critically for narrative detail — aristocratic funeral eulogies (*laudationes funebres*), which Cicero (via Livy and Polybius) explicitly says corrupted the record with "invented triumphs, additional consulships, false claims to patrician status." ^[ambiguous] This source-critical problem is foundational to McCall's method throughout.
- The "Conflict of the Orders" narrative (patricians vs. plebeians) that early Roman historians project onto nearly every year of the 5th–4th centuries BC is likely an oversimplification retrojected by later annalists; the consular *fasti* list several 5th-century consuls whose family names were demonstrably plebeian later, complicating the strict two-class model.
- Fabius Maximus Rullianus received the cognomen "Maximus" ("the Greatest") in 304 BC, founding the Fabii Maximi branch that became the family's most politically significant line, descending through the Fabii Ambusti from the earlier Fabii Vibulani.
- During the Second Punic War, Q. Fabius Maximus Verrucosus earned the epithet *Cunctator* ("the Delayer") for his dictatorship strategy of avoiding pitched battle with Hannibal after Trasimene (217 BC); when his Master of Horse Minucius rashly engaged Hannibal and was nearly destroyed, Fabius's relief force saved the trapped legions, and Minucius publicly submitted to Fabius as "Father" — an episode apparently dramatized by Livy and Plutarch into a moral exemplum of Fabius's superior judgment, though the underlying facts (the rescue itself) are better attested. 
- Ancient sources disputed even the constitutional basis of Fabius's command against Hannibal: Livy notes that "nearly all the annalists" called him dictator, but argues that only the consul Servilius (then in Gaul) had the legal right to name a dictator, so Fabius was technically an "acting dictator" elected by the people under emergency conditions — a title later inflated to full "dictator" by the family's own funeral-inscription embellishments, per Livy's explicit complaint. ^[ambiguous]
- The Fabii Maximi nearly went extinct around 180 BC when all male heirs died out; the line was rescued only when a Fabius Maximus (probably the praetor of 181) adopted one of the sons of L. Aemilius Paullus (conqueror of Macedon), illustrating the Roman aristocratic practice of adoption as a deliberate strategy to preserve a family's name, cults, and political capital when natural succession failed. [[concepts/elite-family-strategy]] ^[inferred]
- By the early Empire, the family's descendants (e.g., Paullus Fabius Persicus, consul AD 34) were mocked by Seneca and Juvenal precisely by invoking the glory of ancestors like Cunctator and the 306 Fabii at the Cremera to shame contemporary degenerate heirs who had achieved rank only through inherited name rather than merit — McCall reads this as marking the practical end of the Fabii Maximi as a politically significant family, concluding (quoting T.S. Eliot) that the line ended "not with a bang, but a whimper."

## Structure / Scope

Seven chapters move chronologically: an introduction establishing Republican institutions (via Polybius) and Roman source-criticism problems; the earliest Fabii under the fledgling Republic (5th century BC); Fabius Rullianus and the Samnite Wars; the Battle of Sentinum (295 BC); Fabius Cunctator against Hannibal; the Fabian strategy and the war's end; and a final chapter covering the Spanish/Gallic wars, civil wars, and the family's decline into the early Empire. Four appendices cover Roman naming conventions, technical terminology, and glossaries of ancient sources. McCall relies principally on Livy and Dionysius of Halicarnassus for the early Republic, Polybius and Livy for the Hannibalic War, and later imperial-period authors (Seneca, Juvenal, Plutarch) for the family's epilogue.

## Significance

McCall explicitly uses the Fabii as a vehicle for teaching source criticism about the Roman Republic generally — the book's introduction devotes substantial space to explaining why evidence for the 5th-century Republic is far weaker than for the 2nd–1st centuries, a methodological framing that shapes how confidently each phase of the family's story can be told. ## Open Questions

- The precise accuracy of the early consular *fasti* and the narrative embellishments attached to them (battle speeches, exact numbers at the Cremera) cannot be verified independently of the (already compromised) family-eulogy tradition. ^[ambiguous]
- Whether the "Conflict of the Orders" as a unified multi-generational struggle is substantially real or a later narrative overlay remains an open historiographical question.
