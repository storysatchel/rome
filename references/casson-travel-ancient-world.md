# Travel in the Ancient World

Lionel Casson (1974, George Allen & Unwin / Hakkert). A wide-ranging survey of travel, from Bronze Age Mesopotamia and Egypt through Roman-era tourism, long treated as a standard reference on the practicalities of ancient mobility.

## Core Argument / Thesis

Casson traces a long arc: travel in the ancient Near East and archaic/classical Greece was overwhelmingly purposeful (trade, diplomacy, military campaign, religious pilgrimage), constrained by political fragmentation, language barriers, and unsafe roads/seas. Rome's conquest and unification of the Mediterranean world created something genuinely new — a shared language of administration (if not everyday speech), common coinage and law, and above all a dense, engineered road network — that for the first time made leisure travel and tourism practical for a meaningful slice of the population, not just the very wealthy or officially sponsored. ^[extracted]

## Key Claims and Evidence

- **"One World":** Part Two of the book, covering Roman times, opens by presenting Rome's unification of the Mediterranean as the structural precondition for the entire later discussion of Roman tourism (Part Three) — before Rome, even the extensive Hellenistic world remained a patchwork of rival kingdoms (Antigonid Macedonia, Seleucid Asia, Ptolemaic Egypt) each a "Greek island" in a non-Greek sea, not a continuous travel space. ^[extracted]
- **Roman road engineering was adaptive, not formulaic:** Casson explicitly debunks the textbook claim that Roman highways were uniformly built to a fixed multi-layer, cement-sealed standard — that account traces to a mistaken 17th-century French scholarly claim repeated uncritically ever since. In practice, construction varied by terrain: straight raised causeways across plains (aiding drainage and visibility in snow), but roads run along valley *sides* rather than valley floors in hilly terrain — deliberately avoiding marshy valley bottoms and fords at a river's full width, and incidentally giving marching troops a tactical slope-advantage against attack from one side. ^[extracted]
- **Concrete engineering feats:** cites the Via Appia cutting at Terracina, where construction crews removed a 126-foot vertical slice of cliff to fit the road between rock and sea — datable because the crew carved a running foot-count into the rock face as they worked — and a still-extant forty-yard tunnel on the Via Flaminia cut in 77 AD. ^[extracted]
- **Roads were primarily military infrastructure with civilian secondary use:** Casson stresses that Roman planners accepted longer, curving routes specifically because the roads were built "first and foremost for the army," with civilian/commercial travel riding on infrastructure whose core purpose was troop movement — a point that nuances [[references/scarre-penguin-atlas-ancient-rome|Scarre's]] trade-route maps, which show the same road network primarily in its commercial-use aspect. ^[inferred]
- **Structure signals a shift from survival/necessity to leisure:** the book's three-part structure itself embodies Casson's thesis — Part One (Near East and Greece) is organized around trade, exploration, and the "first travel writer" (Herodotus); Parts Two and Three (Roman times) add whole chapters on holidays, inns and restaurants, museums, sightseeing itineraries, and guidebook literature ("Baedeker of the Ancient World") — categories that presuppose travel has become routine and often recreational, not merely functional. ^[inferred]

## Structure / Scope

Three parts, 19 chapters: Part One, "The Near East and Greece" (Bronze Age origins through the first travel writer, Herodotus); Part Two, "Travel in Roman Times" (the political/infrastructural unification, miscellaneous travelers, holidays, sea travel, roads, overland travel, inns, the mail system); Part Three, "Tourists and Touring in Roman Times" (what tourists went to see, museums, itinerary-planning, sightseeing practice, ancient guidebooks, and pilgrimage "to the Holy Lands"). First published 1974; a foundational, still widely cited reference in the field.

## Significance

The most comprehensive single source in this wiki on the physical and institutional mechanics of getting around the Roman world — pairs naturally with [[references/hudson-rhetoric-roman-transportation|Hudson's]] literary/rhetorical study of the *vehicles* themselves (Casson covers roads and infrastructure; Hudson covers how Latin literature represents the vehicles that used them) and grounds [[references/scarre-penguin-atlas-ancient-rome|Scarre's]] trade-route cartography in concrete engineering detail. ^[inferred]
