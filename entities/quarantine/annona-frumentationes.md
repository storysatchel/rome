# annona and frumentationes

The *annona*, Rome’s grain supply, was a central element of the relationship between the emperor and citizens during the Principate. Its administration was sufficiently important to be headed by an influential political figure.

## Supply and distribution

Grain obtained for the supply system was distributed through the *frumentationes*. These distributions fed a substantial part of the population, but not everyone. The distinction matters: the political importance of imperial food provision did not mean that all inhabitants depended on, or participated in, the same distribution system.

## Registration and access

The historical overview in *Food at the Heart of the Empire* traces legal provision for grain distribution to Gaius Sempronius Gracchus in 123 BCE. By the later first century BCE, dedicated registers recorded those entitled to a distribution. Eligibility could also change through the transfer or acquisition of a *frumentaria* card bearing a recipient’s name.

Under the imperial system, ordinary eligibility required Roman citizenship, residence in Rome, male sex, and legal age, though exceptions existed. Production on imperial estates and grain collected as tax supplied the system, with Sicily, Africa, and Egypt important in the account. Grain-market intervention and supplementary distributions were therefore parts of a much larger task of feeding the city, not a complete substitute for other supplies.

## Supply is not the same as diet

The article combines this history with stable-isotope analysis of 214 people from six funerary contexts dated to the first–third centuries CE. Its broadly terrestrial dietary pattern and local differences show why administrative provision should not be treated as proof that all inhabitants ate alike. Residence, resources, and access to neighbourhood foods also mattered.
