# Public Lands and Agrarian Laws of the Roman Republic

## Coverage Note
Comprehensive monograph by Andrew Stephenson (Johns Hopkins University Studies in Historical and Political Science, 1891) examining the legal status of *ager publicus* from the archaic period and the Struggle of the Orders through the Licinian-Sextian rogations (367 BC), the Gracchan land commissions (133–121 BC), the post-Gracchan reaction (culminating in the *Lex Thoria* / epigraphic *Lex Agraria* of 111 BC), and the veteran land settlements under Caesar and Augustus.

## Sampled Claims and Institutional Mechanics
- **Quiritarian Ownership vs. Possessio:** Demonstrates that early Roman property law sharply distinguished between formal Quiritarian ownership (*dominium ex jure Quiritium*), which applied strictly to private citizen land (*ager privatus*), and the occupancy (*possessio*) of public domain (*ager publicus*). Ager publicus was acquired by conquest and held subject to state revocation and public rent (*vectigal*).
- **The Licinian-Sextian Rogation (367 BC):** Establishes the statutory ceiling on individual possession of ager publicus at 500 *iugera* (approx. 330 acres), with a limit of 100 head of large cattle (*boves*) and 500 head of small livestock (*oves*) permitted on public pasture (*ager compascuus*), while mandating the employment of a proportional quota of free laborers alongside enslaved workers.
- **The Gracchan Initiative (133 BC):** Analyzes the *Lex Sempronia Agraria* of Tiberius Gracchus as a conservative restoration of the Licinian ceiling, augmented by an allowance of 250 iugera for each son up to a maximum family possession of 1,000 iugera. Surplus land reclaimed by the state was partitioned into inalienable hereditary plots of 30 iugera for impoverished citizens, administered by a standing elective triumvirate (*tresviri agris iudicandis adsignandis*).
- **Three-Stage Post-Gracchan Reaction (121–111 BC):** Traces the legislative dismantling of the Gracchan framework preserved in Appian (*Bellum Civile* 1.27):
  1. A law c. 121 BC lifting the ban on selling assigned plots, leading to immediate recolonization of allotments by wealthy patricians.
  2. The law of the tribune M. Livius Drusus or Borius (c. 119/118 BC) terminating future distributions, guaranteeing tenure to existing possessors, and imposing a rent earmarked for urban grain subsidies.
  3. The *Lex Thoria* (111 BC), identified with the Naples epigraphic bronze *Lex Agraria*, which completely abolished the vectigal on Italian lands, converting all possessed public land into absolute private property (*ager privatus ex jure Quiritium*).
