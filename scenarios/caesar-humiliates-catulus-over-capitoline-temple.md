# Caesar's Public Humiliation of Catulus Over the Capitoline Temple

In 63 BC Julius Caesar had astonished Rome by winning election as pontifex maximus over much more senior candidates, including the eminent Q. Lutatius Catulus — an election widely believed to have been secured through lavish bribery. Catulus, for his part, had accused Caesar of complicity with the Catilinarian conspirators that same year — itself plausibly a retaliation for his defeat in the pontifical election. The grudge match continued into 62 BC, when Caesar, now praetor and newly empowered to call public meetings, moved to settle the score.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Julius Caesar** ([[entities/julius-caesar]]) — goal: publicly embarrass Catulus, display continued support for Pompey, and signal that he was not to be underestimated; resources: praetorian authority to summon contiones, popularity with the people.
- **Q. Lutatius Catulus** ([[entities/quintus-lutatius-catulus]]) — goal: defend his reputation and dignity; resources: consular rank, senatorial allies who intervened on his behalf.

## What Happened

Immediately on entering his praetorship, Caesar called a [[entities/forum-romanum|contio]] and summoned Catulus to explain the lack of progress on restoring the [[entities/temple-of-jupiter-capitolinus|Temple of Jupiter Capitolinus]] — a restoration project that had stalled under Catulus's oversight — and proposed transferring the responsibility to a more "competent" man: Pompey. It was probably on this occasion that Caesar further demanded Catulus speak not from the [[entities/rostra|rostra]] (the normal speaking platform, a mark of dignity) but from the ground, a deliberate act of public humiliation. Catulus's friends managed to ward off this final indignity, but Caesar had already made his point in front of the crowd.

## Outcome

Caesar achieved multiple goals at once: he avenged himself on Catulus for the Catilinarian slander (itself payback for the pontifical election loss), publicly embarrassed a senior statesman, reaffirmed his alliance with Pompey, and signaled his willingness to confront the established nobility. The broader political gambit — using his support for Pompey to push further controversial measures that year — nearly cost Caesar his praetorship when a related proposal triggered violence in the voting assembly, but he talked his way back into office.

## Open Questions

- The precise wording and staging of the "speak from the ground" demand rests on a single later allusion by Cicero rather than a full contemporary account.
