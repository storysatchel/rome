# The History of Rome (Books 1-5)

Theodor Mommsen (1817–1903) published *Römische Geschichte* in Germany between 1854 and 1856; this PDF is the 1862 English translation by Rev. William P. Dickson (from the German third edition of 1861), reissued in the Cambridge Library Collection, covering Mommsen's five structural "Books" — from the earliest inhabitants of Italy through the fall of the Republic and the establishment of Caesar's monarchy. Mommsen received the 1902 Nobel Prize in Literature for the work, the Nobel committee hailing him as "the greatest living master of the art of historical writing." The book was transcribed from a large (2,157-page) scanned edition; this copy's embedded text layer was extracted directly via PDF text extraction rather than the vault's default reader, since the file is a reprint scan.^[inferred]

## Core Argument / Thesis

Mommsen's organizing argument, per the Cambridge Library Collection's own framing (reproduced in this edition's front matter) and confirmed by the work's final chapter, is that Mommsen **rejected Enlightenment-era idealization of ancient Rome** and instead applied "rigorous criticism of sources" to demythologize Roman history, deliberately using modern political vocabulary to draw parallels between nineteenth-century and classical politics. The work's famous closing argument is that the Republic was destroyed "not through outward violence but through inward decay" — political, moral, religious, and literary — and that Caesar's monarchy, while unable to make an exhausted Roman world "young again," nonetheless represented a necessary and in some sense benevolent "tolerable evening after the sultry noon," carrying forward a "seed" that later nations inherited. This sympathetic reading of Caesar as history's instrument rather than the Republic's murderer is Mommsen's most historiographically distinctive and most contested claim.^[inferred]

## Key Claims and Evidence

- Mommsen frames Roman history as one of four parallel "culminating points" of Mediterranean civilization (Egyptian/Coptic, Aramaean/Syrian, Hellenic, and Italian/Roman), each developing independently before converging — an explicitly comparative-civilizational opening frame rather than a Rome-centered just-so narrative.
- Book III's analysis of the "Government and the Governed" in the mid-Republic (the period after the Punic Wars) argues that the formal stability of the Roman constitution in this era was deceptive: he writes that this period's constitutional stability "was here, as everywhere, not a sign of the health of the state, but a token of incipient sickness and the harbinger of revolution" — directly implicating the growing practice of appealing political and military disputes to the comitia (popular assemblies), which he judges structurally incapable of actually governing.
- Mommsen identifies Gaius Flaminius (consul 223, 217 BC) as a precursor whom "the statesmen of the following generation" blamed as the originator of the demagogic political style that led "to the reforms of the Gracchi, and... the democratico-monarchical revolution that followed" — explicitly drawing a direct causal line from mid-Republican populism to the Gracchi to Caesar's eventual monarchy.
- The practical abolition of the dictatorship after Quintus Fabius's political crisis in 217 BC (with only rare, limited exceptions, e.g. urban dictators) is presented as the loss of a constitutional "corrective" that the Senate could ill afford to lose, since collegiate (dual) magistracies needed exactly this kind of emergency check.
- Sulla's constitutional reforms (Book IV, "The Sullan Constitution") are analyzed in granular administrative detail: restoring minimum-age and office-sequence (cursus honorum) requirements, mandating a two-year gap and ten-year interval between magistracies, curbing the tribunate's legislative-initiative power by subjecting it to senatorial permission and barring ex-tribunes from higher office, and separating civil (consul/praetor, in Rome) from military (proconsul/propraetor, in the provinces) authority — with Italy itself (boundary moved from the Aesis to the Rubicon) reorganized as demilitarized "home" territory governed by ordinary magistrates rather than army commanders.
- Mommsen treats the extension of Roman citizens' direct interference in military command and foreign policy — citing examples from the election of anti-Hannibal commanders to Scipio's threat to appeal to the people if denied the African command — as symptomatic of the same disease as the land-distribution crisis: both represent the "sovereign" popular assembly intruding into domains (finance, military command) it was constitutionally and practically unfit to manage.

## Structure / Scope

The translated edition spans five "Books": Book I (earliest Italy to the abolition of the monarchy), Book II (abolition of monarchy to the union of Italy, including chapters on struggles against Pyrrhus and the Italian peoples, plus thematic chapters on religion, law, military system, and art/science), Book III (union of Italy to the destruction of Carthage and subjugation of the Greek states, including the extended "Government and the Governed" analysis of constitutional decay), Book IV ("The Revolution," covering the Gracchi through Sulla's dictatorship and constitutional reforms), and Book V (the fall of the Republic through Caesar's dictatorship, ending with Caesar's assassination-era monarchy and a chapter on late-Republican religion, culture, literature, and art). Mommsen draws on the full range of ancient sources (Sallust, Livy, Polybius, Cicero, Suetonius, Dio, inscriptions) with dense footnoting and, distinctively for its era, extensive comparative-linguistic and legal argument (e.g. on Roman *religio* versus Greek mythology in Book I).

## Significance

Mommsen's work is explicitly positioned by its own translator's preface as both building on and superseding Barthold Georg Niebuhr's earlier *History of Rome*: the preface states that while "much that could not but be obscure... in the days of Niebuhr has since been made clear," Niebuhr's core insights (on the origin of the plebs, patrician-plebeian relations, and the *ager publicus*) remain "unshaken" pillars that Mommsen's own more rigorous source-criticism builds upon rather than discards. Relative to the other books in this batch, Mommsen's interpretation of the late Republic's fall is far more structurally and institutionally argued than Watts's or Campbell's narrative-driven accounts, and explicitly more sympathetic to Caesar as a historical outcome than Watts's framing of autocracy as liberty's negation. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- Mommsen's famously favorable reading of Caesar — "the richly-gifted patriotism of Caesar" and the characterization of monarchy as a "tolerable evening" — sits in direct tension with Watts's thesis that the shift from republic to autocracy represented a tragic, cautionary loss of self-governance; this batch's instruction to flag cross-book interpretive disagreement applies most sharply here: Mommsen (writing 1854–56) and Watts (writing 2018) draw opposite normative conclusions from substantially the same narrative arc (Gracchi → civil wars → Caesar/Augustus).^[ambiguous]
- Because the source PDF is a 2,157-page scan with no indexed table-of-contents page captured in this sample, exact page boundaries between Mommsen's five Books were inferred from chapter-heading text encountered during sampling (e.g. "Book III," "Book IV," "Book V" headers) rather than from a verified contents listing.^[ambiguous]
