# Caesar's Axle Breaks at the Velabrum During His Gallic Triumph

During his quadruple triumph of 46 BC — celebrating victories in Gaul, Egypt, Pontus, and Africa — Julius Caesar nearly suffered a public mishap when the axle of his triumphal chariot (*currus*) snapped as he passed through the Velabrum district of Rome, almost throwing him from the vehicle (Suetonius, *Divus Iulius* 37.2: *Gallici triumphi die Velabrum praeteruehens paene curru excessus est axe diffracto*). Cassius Dio records the breakdown as occurring specifically at the temple of Felicitas.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Julius Caesar** ([[entities/julius-caesar]]) — goal: project an image of unassailable divine favor and military glory during his unprecedented four-fold triumph; resources: the authority of a dictator celebrating the climax of his career.
- **The Roman public / religious tradition** — stakes: triumphal processions were read as omens; a breakdown mid-procession risked being interpreted as a sign of ill fortune for the triumphator.

## What Happened

As Caesar's chariot carried him through the Velabrum — a low, marshy district near the Forum Boarium on the triumphal route — its axle broke, very nearly pitching him out onto the pavement in front of the assembled crowds. Suetonius records the event tersely as part of a catalogue of portents and embarrassments associated with Caesar's later career. Cassius Dio, writing independently, places the breakdown at the temple of Felicitas — an irony not lost on ancient or modern readers, given the temple's name ("Good Fortune"). Pliny the Elder separately attests to Roman superstition surrounding vehicle crashes, noting that Caesar and others took care about riding in vehicles again after such an accident.^[inferred]

## Outcome

Caesar avoided serious injury or obvious public humiliation, but the incident entered the biographical tradition as one of several unsettling omens clustering around the end of his life (Suetonius presents it within a sequence of portents). The precise symbolic reading of the event is not given uniformly by the sources, and whether contemporaries treated it as a bad omen in 46 BC itself, or whether this is retrospective shaping by Suetonius/Dio in light of Caesar's assassination two years later, remains unclear.^[ambiguous]

## Open Questions

- Did Caesar's contemporaries in 46 BC treat the chariot accident as a bad omen at the time, or is the "omen" framing a retrospective construction by later biographers?
- Why do Suetonius and Dio disagree on the precise location (Velabrum generally vs. specifically the temple of Felicitas)?
