# Opimia (Vestal Virgin, d. 216 BC)

Opimia was one of two Vestal Virgins convicted of *incestum* in 216 BC, in the weeks after the Roman defeat at [[entities/cannae|Cannae]]. Her colleague was [[entities/floronia-vestal-216-bc|Floronia]]. Theirs is the last Vestal case recorded between the founding of the Republic and the end of the Second Punic War, and the most discussed.

## Conviction and death

The ancient account is a single sentence of Livy (22.57.2–3). It lists the case among the terrors that followed the slaughter at Cannae and other prodigies. In the same year two Vestals, Opimia and Floronia, were convicted of *incestum*. One was put to death under the earth at the [[entities/colline-gate|Colline Gate]] "as is the custom", and the other took her own life. The same sentence names Floronia as the partner of [[entities/lucius-cantilius-pontifical-scribe|L. Cantilius]], a pontifical scribe. The Pontifex Maximus had Cantilius flogged in the Comitium until he died.

Livy does not say which Vestal was buried and which killed herself, and the modern treatments in the corpus keep that ambiguity. Opimia is therefore either the Vestal executed by the customary penalty or the one who died by her own hand first. The customary penalty was burial alive in an underground chamber outside the Colline Gate, in the [[entities/campus-sceleratus|campus sceleratus]].

## The crisis of 216 BC

The convictions came while Hannibal's army was in Italy and few magistrates remained in Rome. The city was full of public lamentation: the facts were not yet known, so households mourned the living and the dead alike. On the advice of Q. Fabius Maximus, the senators ordered matrons off the streets and silenced mourning. Mourning was later limited to one month. The annual rites of Ceres were suspended, because too few women were out of mourning to perform them.

The case was handled as a religious failure that needed expiation. The senate had the *decemviri* consult the Sibylline Books and sent Q. Fabius Pictor to ask the oracle at Delphi how the gods could be appeased. It also decreed a rite Livy calls hardly Roman: a Gallic man and woman and a Greek man and woman were buried alive in the [[entities/forum-boarium|Forum Boarium]]. Livy reports that the *decemviri* believed the expiation had worked, and he treats them as mistaken, since the course of the war did not change.

## Interpretations

The case is the usual example cited for the view that Vestals were accused only in times of fear and upheaval, and for 216 BC that description fits. Two explanations are offered for the convictions:

- **A sign of broken *pax deorum*.** The Vestals' unchastity counted as one of the warnings the senate found it had missed. The Romans looked to their own conduct for the cause of the disaster.
- **A correction aimed at Roman women.** The charges answered the breakdown of women's expected domestic restraint, seen in their public mourning and later their presence in the debate over ransoming the Cannae captives. The punishment of two Vestals would have served as a symbolic correction of that behaviour.

Neither explanation rests on evidence about Opimia herself. Livy records the conviction but no specific accusation.

## Name

The name Opimia is also one of the recorded variants (with Oppia, Pompilia and Popillia) for the Vestal executed in 483 BC. That is a different woman; see [[entities/oppia-vestal-483-bc]].

There is one ancient witness, Livy 22.57.2–6 with 22.55–56.
