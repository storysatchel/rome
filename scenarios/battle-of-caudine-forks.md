# The Roman Disaster at the Caudine Forks

During the Second Samnite War, a Roman consular army was lured into a narrow defile known as the [[entities/caudine-forks|Caudine Forks]] (Furculae Caudinae) near [[entities/caudium|Caudium]] in [[entities/samnium|Samnium]], where the Samnite commander Gaius Pontius had blocked both exits. Trapped without supplies and unable to fight their way out, the Roman consuls were forced to accept humiliating terms: the army was disarmed and made to pass "under the yoke" (*sub iugum*), a ritual of submission, before being released.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Gaius Pontius** ([[entities/gaius-pontius]]) — Samnite commander; goal: destroy or permanently humiliate the trapped Roman army to force a lasting peace favorable to the Samnites; resources: control of the terrain, a Samnite army positioned at both ends of the pass.
- **The Roman consuls (Spurius Postumius and Titus Veturius)** ([[entities/spurius-postumius-albinus-caudinus]]) — goal: extricate the trapped legions with minimal loss; resources: a full consular army, now cut off and starving.

## What Happened

The Roman army, marching through Samnite territory, entered the Caudine Forks — a valley accessible only through two narrow, easily blocked passes — and found both ends sealed by Samnite forces under Gaius Pontius. Surrounded and without hope of relief or supply, the consuls negotiated a capitulation. Rather than annihilate the Roman force outright, the Samnites imposed a treaty and a ritual humiliation: the Roman soldiers were stripped of their arms and marched, one by one, under a yoke formed of spears — a traditional sign of subjugation — before being allowed to withdraw, along with hostages to guarantee Roman compliance with the peace terms.

## Outcome

The treaty was later repudiated by the Roman Senate, which declared it invalid on the grounds that the consuls had no authority to bind the state without Senate ratification, and the consuls were reportedly surrendered back to the Samnites in a formal act of absolution (*deditio*) — though the Samnites refused to accept them. The war resumed and continued, as part of the broader Second and Third Samnite Wars, until Roman victory at Sentinum in 295 BC finally broke Samnite power.^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- The precise ritual details, including the yoke ceremony, may reflect later Roman literary elaboration of the humiliation; their historicity remains disputed.
