# The Vestal Virgins Scandal of 114–113 BC

Amid a decade dominated by a narrow clique of senatorial families, Rome was shaken by allegations that three [[entities/vestal-virgins|Vestal Virgins]] had broken their vows of chastity. The priestesses were initially tried before a panel of Roman priests — who were themselves all senators — and the outcomes split sharply along the social class of the men the Vestals were accused of consorting with.

## Parties & Stakes

- **An unnamed Vestal accused of a liaison with a Roman equite** — outcome: convicted in 114 BC and buried alive, the traditional punishment for an unchaste Vestal
- **Two unnamed Vestals accused of relations with senatorial men** — initially acquitted by the senatorial panel of priests
- **A tribune of 113 BC (unnamed in Watts's account)** — goal: correct the perceived double standard; proposed a law creating a special, independent court to retry the case
- **The Roman public** — stakes: trust that the courts would not simply protect senators' families from consequences

## What Happened

The first Vestal, linked to a man of the equestrian order, was convicted and suffered the traditional live burial. The two others, accused of affairs with senatorial men, were acquitted by a panel of priests drawn entirely from the Senate's own ranks — a verdict that provoked public outcry over the apparent double standard favoring senators. In response, an incoming tribune proposed and passed a law establishing a special independent commission to reinvestigate the case, free from the priests' potential class bias.

## Outcome

The new commission retried the matter, and all three Vestals were ultimately put to death by 113 BC. The affair fed into a broader climate of anxiety in Rome during these years: a run of military defeats and ominous religious omens convinced many Romans that elite impiety and arrogance had angered the gods, a mood that led directly to further ritual purifications, including the human sacrifice of Greek and Gallic prisoners, and the first-ever criminal conviction of a member of a Roman priestly college for misconduct in 109 BC.

## Open Questions

- Neither Watts nor Mommsen names the three Vestals or their alleged partners in detail; Mommsen's brief reference (via the "Peducaean law of 641 [113 BC]") corroborates the special court's creation but adds no further names.
