# The Archery Contest and Acestes's Omen

**This is a legendary/literary episode**, the fourth contest of Anchises's funeral games in Book V of the *Aeneid*. Aeneas raises a ship's mast as a shooting target and ties a live dove to its top by a cord.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Hippocoon** ([[entities/hippocoon]]) — son of Hyrtacus; shoots first
- **Mnestheus** ([[entities/mnestheus]]) — fresh from his second-place finish in the boat race; shoots second
- **Eurytion** ([[entities/eurytion]]) — brother of the Trojan-War archer Pandarus; shoots third
- **Acestes** ([[entities/acestes]]) — Sicilian host, shoots last after the target dove is already dead
- **Aeneas** ([[entities/aeneas]]) — judge; interprets a mid-air omen to decide the final prize order

## What Happened

Lots drawn from a bronze helmet set the shooting order. Hippocoon's arrow is first to fly and strikes fast in the mast's timber — a near miss that sets the bird fluttering but spares it. Mnestheus, aiming high, misses the dove itself but cuts the cord tethering it to the mast; freed, the bird flies off into the sky. Eurytion, already aiming at the now-loose dove and silently invoking his dead archer brother Pandarus, puts his arrow clean through it, killing it in flight and bringing it down with the shaft still fixed in its body — the contest's only actual hit on a target.

Only Acestes remains, with no dove left to shoot at. He draws anyway and looses his arrow straight into the empty sky, "showing them all his old-time archer's power." In front of the astonished crowd, the arrow catches fire mid-flight, trailing flame across the sky like a shooting star before burning out — an event the poem explicitly frames as a true omen, foretelling "mighty events" that "poets far in the future fabled... in awe."

## Outcome

Aeneas interprets the fiery omen as marking Acestes out by Jupiter himself for special distinction, and crowns him overall winner, gifting him an engraved heirloom mixing-bowl that had belonged to Anchises. Eurytion — who actually killed the dove — graciously accepts second place without complaint ("preferment never grudged by that good fellow"). Mnestheus, who cut the cord, takes third; Hippocoon, who only struck the mast, finishes last among the four.

## Open Questions

- Acestes wins the top prize without ever striking the stated target, a result the poem itself treats as uncontested despite its obvious oddity by ordinary contest rules — whether the games' onlookers saw this as a fair outcome or simply deferred to a religious omen is left unaddressed. ^[ambiguous]
