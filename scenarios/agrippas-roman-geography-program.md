# Agrippa's Roman Geography Program

Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa, Augustus’ lieutenant and intended heir, compiled the reports behind a monumental representation of the world erected in Rome’s Porticus Vipsania in the last decade of the first century BC, after his death. It is conventionally called the “Map of Agrippa,” but neither its exact appearance nor its function is securely known. The surviving discussion suggests a visual component, often understood as a world map, together with some form of written commentary.

## Geography in Augustan Rome

The display belonged to a period of intensified census-taking, land surveys, and representations of the world in the imperial capital. Augustus’ political and physical remaking of Rome gave geographical knowledge a setting in which descriptions of the world could also express claims to universal power.

## A disputed legacy

Comparisons with Pliny’s *Natural History* show why the display matters to interpretations of Roman knowledge. Pliny’s geography may be read as an attempt to emulate or surpass Agrippa’s project, but that interpretation does not establish a single, definitive imperial picture: Pliny assembled competing accounts and accumulated fragments, deriving authority from the scale and diligence of his compilation. Agrippa’s lost display therefore provides a point of comparison, not proof that Roman geographical writing followed one uniform programme.

## Completing the display

The display was erected in the last decade of the first century BC in the southern Campus Martius. Pliny credits Augustus with completing the portico begun by Agrippa’s sister, following Agrippa’s design and *commentarii*. Agrippa’s reports thus became a public monument through work continued after his death, rather than a map whose finished form can simply be attributed to his own execution.

Pliny names Agrippa about thirty times in the geographical books of the *Natural History*, using his work for particular information. Whether Pliny consulted the display itself, an accompanying commentary, or another textual transmission is disputed. Later descriptions of the Porticus Vipsania do not mention the survey, a useful warning against assuming that its prominence in modern accounts reflects its visibility to every ancient visitor.
