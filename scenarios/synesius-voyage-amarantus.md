# Synesius's Near-Disastrous Voyage Under the Debt-Ridden Captain Amarantus

In AD 404 the philosopher and future bishop Synesius of Cyrene took passage on a small coastal vessel from Alexandria toward Cyrene, along the Egyptian and Libyan coast. He later described the harrowing trip in a letter to his brother. The ship's owner was "being crushed to death by a load of debt," and the economic desperation behind the voyage's cut corners — an inexperienced crew of farm boys, a missing spare anchor, no stormsail — nearly cost the fifty-plus passengers their lives in a storm.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Synesius of Cyrene** ([[entities/synesius-of-cyrene]]) — a passenger; stakes: his life and those of his fellow travelers, caught at the mercy of an underfunded ship's operation.
- **Captain Amarantus** ([[entities/amarantus]]) — the ship's captain, one of several Jewish crew members aboard; goal: complete the voyage despite being deeply indebted, reportedly hoping privately that his own death at sea would relieve him of his creditors; resources: an undermanned, under-equipped vessel.
- **The ship's owner** (unnamed) — described as "crushed to death by a load of debt," whose financial distress is presented as the root cause of the vessel's inadequate safety equipment and crew.

## What Happened

Synesius's ship carried a crew of twelve plus the captain, over half of them Jewish, and the rest inexperienced farm boys who had never rowed before that year — each crew member nicknamed for a bodily defect (Cripple, Ruptured, One-Arm, Squint). More than fifty passengers, roughly a third of them young women separated from the men only by a torn sailcloth curtain, were aboard. Early in the voyage, Captain Amarantus steered dangerously close to rocks near a cape, attempting a risky shortcut, and had to be shouted down by alarmed passengers before changing course. That night a storm hit; the sail could not be shortened because the lines had jammed, and the crew — in a moment of black humor Synesius found almost comic even in terror — was observed with swords drawn, preferring to die by their own hand on deck rather than drown. Passengers hung gold and money around their necks to pay for their own funeral rites should their bodies wash ashore. Synesius noted that Amarantus alone seemed cheerful amid the panic, apparently calculating that his own death would cancel his debts to his creditors. The ship survived until dawn, when the wind eased and the crew — once the ropes dried out enough to handle — brought the vessel to a remote, unpopulated stretch of coast. It had only a single anchor remaining, because Amarantus had already sold the second one, and owned no third; there was no stormsail aboard, as it, too, was in a pawnshop.

## Outcome

The passengers survived and disembarked onto open, unprotected coastline far from any town, "embracing the land like a living mother." Synesius's letter stands as a vivid firsthand account of how a shipowner's and captain's personal indebtedness directly translated into inadequate safety margins for dozens of paying passengers on an ordinary coastal run — an economic negligence story rather than a grand disaster.

## Open Questions

- The letter is a literary composition by Synesius, intended partly to entertain his brother; some exaggeration for effect cannot be ruled out.
