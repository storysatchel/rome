# Roman Republican Augury: Freedom and Control

Lindsay G. Driediger-Murphy (University of Calgary) published this monograph with Oxford University Press in 2019, in the Oxford Classical Monographs series. It is a revised and expanded version of her Oxford DPhil thesis (2007–11, supervised in part by Simon Price, to whose memory the book is dedicated). The book interrogates [[concepts/roman-religion-and-augury]] — the Roman system of divination through birds, sky-signs, and other prodigies used to validate or block public action — and argues against the dominant modern scholarly consensus that Romans treated augury as a tool for human elites to control or "bind" the god Jupiter to their own purposes.

## Core Argument / Thesis

Driediger-Murphy's central claim is that modern scholarship has inverted the Roman understanding of augury's power dynamics. Where the "current consensus view" (associated above all with Jerzy Linderski, but also John Scheid and Jörg Rüpke) holds that Roman magistrates and augurs "created" divine signs and used religion instrumentally to manage political outcomes and reassure anxious populations (a broadly functionalist reading), she argues the Romans themselves believed augury expressed and responded to Jupiter's genuine, independent will — a will that could clash with human desires and that humans were expected to obey even against their own interests. She frames the book around two questions: is the functionalist/control reading historically accurate, and if not, what does the alternative tell us about Roman religious psychology more broadly? Her answer: Roman augury is better evidence for Rome as a "deeply, consistently, passionately religious society" than for Roman legalistic cunning.

## Key Claims and Evidence

- Public augury took two forms: permanent-effect signs that "inaugurated" people or places (*auguria*), and action-specific signs pertaining to a proposed act at a specific moment (*auspicia*), which could arrive spontaneously (*auspicia oblativa*) or be solicited by ritual (*auspicia impetrativa*).
- Jupiter was consulted via augurs/magistrates before nearly every major act of public life: creating priests, electing magistrates, holding assemblies, passing legislation, crossing the *pomerium*, crossing rivers with an army, or joining battle.
- Cicero's *De Legibus* 2.31 is the key ancient text cited for the augurs' formal powers: the right to adjourn or nullify assemblies, invalidate laws, and compel a consul's abdication — powers Cicero (himself an augur from 53/52 BC) openly celebrates.
- The author catalogs the functionalist consensus in detail (Linderski, Scheid, Rüpke, and a long list of others cited in the introduction's footnotes) before building her counter-case chapter by chapter, examining: (Ch. 1) whether augural "report" created rather than merely conveyed signs; (Ch. 2) whether *servare de caelo* ("sky-watching") reliably and technically sufficed to block assemblies, using the Bibulus affair of 59 BC as an extended case study; (Ch. 3) the real political effects and motives behind augury's use in public life, including the *augurium salutis* and the behavior of Cicero, Bibulus, and other named actors.
- She distinguishes this project from, but engages with, constitutional/structural approaches to the auspices (Vervaet, Drogula, Berthelet) that focus on magistrates' formal powers rather than theological content.
- Draws on anthropological critiques of functionalist divination theory (Eidinow on Dodona, Kearns on Greek ritual) to argue that ancient participants experienced divination as "a crucial interdependence with the divine," not merely a social-consensus mechanism. ^[inferred]

## Structure / Scope

Covers roughly the Middle and Late Republic through the early Principate, concentrating heavily on the first century BC (Cicero, Bibulus, Caesar, Milo, Antony). Draws principally on Cicero's philosophical/rhetorical corpus (especially *De Divinatione* and *De Legibus*), supplemented by Livy, Varro, Festus, Valerius Maximus, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Macrobius, and the technical augural commentary tradition in Servius. The book is organized into an Introduction (four "guiding principles" framing the inquiry), three main chapters (on report vs. reality, sky-watching, and augury's real-world effects), and a Conclusion, followed by an extensive bibliography and indexes (general index spans "Etruria" through "Zeus," reflecting the work's comparative range into Greek, Chinese, and African divination systems for anthropological contrast).

## Significance

The book positions itself explicitly against a "current consensus" it treats as near-universal among modern scholars of Roman religion (naming Linderski as "the most distinguished modern scholar of augury"), arguing that this consensus — despite differences among its proponents — shares an unexamined assumption that divine agency in augury was fictive or instrumentalized. Driediger-Murphy argues this reverses the actual Ciceronian testimony, in which the human augur's power derives from genuinely representing Jupiter's will, not substituting for it. She situates her revisionist argument within a broader shift in anthropology away from functionalist theories of divination.^[inferred]
