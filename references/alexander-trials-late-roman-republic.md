# Trials in the Late Roman Republic, 149 BC to 50 BC

Michael C. Alexander (Department of Classics, University of Illinois at Chicago) published this reference work in 1990 as Phoenix Supplementary Volume 26 (University of Toronto Press). Its purpose is not narrative or interpretive but tabulatory: it catalogs, "as exhaustively as possible," the 391 known or possible criminal and civil [[concepts/roman-law-and-trials]] from the last century of the Republic, filling the absence of actual Roman court records with a standardized reference format explicitly modeled on Broughton's *Magistrates of the Roman Republic* (*MRR*).

## Core Argument / Thesis

The book makes no interpretive argument of its own; its purpose is to "convey the sort of information which we might expect to find in court records" so that other scholars of Roman political history, legal history, and rhetoric can use a compressed, standardized dataset rather than resorting to scattered primary citations each time. Alexander is explicit that the work "is not in reality such a documentary source, and should not be treated as one" — it reconstructs from literary testimony (Cicero's speeches above all, plus Livy, Appian, Valerius Maximus, and others), not from archival records.

## Key Claims and Evidence

- The catalog spans 149 BC (the *lex Calpurnia*, establishing the first permanent *quaestio perpetua*) to 50 BC (the eve of Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon and the collapse of normal Republican institutional functioning).
- For each trial, Alexander records up to eleven categories of data where available: date; charge/claim (procedural + substantive); defendant; advocate(s); prosecutor(s)/plaintiff(s); presiding magistrate; jurors; witnesses; parties (for civil suits of unclear role); miscellaneous participants; and verdict.
- Cases #1–351 are dated chronologically; cases #352–391 are of indefinite date, reflecting the fragmentary nature of the evidence.
- Alexander restricts scope to trials that took place in the city of Rome itself, explicitly excluding provincial, military, and most religious jurisdiction trials — with a deliberate exception for the three trials of [[entities/vestal-virgins]] *apud pontifices* (cases #38–40, the 114 BC incestum prosecutions of Aemilia and Licinia), retained as background for four related subsequent cases.
- Extortion (*repetundae*) trials are frequently dated to the year after a defendant's provincial command ended — a reasonable but not certain inference flagged explicitly as such in the introduction.
- Sample entries (e.g., cases #14–37, covering 138–115 BC) show the catalog's density: each records defendant identity via *Realencyclopädie* numbering, the specific or generic statute invoked, named advocates/prosecutors with their consular dates, verdict, and footnoted scholarly controversies (e.g., disputes over whether C. Papirius Carbo's case in 119 BC fell under the *lex Acilia de repetundis* or the *lex Sempronia ne quis iudicio circumveniatur*).
- Trials before the *comitia* (*iudicia populi*) and the religious *quaestio apud pontifices* for Vestals are distinguished methodologically from standing-court (*quaestio perpetua*) trials throughout.

## Structure / Scope

The book covers Rome's judicial system city-wide across the "last century" of the Republic (149–50 BC), organized as: Introduction (methodology and Latin/English terminology equivalences), the 391 trial entries in rough chronological order, a Works Cited list, and nine separate indexes (procedures, names, defendants, advocates, prosecutors/plaintiffs, magistrates, jurors, witnesses, parties). Alexander draws overwhelmingly on Cicero's speeches and rhetorical works (*Brutus*, *de Oratore*, *Pro Murena*, etc.), supplemented by Livy's *Periochae*, Appian, Plutarch, Valerius Maximus, Velleius Paterculus, and the scholiasts (Asconius, Scholia Bobiensia), cross-checked against modern prosopographical scholarship (Broughton's *MRR*, Gruen's *Roman Politics and the Criminal Courts*, Badian's studies).

## Significance

Alexander positions the work explicitly as a companion to Broughton's *MRR*, aiming to do for the judicial sphere what Broughton did for magistracies — compress scattered testimonia into a standardized, cross-referenced format usable by other scholars without requiring them to reassemble primary citations from scratch.^[inferred] Its continuing value lies less in novel argument than in its role as a controlled dataset: any study of Late Republican political prosecution, oratory, or the operation of the *quaestiones* (including trials touching religious offices, cf. [[concepts/roman-religion-and-augury]]) can use it to establish baseline facts about participants and outcomes before building interpretive claims.^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- Alexander repeatedly flags cases where the charge's governing statute is unknown or disputed (e.g., case #30, #31), leaving the precise legal basis for many Late Republican prosecutions genuinely uncertain in the primary record.
- The dating of many trials rests on inference from magistrates' known tenures rather than direct evidence, a methodological limitation the author states openly rather than resolves.
- The original reading sampled the introduction and roughly fifty consecutive early entries (cases #1–~45, through the 110s BC); a subsequent re-scour pass (see Related Scenarios above) extended coverage through case #217 (64 BC), though the bulk of the Ciceronian-era prosecutions from the mid-60s through 50 BC (including Verres, Clodius, Milo, and Catiline's co-conspirators) remain unsampled. ^[ambiguous]
