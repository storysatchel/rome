# Marcus Porcius Cato (Cato the Younger)

Cato the Younger is one of van der Blom's six non-Ciceronian case studies, where his career is read through the lens of deliberate self-presentation rather than technical oratorical skill — and he resurfaces in the companion volume as a politician capable of pragmatic alliance despite his reputation for rigid principle.

## Biography / Role

- Van der Blom's case-study chapter on Cato is built around "powerful profiling" via self-presentation, distinguishing his approach from Gracchus's tribunician oratory, Pompey's "behind the scenes" politics, and Caesar's use of oratory as a career springboard.
- Van der Blom classes Cato, alongside Gracchus and Caesar, among politicians who actively sought out oratorical occasions and were recognized as "good orators" by contemporaries and posterity — in contrast to the "reactive" speakers Pompey, Piso Caesoninus, and Mark Antony.
- In *Institutions and Ideology in Republican Rome*, Morrell's chapter (summarized by van der Blom, Gray, and Steel) reinterprets Cato's and [[entities/pompey]]'s uneasy cooperation in 52 BC, after Clodius's murder, as a shared commitment to civic order overriding their mutual suspicion — evidence that Cato's famously principled stances could still accommodate pragmatic alignment with a political rival when public order was at stake.
- Van der Blom's closing argument notes that a revised, broader inclusion criterion for Cicero's *Brutus* (shifting from oratorical ability to the capacity to project a coherent public narrative) would newly admit figures like Piso, Antony, Sulla, Catiline, and Clodius — but Cato, like Gracchus, Pompey, and Caesar, was already among the orators Cicero's narrower, stricter criterion could not ignore.
