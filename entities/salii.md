# Salii

The *Salii* (The Leaping Priests, derived from the Latin verb *salire*, "to leap" or "to dance") were an archaic patrician sodality dedicated to Mars, the Roman god of war and agricultural vigilance. Organized into two distinct colleges of twelve priests each—the *Salii Palatini* and the *Salii Collini*—the Salii served as ritual guardians of the sacred bronze shields (*ancilia*), one of which had allegedly fallen from heaven during the reign of King Numa Pompilius as a divine pledge of Roman imperial dominion (*pignus imperii*). At the opening (March) and closing (October) of the traditional military campaigning season, the Salii processed through the streets of Rome in archaic bronze armor, performing rhythmic three-step dances and chanting the venerable *Carmen Saliare*.

## Origins and Dual Organization

Roman foundation tradition (Livy 1.20; Dionysius of Halicarnassus 2.70; Plutarch, *Numa* 13) attributed the creation of the Salii to King Numa Pompilius. When a deadly plague struck Rome, a bronze shield with curved indents on either side (a figure-eight shield) fell from heaven onto the Palatine. Egeria, Numa’s divine consort, revealed that this shield (*ancile*) was a guarantee of Rome's eternal power. To prevent theft or desecration, Numa commissioned the artisan Mamurius Veturius to forge eleven identical replicas. Numa then established a college of twelve patrician youths to guard the shields.

The priests were divided into two sodalities, reflecting the archaic dual settlement of Rome:
1. **`Salii Palatini`:** Dedicated to *Mars Gradivus* ("Mars the Strider"), based at the *Curia Saliorum* on the Palatine Hill. This was the senior and more prestigious of the two groups.
2. **`Salii Collini` (or *Salii Agonales*):** Dedicated to *Quirinus*, based on the Quirinal Hill (*Collis Quirinalis*). Tradition attributed their foundation to King Tullus Hostilius following a vow during the war against the Sabines of Fidenae.

Membership was strictly limited to patrician youths whose parents were both living at the time of co-optation (*patrimi et matrimi*). Because service in the Salii required strenuous athletic dancing, members usually resigned or vacated their positions upon being elected to higher public magistracies or major priesthoods (for example, Scipio Africanus and the future emperor Marcus Aurelius served as Salii in their youth).

## Vestments, Armor, and the *Ancilia*

The ceremonial attire of the Salii preserved the military equipment of archaic Bronze Age Italy:
- **`Trabea`:** An embroidered tunic over which was draped an archaic, short military mantle of purple wool bordered with scarlet stripes.
- **Bronze Armor:** A heavy bronze breastplate (*aeneum pectorale*) worn over the chest.
- **`Apex`:** A conical, pointed cap of olive wood covered in sheepskin, fastened beneath the chin with leather thongs.
- **Weapons:** In their right hand, each priest carried a short bronze thrusting spear or dagger; on their left arm, they bore one of the twelve sacred *ancilia*.

The *ancile* was an oval shield of beaten bronze deeply indented on both sides, resembling a Mycenaean or archaic Italian figure-eight shield. Throughout the year, the twenty-four shields were housed within the *Curia Saliorum* on the Palatine and the sanctuary on the Quirinal. During the months of festival activity, they were carried on wooden poles by the priests through the city.

## Festival Cycles: March and October

The ceremonial activity of the Salii marked the annual cycle of Roman warfare, framing the period between spring planting and autumn harvest when citizen-soldiers mobilized for military campaigns:

### The Spring Cycle (March: *Ancilia Mota*)
On March 1 (the Kalends of March, originally the archaic Roman New Year and the birth-month of Mars), the Salii initiated the ceremony known as *ancilia movere* ("moving the shields"):
- For three weeks, the Salii carried the shields in daily processions visiting the major sacred centers of Rome: the Forum, the Capitoline, the Aventine, and the Comitium.
- At designated public squares and crossroads shrines, they halted to perform their sacred ritual dance (*tripudium*). Striking their bronze shields with their daggers in synchronized cadences, they executed intricate, athletic leaping steps.
- The festival concluded on March 19 with the *Quinquatrus* and on March 23 with the *Tubilustrium* (the ritual purification of the sacred bronze war-trumpets on the Volcanal), after which the shields were deposited back into their sanctuaries (*ancilia condere*).

### The Autumn Cycle (October: *Ancilia Condita*)
In October, at the conclusion of the summer campaigning season, the Salii once again brought out the shields for the *Armilustrium* (October 19):
- The citizen army assembled on the Aventine Hill for ritual purification.
- The Salii danced and offered sacrifices to purify the army's weapons, shields, and standards from the bloodguilt of battle before storing the weapons for winter and locking the *ancilia* away in their vaults.

### The Salian Banquets (*Cenae Saliares*)
At the end of each festival day, the Salii retired to a designated public guest house (*mansio Saliorum*) or an elite residence to dine. The *cenae Saliares* (Salian banquets) were renowned throughout antiquity for their unmatched extravagance, luxury, and rare wines. Both Horace (*Odes* 1.37.2–4) and Suetonius used the phrase "a Salian feast" as an ancient proverb for the ultimate in culinary excess, noting that the sodality spared no expense from their wealthy corporate endowments.

## The *Carmen Saliare* and Imperial Inclusion

During their processions, the Salii chanted the *Carmen Saliare*, an archaic litany composed in Saturnian verse. 

By the late Republic, the Latin of the *Carmen Saliare* had become so archaic that even learned philologists like Marcus Terentius Varro and Cicero admitted that the priests themselves could no longer understand the literal meaning of their own hymns (*De Lingua Latina* 7.1–3; Horace, *Epistles* 2.1.86). The hymn invoked the archaic pantheon of Rome—Janus (*Duonus Cerus*), Jupiter (*Lucetius*), Mars, Quirinus, and Minerva—and praised the mythical craftsman Mamurius Veturius.

### The Augustan Innovation
Under the Principate, the *Carmen Saliare* was transformed into a constitutional and dynastic instrument:
- In 29 BCE, following the Battle of Actium, the Senate decreed that the name of Augustus should be permanently inserted into the *Carmen Saliare* alongside the immortal gods, an unprecedented sacral honor conferring semi-divine status upon the living princeps.
- This exceptional honor was later extended by senatorial decree to deceased imperial princes and designated heirs: Germanicus (19 CE), Drusus the Younger (23 CE), and the young Lucius Verus. 

The emperor Marcus Aurelius, who was co-opted into the Salii at age eight by Hadrian, was renowned for his deep piety; he memorized all the ancient Salian verses and advanced through all the sodality's internal administrative ranks (*praesul*, *vates*, and *magister*) before his imperial elevation.
