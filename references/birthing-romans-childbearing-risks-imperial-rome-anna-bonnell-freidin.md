# Birthing Romans

> [!tldr] The EBSCO source contains a review of Anna Bonnell Freidin's study of childbirth, medical risk, and reproductive expectations in the Roman Empire.

## Scope

The source is a three-page clipped review from the *American Historical Review*, June 2025. It identifies Anna Bonnell Freidin's *Birthing Romans: Childbearing and Its Risks in Imperial Rome* (Princeton University Press, 2024) and reviews its account of childbirth from Augustus through roughly the first three centuries CE.

The review describes a study of the Latin West and North Africa, with attention to the Greek East, and presents childbearing as a social, medical, and ideological practice within the Roman Empire. It is a review of Freidin's book, not the book itself; claims below are therefore limited to what the review reports. ^[ambiguous]

## Themes reported by the review

- Roman marriage and childbearing carried social duties and expectations for families and communities.
- Freidin uses legal, poetic, medical, papyrological, and iconographic evidence to reconstruct reproductive risk and experience.
- Soranus's early second-century *Gynecology* is a major medical source.
- Amulets, material practices, fate, and fortune show that medical explanations coexisted with religious and social responses to risk.
- Evidence from second-century Aquincum in Pannonia extends the discussion of childbearing beyond Rome itself.

The topic complements the vault's broader Roman social history and can be read alongside [[entities/rome]] and questions of family continuity in [[concepts/roman-elite-adoption-practices]].
