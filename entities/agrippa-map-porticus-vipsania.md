# Agrippa map / Porticus Vipsania

The “map of Agrippa” is the conventional modern name for a monumental representation of the world erected in the Porticus Vipsania during the last decade of the first century BCE. The portico stood in the southern Campus Martius at Rome.

## An Augustan geographical monument

The display drew on reports compiled by Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa, Augustus’ lieutenant and prospective heir. It formed part of the monumental environment of the Augustan city, presenting geographical knowledge in a public setting.

The work is lost. Knowledge of it depends primarily on a small number of fragmentary references in Pliny the Elder’s *Natural History*. The modern label “map” should not be mistaken for a surviving object whose precise visual form can be inspected.

## Display, reports, and geographical authority

Pliny credits Augustus with completing the portico following Agrippa’s design and *commentarii*. He invokes Agrippa and Augustus as authorities when discussing the displayed world. Agrippa is named roughly thirty times in the geographical books of the *Natural History*, but it remains uncertain whether particular measurements came from the public display, an accompanying text, or later copies.

The monument belonged to a wider culture of visual geographical representation. Republican examples included a panel showing Sardinia and battles, dedicated by Sempronius Gracchus, and a plan of Carthage displayed by Mancinus. Such precedents make a public world image intelligible without proving that Agrippa’s work followed modern cartographic conventions. No surviving large-scale public Roman map settles its precise appearance.
