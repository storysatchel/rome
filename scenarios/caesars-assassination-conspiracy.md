# Caesar's Assassination Conspiracy

In one reconstruction of the conspiracy against Caesar, the plot grew around Cassius’s political associates rather than beginning as a group led by Brutus. Cassius originated the plot, while Brutus joined only in the final weeks before the assassination; this sequence depends on the interpretation of Plutarch and Appian.

## Building the conspiracy

Cassius initially struggled to broaden his circle. It could be dismissed as a group of aggrieved Pompeians unwilling to accept Caesar’s regime, and his personal hostility to Caesar made commitment risky for potential recruits. Caesar’s armed protection posed a further practical obstacle.

## Agreement on Caesar, disagreement on Antony

As the conspiracy expanded, secrecy and communication became harder to maintain. Its members agreed on removing Caesar but differed over what else to do. Cassius and others wanted Antony killed as well. Brutus objected that killing Caesar’s deputy would undermine the moral and constitutional justification they claimed for their action, especially because Antony was consul.

Brutus expected leading Caesarians to cooperate in restoring the Republic after Caesar’s death, but that expectation did not amount to a secured political settlement. The plot’s shared target should not be mistaken for agreement on what would follow.

## Getting Caesar to the meeting

The final preparations show the importance of conspirators who retained Caesar’s trust. When Caesar delayed attending the senate meeting at the Theatre of Pompey, the plotters sent Decimus Brutus to his house. In the ancient narrative, Decimus appealed to the dignity of the waiting senate and persuaded him to appear despite his wife’s anxiety, adverse omens, and concern about his health. These details come through a heavily narrated ancient tradition and should not be read as access to Caesar’s private thoughts.

## Isolating the target

Decimus and Trebonius were assigned to keep Antony occupied outside, allowing Caesar to enter without a man who might resist the attack. Inside, Cimber pressed a request for his brother’s recall from exile while other senators crowded around Caesar’s chair. Cimber then pulled down Caesar’s toga as the signal for violence; Casca struck the first blow. Caesar reportedly struggled back, including using his stylus against Casca. The operation depended on a familiar petitioning encounter becoming a coordinated assault, not merely on the conspirators sharing political grievances.
