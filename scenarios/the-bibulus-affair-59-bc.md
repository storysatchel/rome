# The Bibulus Affair: Sky-Watching Against Caesar's Consulship (59 BC)

Unable to stop his co-consul Julius Caesar's legislative agenda by ordinary political means, M. Calpurnius Bibulus resorted to a specifically religious obstruction tactic in 59 BC: retreating to his own house for most of the year and claiming to be continuously "watching the sky" (*servare de caelo*) for unfavorable omens, a procedure which, if validly invoked, should have legally voided any assembly held while it was in effect — including the assemblies that passed [[entities/julius-caesar]]'s agrarian and other legislation.

## Parties & Stakes

- **M. Calpurnius Bibulus** ([[entities/marcus-calpurnius-bibulus]]), cos. 59 — Caesar's conservative co-consul; attempted to block Caesar's legislative program by announcing he was sky-watching for the rest of the year.
- **C. Iulius Caesar** ([[entities/julius-caesar]]), cos. 59 — pressed ahead with his assemblies and legislation despite Bibulus's announced sky-watching, on the theory that Bibulus had never properly completed the required in-person ritual announcement (*obnuntiatio*).
- The College of Augurs — their doctrine on whether *servare de caelo* alone (without a formal, in-person *obnuntiatio*) legally voided an assembly in progress became the crux of the entire dispute.
- Cicero — an augur himself from 53 BC, later analyzed and cited the affair repeatedly (especially in the *Philippics*, against Antony) as a precedent for what counted as valid augural obstruction.

## What Happened

As one of two consuls for 59 BC, Bibulus found himself outmaneuvered by Caesar's superior popular support and allies (including the tribune Vatinius) and unable to block Caesar's land-redistribution and other legislation through ordinary vetoes or physical obstruction, which Caesar's supporters met with violence. Bibulus then invoked a specifically religious procedure: he announced he would be "watching the sky" for the religiously significant remainder of the year, a status which — if properly and formally declared — should have rendered any public assembly held during that period *inauspicata* (religiously flawed) and its resulting legislation void. Bibulus, however, made this declaration from his own house rather than delivering it in person at the relevant assemblies, and scholars dispute (both in antiquity and today) whether a sky-watching declaration not formally and personally announced (*obnuntiatio*) actually carried binding legal force, or whether it had to be delivered face-to-face to have effect. Caesar proceeded with his assemblies regardless, and his legislation passed.

## Outcome

Caesar's laws took effect and were never formally invalidated during the Republic's remaining years, but the religious-legal cloud over their validity persisted for the rest of his career — opponents, including Cicero at various points, periodically raised the possibility that Caesar's 59 BC legislation was void *ab initio* because it had been passed in defiance of Bibulus's announced (if arguably procedurally defective) sky-watching. The affair became a central reference point in later Roman debates (including Cicero's own theoretical writings on augury) over exactly what formal steps were required to make a magistrate's religious obstruction of an assembly legally binding.

## Open Questions

- Whether sky-watching alone, without a formal in-person *obnuntiatio*, sufficed to block assemblies remains disputed. One interpretation holds that it did and that Caesar's violent disregard for law explains the assemblies' continuation; another holds that a binding obstruction required the formal announcement. The latter interpretation underlies the procedural qualification used here, but its detailed basis has not been fully assessed. ^[ambiguous]
