# The Immolation of the Vestal Oppia Amid Patrician-Plebeian Unrest (483 BC)

The earliest Vestal incestum case recorded for the Republican period, in 483 BC, ended with a Vestal named (per Livy) Oppia accused, convicted, and put to death — against a political backdrop of early patrician-plebeian conflict over land that Wildfang argues was not coincidental to the charge's timing.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Oppia** ([[entities/oppia-vestal-483-bc]]) — the accused Vestal; ancient sources vary on her name (also given as Opimia, Pompilia, or Popillia), but Livy's "Oppia" is generally preferred by modern scholars. If this name is correct, she was likely a plebeian — an unusually early instance of a non-patrician Vestal.
- The Pontifical College — exclusively patrician at this date, the body that tried and convicted her.
- The plebeian faction, more broadly — the political constituency Wildfang argues was implicitly targeted by the verdict, amid contemporaneous unrest over agrarian reform between patricians and plebeians.

## What Happened

Livy records that suspicion against Oppia first arose because of unspecified ominous portents (*prodigia*) occurring that year, with no further circumstantial detail surviving about the specific accusation, trial, or evidence. Because the Pontifical College trying her was entirely patrician, and because 483 BC fell within a period of sustained political conflict between patricians and plebeians over agricultural land reform, Wildfang argues that — if Oppia was indeed plebeian, as her name suggests — her accusation and execution functioned (consciously or not, on the part of the patrician pontifices) as an implicit warning to the plebeian faction about the dangers of challenging the political status quo, using the body of a plebeian priestess as the vehicle for that message.

## Outcome

Oppia was convicted of *incestum* and executed — the standard punishment of live burial, though Livy's brief notice for this earliest case does not specify the method in the same detail given for later, better-documented immolations.

## Open Questions

- The case for a plebeian, politically motivated reading rests substantially on accepting "Oppia" as the correct name among four variants in the ancient sources, and on Wildfang's broader thesis about political triggers for Vestal accusations generally — not on any direct ancient statement connecting the trial to contemporary politics. ^[ambiguous]
