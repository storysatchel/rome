# Roman Slavery, Agrarian Exploitation, and Manumission

In Roman civil law, an enslaved person (*servus*) was classified as a chattel possession (*res*), looked upon in law as property of the household rather than a member of it. Slavery underpinned agricultural production, domestic maintenance, mining, manufacture, and large sectors of imperial administration and commerce.

## Agrarian Exploitation: The Agronomists

Archaic and Republican agricultural manuals treat enslaved workers as economic inputs subject to strict balance-sheet management. In *De Agri Cultura* (2.7), Cato the Elder instructs the estate owner on the annual inspection of a rural villa:
> "You should sell the oil, if you can get your price, the surplus wine and corn, the old cattle, the worn out oxen, and the cull sheep, the wool and the hides, the old and sick slaves, and if any thing else is superfluous you should sell that. The appetite of the good farmer is to sell, not to buy." (*patrem familias vendacem, non emacem esse oportet*).

Modern scholarship cautions against reading this aphoristic list as evidence of systematic, regular disposal of aged slaves across all estates. The passage builds rhetorical momentum toward a concluding maxim on thrift. However, the manual documents the legal and economic premise that an incapacitated slave was treated as an expendable asset to be monetized rather than maintained at estate expense.

## Philosophical Counter-Perspectives

The reduction of human beings to pure legal chattel prompted moral critiques within Roman literature, most prominently in Stoic philosophy. In *Epistulae Morales* (47.1), Seneca challenges the social contempt directed at household slaves:
> "'They are slaves,' people declare. Nay, rather they are men. 'Slaves!' No, comrades. 'Slaves!' No, they are unpretentious friends. 'Slaves!' No, they are our fellow-slaves, if one reflects that Fortune has equal rights over slaves and free men alike."

Seneca does not advocate the abolition of legal slavery, but urges humane domestic treatment (*contubernales*), shared meals, and the recognition of moral agency, arguing that master and slave share the same human nature and vulnerability to catastrophe.

## Manumission and Citizenship

Uniquely among ancient Mediterranean slave societies, Roman law permitted enslaved persons freed by a Roman citizen owner to acquire civic freedom and, under established conditions, Roman citizenship.

### Formal Modes of Manumission
1. ***Manumissio vindicta:*** A fictitious lawsuit before a praetor or provincial governor, where a third party asserted the slave's freedom and the owner offered no defense.
2. ***Manumissio censu:*** The owner enrolled the enslaved person on the Roman citizen tax-and-census roll during the Republican censorship.
3. ***Manumissio testamento:*** Freedom granted in the owner's will, taking effect upon the testator's death.

### Augustan Statutory Regulation
During the reign of Augustus, fears of mass manumission eroding civic cohesion and public finances led to restrictive statutory frameworks:
- ***Lex Fufia Caninia* (2 BC):** Limited the proportion of household slaves an owner could free by testament (capping testamentary releases at a maximum of one hundred).
- ***Lex Aelia Sentia* (AD 4):** Required owners under age twenty and slaves under age thirty to show "due cause" (*iusta causa*) before a council of senators and equestrians; manumissions intended to defraud creditors or patrons were voided; slaves who had suffered disgraceful punishments (*torture, branding*) were granted only *dediticii* status, permanently barred from citizenship and forbidden within a hundred miles of Rome.
- Slaves freed without formal statutory procedures acquired informal liberty as Junian Latins (*Latini Iuniani*), living as free individuals but seeing their property revert to their patrons upon death (*quasi peculium*).

## The Patron–Freedman Obligation

A freedman (*libertus*) became a Roman citizen or Latin, taking the *praenomen* and *gentilicium* of his former master, who became his legal patron (*patronus*). The relationship maintained substantial legal inequality:
- The freedman owed *obsequium* (reverence and deference) and could not bring defamatory lawsuits or capital accusations against his patron without praetorian permission.
- The patron could contractually claim *operae* (stipulated days of labor or economic services).
- The patron retained statutory rights of inheritance in the freedman's estate if the freedman died without biological children.
