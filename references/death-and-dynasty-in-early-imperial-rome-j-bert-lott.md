# Death and Dynasty in Early Imperial Rome

> [!tldr] J. Bert Lott uses Roman inscriptions, senatorial decrees, laws, and literary texts to explain how dynastic deaths communicated succession and imperial ideals.

## Scope

Lott assembles key sources on the deaths and commemorations of Gaius Caesar, Lucius Caesar, Germanicus, and Drusus. The book includes Latin texts, translations, commentary, Tacitean excerpts, and biographies of the emperors.

The introduction treats death as a public dynastic event. Rituals, monuments, official business, and Senate-produced memorial guidelines transformed private loss into communication about succession and the stability of the Principate.

## Evidence and argument

- The source set includes seven government documents and eight stone or bronze inscriptions.
- Documents concerning Germanicus's death and the Piso trial show how official commemoration and treason proceedings could define acceptable dynastic memory.
- Funerary and memorial texts complement the *Res Gestae* by showing how imperial ideals were communicated through public responses to death.
- The case studies connect dynastic succession to the institutional and commemorative order established under [[entities/augustus]].

The material is especially useful with [[concepts/laudatio-funebris]] and questions of adoption and succession addressed in [[concepts/roman-elite-adoption-practices]].

## Source criticism

The inscriptions and decrees are official or public commemorative artifacts. They reveal authorized representations of dynastic legitimacy, not an unfiltered record of private grief or political opinion. ^[inferred]

## Open questions

- How did local communities adapt imperial memorial formulas to their own political circumstances? ^[ambiguous]
- When did commemoration of a dead heir stabilize succession, and when did it expose a succession crisis? ^[ambiguous]
