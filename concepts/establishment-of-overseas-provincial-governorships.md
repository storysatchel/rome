# Establishment of Overseas Provincial Governorships

Informal mid-Republican hegemony developed into standing provincial governorships through a sequence of institutional steps rather than a single reform: new praetorships to govern annexed territory, tax administration in Spain, extortion complaints and unsuccessful trials, and the permanent extortion court established in 149 BC. This sequence helps explain the origins of the system described in [[concepts/roman-provincial-administration]].

## Key Ideas

- **Praetorian governors with permanent troops, unlike the Greek "liberation" model**: by 196–194 BC the senate had three templates for controlling conquered territory — citizen/allied colonization, informal "hegemony" with no resident official, or "the dispatch of a praetorian governor, as in Sicily and Sardinia and now Spain," where "troops accompanied the governor to enforce his will and secure Roman rule." The evacuation of Greece in 194 BC rather than establishment of another such governorship may reflect the political cost of another standing praetorship: "each praetor hoped to advance to one of the two consulates," so more praetors intensified competition. This offers a structural explanation for different treatment of conquered regions.
- **The praetor count tracks provincial expansion**: praetors grew from one (to c. 242 BC) to four (to 197 BC) to six thereafter — the four-praetor stage included resident governors for Sicily and Sardinia, and the six-praetor stage corresponded to the division of Spain into Hispania Citerior and Ulterior in 197 BC. (See [[concepts/quaestorship-and-provincial-finance]] for the associated increase in quaestors.)
- **A concrete named tax-administration system in Spain**: Ti. Sempronius Gracchus (the elder, father of the later tribunes — [[entities/tiberius-sempronius-gracchus-cos-177-163]]) put in place a settlement that required Spanish communities to supply grain to the Roman forces stationed there — the system functioning, in effect, as a standing provincial *stipendium*-in-kind levy administered by the governor rather than a Roman magistrate in Rome.
- **Governor extortion allegations and failed prosecutions (171 BC)**: Spanish delegations complained to the senate that several praetorian governors had set an arbitrarily high price on the grain required by Gracchus's settlement, demanded cash at that price, bought the grain needed at market prices, and pocketed the difference. They also allegedly sent Roman officers to collect directly from towns rather than allowing local administration, "which invited abuse" (Livy 43.2.1–11). The senate ordered trials, but provincials had to use senatorial patrons rather than speak for themselves, and the supervising praetor selected senatorial jurors. One defendant was acquitted, two departed into voluntary exile, and the praetor's departure for his province foreclosed further hearings. The episode can be read as senatorial protection of peers over provincial subjects.
- **The 149 BC quaestio de rebus repetundis as permanent accountability machinery**: Ser. Sulpicius Galba's massacre and enslavement in 150 BC of Lusitanians who had surrendered under a promise of resettlement "outraged public opinion at Rome" and nearly produced his prosecution. The fallout contributed to legislation creating the first permanent criminal court, concerned with "abuse of magisterial power, particularly in the provinces and particularly in money matters." Its senatorial jurors convicted no defendant in its first twenty-eight years. One interpretation therefore sees it primarily as an attempt to control governors through the threat of post-tenure prosecution, rather than effective protection for provincial subjects.
- **Timing bridges the mid-Republic and the inherited Late Republican system**: standing praetorian governors and garrisons (227–197 BC), in-kind tax administration in Spain, attested complaints of abuse (171 BC), and a permanent accountability court (149 BC) form an institutional chain toward the extortion-prone, senatorially supervised governorships of the Late Republic (see [[scenarios/verres-quaestorial-embezzlement-under-carbo]]).

## Open Questions

- The later extension of provincial organization to Macedonia and the changing arrangements in Greece require separate treatment; the sequence outlined here does not establish that all conquered regions became provinces in the same way.
- The court's failure to convict for twenty-eight years distinguishes creation of accountability machinery from effective provincial protection. The effects of later reforms, including changes to jury composition under the Gracchi, remain outside this account. ^[ambiguous]
