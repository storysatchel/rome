# Rome and the Mediterranean 290 to 146 BC: The Imperial Republic

Nathan Rosenstein's volume in the Edinburgh History of Ancient Rome series (Edinburgh University Press, 2012) covers Rome's transformation from a regional Italian hegemon in 290 BC — the year the Third Samnite War ended at Sentinum — to master of the entire Mediterranean basin by 146 BC, the year of Carthage's and Corinth's destruction. Rosenstein, then Professor of History at Ohio State (and honoree of the companion volume *Romans at War*, this batch's second source), explicitly reframes Polybius's founding question — "by what means...was almost the whole of the inhabited world conquered" — for a modern audience informed by decades of scholarship that has substantially revised Polybius's own account of Roman government.

## Core Argument / Thesis

Rosenstein argues that Rome's conquest cannot be explained by Polybius's "mixed constitution" model (which he states outright "was wrong" about the Roman constitution — an aristocracy, not a balanced polity, governed Rome until Caesar) nor reduced to a single cause. He synthesizes and intervenes in the modern "Roman imperialism" debate — from Harris's thesis of a uniquely war-driven, profit-seeking aristocratic society to Eckstein's "interstate anarchy" model, in which all ancient states were comparably bellicose and Rome's success instead rested on superior alliance management and resource mobilization. Rosenstein's own distinct contribution, carried through from his earlier *Rome at War: Farms, Families, and Death in the Middle Republic* (2004), is that the allied manpower system (*socii* bound by the *formula togatorum* and *foedera*) and a resilient agrarian demography — not land dispossession of small farmers, nor plunder as the primary war-financing mechanism — together explain how Rome sustained decades of simultaneous warfare without social collapse. ## Key Claims and Evidence

- Rome's directly controlled territory (*ager Romanus*) grew from roughly 38,600 km² after Sentinum (294 BC) to nearly 100,000 km² by 225 BC, while the allied (*socii*) population numbered some 725,000 by 290 BC across the peninsula — a demographic base Rosenstein treats as the structural precondition for sustained large-scale warfare.
- *Imperium* in the 3rd–2nd centuries meant "hegemony" or "dominion," not a territorial empire in the modern sense; the degree of subordination it implied varied hugely, from the nearly untouched Illyrian tribes to the formal treaty-bound Italian *socii*, whom Rome demanded military manpower from (via the *formula togatorum*) but did not tax in money or interfere with internally.
- Citing Polybius's Fabius Pictor-derived 225 BC manpower table, Rosenstein notes Rome and its allies could field roughly 580,000 infantry and 54,000 cavalry — a force Rosenstein compares favorably to Alexander the Great's invasion army of fewer than 60,000, underscoring the scale advantage Rome's alliance system conferred.
- Rosenstein directly challenges the older scholarly consensus (citing earlier historians by name) that war-driven conscription dispossessed small Roman and Italian farmers, enriching senatorial landowners who then built slave-staffed latifundia; he argues (per his own *Rome at War*) that marriage-timing flexibility, widespread small-scale slaveholding, and women's agricultural labor let small farms survive sustained military service without systemic collapse.
- He estimates combat and disease mortality among legionaries and allied *socii* between 200–168 BC at over 100,000 combat deaths plus 75,000–100,000 disease deaths, a combined annual mortality rate of 4.75–5.45%, meaning 34–40% of men serving in this period may never have returned — yet argues this mortality was net *beneficial* to surviving rural families via earlier inheritance, better land access, and favorable marriage markets. ^[inferred]
- Urban population growth at Rome (from roughly 200,000 c. 200 BC to 375,000–500,000 by mid-century) is attributed mainly to the pull of construction employment and festival/spectacle life rather than to displaced farmers fleeing dispossession, a point Rosenstein makes by calculating that supplying Rome's wine and oil needs required only about 0.5% of Italy's arable land and 35,000–70,000 slave workers — far too small a labor footprint to explain mass displacement.
- The 186 BC Bacchanalian Conspiracy suppression is read not primarily as religious persecution but as senatorial anxiety about any organization operating outside aristocratic oversight, compounded by genuine uncertainty in the evidence about the cult's actual scale and intentions. ^[ambiguous]
- Rosenstein's account of the "new brutality" in Greece during 171 BC names the specific episode behind it: two tribunes of the plebs successfully prosecuted the praetor who had commanded the fleet for abuses against the allies, securing his conviction, exile, and a fine — corroborating and dating the Sage sourcebook's account of the sack of Haliartus (see [[scenarios/siege-and-sack-of-haliartus]] and the companion trial-procedure page [[scenarios/the-trial-of-gaius-lucretius]]).

## Structure / Scope

Seven chapters move chronologically and thematically: an introductory survey of the mid-Republican aristocracy and Roman constitution (magistrates, priesthoods, popular assemblies including the wealth-weighted *comitia centuriata*); Rome's wars with Pyrrhus and Carthage; the mechanics of *imperium* and the allied army system; the Hannibalic War; the conquest of Gaul, Greece, and Spain; a chapter on "the new brutality" of later 2nd-century warfare; and a concluding chapter on the social and economic impact of empire. Primary source reliance centers on Polybius (for the constitution and the Hannibalic War) and Livy (for narrative detail, especially via Table 1's reconstruction of the *comitia centuriata*'s wealth-weighted voting structure), supplemented by numismatic evidence (coin reverses illustrating magistrates, deities, the *corvus*) and archaeological material (the Numantia camps, battle-site plans for Trebia, Trasimene, Cannae, Ilipa, New Carthage).

## Significance

Rosenstein situates the book explicitly against both ends of the modern imperialism debate — Harris's "Roman exceptionalism" thesis and Eckstein's "IR Realist" counter-model — without fully endorsing either, instead building a case from the specific mechanics of Italian alliance and agrarian demography that he developed at length in his own earlier monograph *Rome at War* (2004). As the honoree of *Romans at War: Soldiers, Citizens, and Society in the Roman Republic* (this batch's companion volume, dedicated to him and citing this book by name), Rosenstein's demographic and economic arguments here are treated by his students and collaborators as a load-bearing contribution to the field's current "war and society" consensus. ## Open Questions

- Rosenstein's revisionist demographic argument depends on model-based estimates (life-table-style reconstructions of land availability, marriage markets, and migration) rather than direct ancient testimony, and he acknowledges that Roman census figures for this period are themselves contested (compare his Table 4's "raw" vs. Brunt's "corrected" counts, which differ by 10–20% in several years). ^[ambiguous]
- The causes of the Bacchanalian suppression are explicitly left unresolved by Rosenstein himself, who states the episode "defies a convincing solution" given how thoroughly the evidence has been overlaid with melodrama.
