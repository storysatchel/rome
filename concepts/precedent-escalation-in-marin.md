# Precedent Escalation in Late Republican Political Violence

The breakdown of the late Roman Republic functioned as an escalating sequence of unconstitutional precedents, where each norm violation lowered the barrier for subsequent, more severe interventions.

## Key Ideas

- In 132 BC, following the murder of Tiberius Gracchus, an extraordinary senatorial commission empowered consuls to execute and exile citizens without trial—a measure Gaius Gracchus later outlawed, but which established a precedent for emergency suspensions of civic protections in subsequent civil conflicts.
The election of Saturninus to a third consecutive tribunate in 100 BC established a precedent for continuous officeholding, breaking annual rotation norms.
Sulla's march on Rome in 88 BC set the unprecedented precedent of marching citizen legions against the constitutional government of the capital.
The Sullan proscriptions in 82 BC formalized judicial murder as a tool of political settlement, replacing trial rights with state-sponsored execution lists.
The precedent of stepping down from emergency autocratic power was broken by Caesar's acceptance of the perpetual dictatorship in 44 BC.
Augustus subsequently stabilized the Roman state by formalizing extraordinary powers within traditional constitutional forms rather than openly overriding them.

## Open Questions

Precedent escalation operated both through institutional decrees and through armed coercion by individual warlords.
The long-term institutionalization of extraordinary commands under Augustus bridged Republican precedent with imperial monarchy.
