# Gnaeus Pompeius Magnus

Pompey is one of the three citing sources' most-attested late Republican figures: a general who reached unprecedented command and honors largely through military achievement, patronage networks, and reputation rather than public speaking.

## Biography / Role

- Van der Blom's case-study chapter on Pompey is built around the theme of politics conducted "behind the scenes." She classifies him, alongside Piso Caesoninus and Mark Antony, as a "reactive" rather than active orator: he spoke when magistracy required it but used visual spectacle, military reputation, and patronage to build his public profile instead of actively seeking out speaking occasions, and he was never designated a "good orator" by contemporaries or posterity — a sharp contrast with Gracchus, Caesar, and Cato, who are classed as active seekers of oratorical occasions.
- In the companion volume *Institutions and Ideology in Republican Rome*, Santangelo's chapter (summarized in van der Blom/Gray/Steel) argues that Theophanes of Mytilene's importance to Pompey was political as much as cultural — Theophanes functioned as a discreet confidential agent, a role paralleled by Balbus for Caesar. This reinforces the "behind the scenes" reading of Pompey's methods: cultivating trusted intermediaries rather than public oratory to manage his political interests.
- The same volume notes Morrell's reinterpretation of Cato the Younger's and Pompey's uneasy 52 BC cooperation (after Clodius's murder) as a shared commitment to civic order overriding mutual suspicion — evidence that even Pompey's rivalries with committed oratorical politicians like Cato could yield to pragmatic alliance when public order was at stake.
- Campbell's narrative places Pompey's supporters among those Caesar pardoned after the civil war, framing Caesar's clemency (in contrast to Sulla's proscriptions) as a deliberate, ultimately failed attempt to end the Optimates-Populares cycle of vengeance — Pompey himself is thus positioned as the civil war's losing pole against Caesar, with Pompey's death implicit in the broader arc toward Octavian/Augustus's eventual consolidation.
- Van der Blom's volume notes that if Cicero's *Brutus* were rewritten with a broader inclusion criterion (projecting a coherent public narrative rather than oratorical skill alone), Pompey is already among the figures Cicero's own history "could not ignore" even under the narrower, oratory-only criterion — unlike Sulla, Piso, Cato, Antony, Catiline, and Clodius, who would only be admitted under the revised standard. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- Van der Blom's reading of Pompey as a non-orator who built power through alternative channels sits specifically against a Cicero-centric model of political success; how far this corrective generalizes beyond her six case studies is not addressed in the citing pages.
