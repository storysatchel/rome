# The Duel of Marcellus and Viridomarus at Clastidium

In 222 BC the consul Marcus Claudius Marcellus led a Roman army against the Insubrian Gauls near [[entities/clastidium|Clastidium]] in [[entities/cisalpine-gaul|Cisalpine Gaul]]. The Insubrian king, Viridomarus, challenged Marcellus to single combat. Marcellus accepted, killed Viridomarus, and stripped his armor on the battlefield. Because Marcellus was the *supreme* Roman commander in the field and had killed an *enemy* supreme commander in personal combat, Roman tradition recognized the kill as *spolia opima* — "triumphal" or "supreme spoils" — a category of battlefield trophy traditionally dedicated only twice before: mythically by Romulus, and historically by Cornelius Cossus against Veii in the mid-fifth century BC.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Marcus Claudius Marcellus** ([[entities/marcus-claudius-marcellus]]) — goal: battlefield glory and the unique religious-political honor of the *spolia opima*; resources: consular imperium, a Roman field army, personal reputation as (per Plutarch) a man with "a penchant for single combat" who fought other duels besides this one.
- **Viridomarus** ([[entities/viridomarus]]) — goal: defend Insubrian territory and personal standing as king by defeating the Roman commander in the customary aristocratic-warfare ritual of a champions' duel; resources: status as supreme commander of the Insubrian Gauls.

## What Happened

Plutarch’s account states that Marcellus "had defeated and killed in single combat the king of the Insubrian Gauls, Viridomar, at Clastidium in 222." Marcellus then dedicated the stripped armor to the [[entities/temple-of-jupiter-feretrius|Temple of Jupiter Feretrius]] on the [[entities/capitoline-hill|Capitoline hill]], the traditional recipient of *spolia opima*. According to the account, he commemorated the kill with an unusual spectacle: he had a tall, straight oak trunk cut and worked into a kind of mannequin, onto which he fastened the Gallic armor piece by piece, and paraded this trophy in his procession. The custom of single combat between commanders is noted to have parallels elsewhere in the Mediterranean and may originate in archaic-period warfare between aristocratic warbands. ^[inferred]

Tradition recorded only three dedicators of the *spolia opima* in all of Roman history: the mythical Romulus, Cornelius Cossus against Veii in the mid-fifth century BC, and Marcellus himself in 222 BC. Some evidence allows the possibility that officers besides the supreme commander were once eligible to win these spoils; if so, the strict "commander-versus-commander" rule hardened only later. ^[ambiguous]

## Outcome

Viridomarus was killed and his kingdom's claim on the contested Cisalpine territory suffered a major blow; Marcellus won the third attested *spolia opima* in Roman history and a lasting religious-political distinction, later serving as his signature exploit (he also went on to sack Syracuse in 211 BC). The oak-trunk trophy display became a famous piece of triumphal theater.

## Open Questions

- Whether the duel was a formal pre-battle challenge accepted under a recognized ritual code, or an opportunistic kill during a general engagement, is not fully specified in the extracted text. ^[ambiguous]
- Marcellus reportedly fought "other duels" besides this one, but their details are not supplied in the passage used here.
