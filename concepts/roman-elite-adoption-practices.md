# Roman Elite Adoption Practices

Roman aristocratic adoption moved a son out of one patrician family's legal power and into another's, and functioned chiefly to perpetuate a family's name, cults, and property across generations rather than to formalize an emotional or purely biological bond. McCall's narration of how the [[entities/clan-fabius|Fabii Maximi]] averted extinction in the 180s BC, paired with the parallel adoptions into the Cornelii Scipiones, gives a detailed worked example of the mechanics and logic behind the practice.

## Key Ideas

- Around 180 BC, L. Aemilius Paullus — worried, per McCall, that four sons would "strain his estate excessively" — gave away his two elder sons (from his first marriage to Papiria) for adoption into other families while keeping his two younger sons (from a second marriage) as his own heirs; the elder two went to the Cornelii Scipiones and the Fabii Maximi respectively. ^[inferred]
- Adoption did not erase the adoptee's birth identity: he kept the adoptive family's full name but added an agnomen in "-anus"/"-ianus" marking his gens of birth, so Paullus's sons became Fabius Aemilianus and Scipio Aemilianus on adoption — publicly advertising rather than concealing the transfer.
- McCall frames the choice of adoptive family as mutually transactional: he hedges ("presumably," "may have been") that the wealthy, possibly heirless Scipio Africanus line was doing Paullus a favor (or vice versa) by taking one son, while the Fabii Maximi took the other because that branch "lacked male heirs to carry on... to future generations" after its own male line had died out. ^[ambiguous]
- Adoption did not sever ties to the biological father: both adoptees accompanied Paullus on his Macedonian campaign in 168 BC and rode in his triumph, and when Paullus died, Scipio Aemilianus — heir to the far wealthier Scipio fortune — gave his portion of Paullus's estate to his comparatively poorer adoptive-out brother Fabius Aemilianus, showing adoption created a durable, parallel kin network rather than a clean break. ^[inferred]
- The Fabii Maximi's rescue was not a single adoption but at least two: alongside Fabius Aemilianus, the family separately adopted a son of the Servilius Caepio family (probably a brother of the consul of 140 BC), who took the name [[entities/fabius-maximus-servilianus|Fabius Maximus Servilianus]] — a family facing extinction hedged its bets across multiple promising young men from different allied gentes rather than relying on one heir. ^[inferred]
- The strategy paid off across generations: both adoptees reached the consulship ([[entities/fabius-maximus-aemilianus|Aemilianus]] in 145 BC, Servilianus in 142 BC), and Aemilianus's own biological son went on to earn the cognomen Allobrogicus, becoming — per McCall — "the third most famous of the Fabii Maximi" after Rullianus and Cunctator; see [[entities/fabius-maximus-allobrogicus]].
- McCall situates the practice within a wider Roman aristocratic principle: "the name of an aristocratic family [was] far more important than any specific biological ties" — adoption was a routine, unremarkable succession instrument precisely because gens continuity, not bloodline purity, was what elite standing actually required.

## Open Questions

- McCall does not specify which Roman legal procedure — *adoptio* before a praetor (for a son still under his father's power) versus *adrogatio* before the people's assembly (for an independent adult) — applied to the Fabii's or Scipiones' 180s-BC adoptions, since Paullus's sons were evidently still minors; the book narrates the social transaction without naming the legal mechanism.
- Whether multi-adoption hedging, as the near-extinct Fabii Maximi practiced it, was routine among elite gentes or an unusually severe response to near-total male extinction is not addressed beyond this single family's case.
