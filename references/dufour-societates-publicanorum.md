# Societates Publicanorum : Existait-il une bourse ou un réseau de courtiers sous la République romaine?

## Coverage Note
Extensive legal and economic treatise by Geneviève Dufour (*Revue de Droit de l'Université de Sherbrooke* 41.2, 2011, pp. 307–380) analyzing the corporate status, capital mobilization, financial governance, and market mechanisms of the Roman tax-farming corporations (*societates publicanorum*).

## Sampled Claims and Institutional Analysis
- **Legal Entity Status (*Corpus*):** Establishes that while classical Roman civil law (*ius civile*) treated standard commercial partnerships (*societas*) as strictly personal contracts dissolving upon the death or withdrawal of any single partner (*socius*), the *societas publicanorum* enjoyed exceptional corporate personality (*corpus habere*). Recognized by public law (*ius publicum*), these corporations held indivisible corporate property, operated under continuous legal succession, and could litigate through an appointed syndic.
- **Corporate Hierarchy and Governance:** Details the administrative division of labor within the companies:
  1. The *manceps*: the prominent equestrian entrepreneur who personally submitted the winning bid at the censorial auction in Rome and formally contracted with the state under personal surety (*praedes*).
  2. The *magister societatis*: the managing director elected annually by the shareholders, resident in Rome, who maintained company ledgers and correspondence.
  3. The *promagistri*: regional directors stationed in overseas provinces (e.g. Asia, Sicily) commanding armed collection agents, couriers, and enslaved accountants.
- **Transferable Shares (*Partes* and *Particulae*):** Analyzes textual evidence from Cicero (*In Verrem*, *Pro Rabirio Postumo*, *Pro Lege Manilia*, *Ad Atticum*) demonstrating that capital was raised through transferable shares (*partes*) held by major partners and fractional micro-shares (*particulae*) widely dispersed among senators, equestrians, and prosperous freedmen.
- **Financial Market at the Forum:** Investigates whether Rome developed a stock exchange, concluding that while an institutional bourse did not exist, the northern Forum around the Basilica Aemilia, the Janus Medius, and the banking tables (*mensae argentariae*) operated as an active over-the-counter financial market where shares were bought, sold, and discounted through private bankers (*argentarii*).
