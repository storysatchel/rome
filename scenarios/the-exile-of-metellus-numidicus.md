# The Oath Refusal and Exile of Metellus Numidicus

When the radical tribune Lucius Appuleius Saturninus passed a land law in 100 BC, he included a clause requiring every sitting senator to publicly swear an oath to uphold it. On the fifth day after the law's passage, the senators were summoned to take the oath. Gaius Marius, hedging his bets, swore to uphold the law only "insofar as it was a law" — leaving himself a future excuse that the law's violent, irregular passage had invalidated it. Seeing Marius's loophole, the other senators followed suit and swore. Metellus Numidicus alone refused outright, on principle.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Quintus Caecilius Metellus Numidicus** ([[entities/quintus-caecilius-metellus-numidicus]]) — goal: refuse to legitimize a law passed through violence and intimidation; stakes: his Senate seat, his place in Rome
- **Lucius Appuleius Saturninus** ([[entities/lucius-appuleius-saturninus]]) — goal: enforce unanimous senatorial submission to his law; resources: armed supporters sent to physically remove Metellus from the [[entities/curia-hostilia|Senate house]]
- **Gaius Marius** ([[entities/marius]]) — role: swore the oath with a deliberately ambiguous formula, avoiding direct confrontation
- **Furius (tribune, 99 BC)** — goal: block Metellus's recall and seize his estate; outcome: later torn apart by a mob after being prosecuted himself

## What Happened

When other tribunes objected to Saturninus's attempt to physically eject Metellus from the Senate house, Saturninus instead moved to formally charge Metellus with violating the new law and have him tried directly before the people. Metellus, unwilling to fight the charge or compromise his principles, chose exile rather than contest the case.

## Outcome

Metellus went into exile in 100 BC. The political tide turned quickly against Saturninus, who was killed before the year was out. In 99 BC the tribune Furius tried to block Metellus's recall and confiscate his estate, but a law attracting broad popular support secured Metellus's return that same year. The following year, 98 BC, a mob tore Furius to pieces after he was himself prosecuted — before a verdict could even be reached. Decades later, Cato the Younger and Metellus Celer would invoke Metellus Numidicus's principled refusal as precedent when they considered (but ultimately did not follow through on) refusing to swear an oath to one of Caesar's laws.

## Open Questions

- Watts does not specify the formal charge brought against Metellus or the exact vote by which he was condemned.
