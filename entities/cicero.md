# Marcus Tullius Cicero

Cicero is the dominant evidentiary source across all three citing works, but the three sources diverge sharply on how representative his career should be taken to be — a tension that is itself one of the most important cross-source findings in this batch.

## Biography / Role

- Morstein-Marx's *Mass Oratory and Political Power in the Late Roman Republic* draws chiefly on Cicero (alongside Sallust) to reconstruct the ideology of the *contio*, the Republic's central public political meeting. Cicero's self-presentation in the *Catilinarian* orations — always wakeful, always anticipating hidden plots — is Morstein-Marx's clearest example of the "revelatory" rhetorical mode (verbs like *docere*, *monere*, *ostendere*, *exponere*) by which orators presented themselves as privileged disclosers of hidden senatorial truths to an audience with no independent way to verify those claims.
- *Institutions and Ideology in Republican Rome* (van der Blom, Gray, and Steel, eds.) likewise treats Cicero's speeches and letters as "the single richest body of evidence" for the late Republic, supplemented heavily by the fragmentary non-Ciceronian oratory the volume's contributors assembled via the FRRO project. Within this volume, Clark's chapter argues Cicero's oratory is the fullest surviving evidence for how speakers invoked the gods in political argument, and Haimson Lushkov's chapter examines his *Pro Murena*.
- Van der Blom's own monograph, *Oratory and Political Career in the Late Roman Republic*, takes the opposite tack: it deliberately moves analysis away from Cicero — whose surviving speeches dominate the field precisely because they are uniquely fully preserved — toward six other politicians (Gaius Gracchus, Pompey, Caesar, Piso Caesoninus, Cato the Younger, Mark Antony). Her explicit argument is that Cicero's career, beginning in the courts at the comparatively late age of thirty-six and continuing forensic advocacy even as a senior consular, is itself **exceptional rather than paradigmatic** — a direct methodological corrective to treating Cicero-based evidence as representative of Republican political careers generally.
- This produces a documented tension across the batch: Morstein-Marx's and the edited *Institutions and Ideology* volume's analyses are substantively Cicero-centric by evidentiary necessity (his is the best-preserved oratory), while van der Blom's solo monograph is explicitly conceived as a corrective to that same Cicero-centrism, arguing that Tacitus's *Dialogus de oratoribus* — which presents eloquence as a near-absolute necessity for Republican public success — inherited this view largely from Cicero's own self-presentation as the orator-statesman *par excellence*, rather than from a representative survey of actual Republican careers. ^[ambiguous] (an explicit methodological disagreement among sources in this batch, not silently resolved)
- Van der Blom's volume notes that Cicero's own history of Roman orators, the *Brutus*, used oratorical ability as its inclusion criterion, which is why it excludes effective-but-non-oratorical politicians like Marius; her closing argument proposes that a rewritten *Brutus* judged instead by the ability to project a coherent public narrative would admit figures like Piso, Cato, Antony, Sulla, Catiline, and Clodius alongside the orators Cicero's criterion already could not ignore (Gracchus, Pompey, Caesar) — an implicit critique of Cicero's own evidentiary and historiographical standard as itself too narrow. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- The degree to which Morstein-Marx's and the *Institutions and Ideology* volume's necessarily Cicero-heavy evidence base might itself skew their broader conclusions about contional ideology is a tension the sources raise about each other but do not resolve within this batch — van der Blom's corrective is aimed specifically at the career-success literature, not at the contional-ideology scholarship directly.
