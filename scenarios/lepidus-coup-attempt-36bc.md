# Lepidus's Failed Bid for Power Against Octavian, 36 BC

Marcus Aemilius Lepidus, the junior member of the Second Triumvirate alongside Mark Antony and Octavian, joined Octavian's campaign in 36 BC against Sextus Pompey (son of Pompey the Great), who had seized [[entities/sicilia|Sicily]] and was disrupting Rome's grain supply. After the defeat of Sextus Pompey at the naval Battle of [[entities/naulochus|Naulochus]], Lepidus attempted to assert control over Sicily and challenge Octavian for supremacy — an attempted coup that collapsed when his own soldiers deserted to Octavian.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Marcus Aemilius Lepidus** ([[entities/marcus-aemilius-lepidus]]) — goal: parlay his military contribution to the Sicilian campaign into a larger share of power, challenging Octavian's dominance; resources: an army under his command stationed in Sicily.
- **Octavian** ([[entities/augustus]]) — goal: eliminate the weakest triumvir and consolidate control over the western provinces; resources: superior political standing, the loyalty (ultimately) of Lepidus's own troops.

## What Happened

Octavian defeated Sextus Pompey's fleet at the Battle of [[entities/naulochus|Naulochus]] in 36 BC, ending Pompey's hold on [[entities/sicilia|Sicily]] and restoring Rome's grain supply. In the campaign's aftermath, Lepidus, who had contributed forces to the victory, attempted to claim Sicily for himself and mount a direct challenge to Octavian's authority, effectively attempting a coup against his fellow triumvir. In the reconstructed account followed here, Octavian confronted Lepidus's camp personally; Lepidus's own soldiers abandoned him and went over to Octavian rather than fight, collapsing the attempted power grab without a battle.^[inferred]

## Outcome

Lepidus was stripped of his triumviral powers and his provinces, retaining only the largely ceremonial office of Pontifex Maximus for the rest of his life; he was effectively placed under informal house arrest/exile and played no further role in Roman politics. Octavian absorbed Lepidus's former territories and forces, leaving only Octavian and Antony to contest supremacy — a rivalry that would culminate in the Battle of Actium five years later.

## Open Questions

- The precise mechanics of how Lepidus's troops were persuaded to defect (bribery, personal appeals by Octavian, or simple recognition of the political reality) are not detailed in the source consulted here.
