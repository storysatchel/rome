# Caesar Judges Sulla's Hitmen: The 64 BC Proscription-Murder Trials

In 64 BC — nearly two decades after Sulla's proscriptions had legally shielded those who killed the men on his death-lists — [[entities/julius-caesar]], in a disputed role as presiding judge or prosecutor, helped reopen charges against surviving Sullan-era killers. Bellienus and Luscius were convicted, while his political ally Catiline was acquitted on a related charge.

## Parties & Stakes

- **L. Bellienus** ([[entities/lucius-bellienus]]), pr. 105 — convicted of murdering Q. Lucretius Afella (a Sullan partisan killed for defying Sulla's own authority, not technically a victim of the formal proscription lists).
- **L. Luscius** ([[entities/lucius-luscius-centurion]]), a centurion — convicted of three separate murders of men formally proscribed under Sulla.
- **L. Sergius Catilina** ([[entities/lucius-sergius-catilina]]), pr. 68 — tried on the same charge (killing of the proscribed) shortly after the other two, and acquitted, with consular *laudatores* speaking in his support.
- **C. Iulius Caesar** ([[entities/julius-caesar]]) — involved in all three cases, either as presiding *iudex quaestionis* or as *accusator* (prosecutor); his formal role is disputed. The proceedings reopened the legal consequences of Sulla’s proscriptions two decades later.

## What Happened

Caesar's political project in these years included rehabilitating the memory and the surviving victims' families of Sulla's proscriptions, which by 64 BC had passed from live political controversy into contested historical memory. On the reconstruction that places him as *iudex quaestionis* for the standing murder court (*lex Cornelia de sicariis et veneficis*), Caesar heard a cluster of cases against men who had killed individuals under color of Sulla’s now-defunct proscription authority. The proceedings came shortly before that year’s consular elections; deliberate electoral timing is an interpretation, not a directly established motive. Bellienus and Luscius, both relatively obscure figures without consular rank or major political protection, were convicted. Catiline — tried on essentially the same legal theory for his own killings during the proscriptions — was acquitted shortly afterward, with an array of consulars speaking in his defense, suggesting that political connections may have influenced the different outcomes, without establishing that they alone determined the verdicts.

## Outcome

Two minor Sullan killers were convicted decades after their crimes, showing that Sulla’s legal protection of proscription killings did not permanently prevent later prosecution. Catiline’s acquittal on a related charge may reflect the importance of political standing, but the different verdicts alone do not establish their causes. Suetonius and Cassius Dio portray Caesar’s involvement as selective and politically calculated; that ancient interpretation must be distinguished from a demonstrated account of his motives.

## Open Questions

- Whether Caesar's formal role was presiding *iudex quaestionis* or *accusator* (prosecutor) remains disputed. ^[ambiguous]
