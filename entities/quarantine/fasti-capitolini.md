# Fasti Capitolini

The Fasti Capitolini are monumental lists of Roman consuls and men who celebrated triumphs or ovations, carved on an arch in the Forum Romanum during Augustus’ reign. Their surviving portions are now in the Musei Capitolini, which gives the lists their modern name. They preserve chronological information, but they also reveal how Romans of the Augustan period organized and interpreted their past.

## A senatorial monument under Augustus

The Senate, rather than Augustus personally, decreed the erection of the inscription. The arch honored Augustus and carried his statue, but its lists also gave the Senate a way to represent its own corporate identity. The triumphal list runs from Romulus to Lucius Cornelius Balbus in 19 BCE; the consular list extends from the Republic’s beginning to 13 CE. Bringing these names together transformed competing family memories into a collective account of officeholding.

## A continuous history of office

The scale and regularity of the consular lists made a historical argument: the Republic appeared to have rested on consular power from its beginning. That presentation mattered because late Republican and early imperial antiquarians were debating the development of Rome’s magistracies, including whether the consulship really began in 509 BCE and why some accounts called the earliest chief magistrate a praetor. The monument’s apparent continuity should therefore be read as a claim about institutional history, not simply as an unmediated record of it.

## From family memories to a communal past

Consul lists had already been used to construct public history. Earlier lists displayed in the temple of Hercules Musarum sought to create a unified, communal account of Rome's past. The Fasti Capitolini continued this tradition on a far more monumental and comprehensive scale. The earlier fasti associated with Fulvius Nobilior are poorly known, so the comparison does not establish their precise contents; it instead highlights the unusual breadth of the Augustan monument.

Their public authority did not end disagreements over the remote past. Surviving texts provide no record of an ancient historian consulting the monument as a definitive chronological authority. Its importance was also social: publishing the names of consuls asserted the boundaries of consular distinction at a time when promotions to the Senate and the multiplication of suffect consulships had made elite status less exclusive.
