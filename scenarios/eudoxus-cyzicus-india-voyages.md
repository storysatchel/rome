# Eudoxus of Cyzicus's Voyages to India and Vanishing Circumnavigation Attempt

Around 120 BC, a half-drowned sailor was brought to the Ptolemaic court at Alexandria, the sole survivor of a shipwreck. Nursed back to health and taught Greek, he claimed to be an Indian who could guide a Greek expedition to his homeland by sea. The navigator Eudoxus of Cyzicus, backed by King Ptolemy VIII ("the Pot-Bellied") of Egypt, used this guide to make two pioneering voyages from the Red Sea to India and back — only to have his valuable cargo of spices confiscated by Ptolemy's customs officials on both occasions. Eudoxus then attempted to bypass Egyptian control entirely by sailing around Africa, a venture that ended his career and life in mystery.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Eudoxus of Cyzicus** ([[entities/eudoxus-of-cyzicus]]) — goal: break into the lucrative India-Arabia spice trade and, later, to circumvent Ptolemaic customs control by finding an alternative route; resources: royal backing, a rescued native guide with knowledge of the monsoon winds.
- **Ptolemy VIII of Egypt** ([[entities/ptolemy-viii]]) — goal: capture a share of the India trade for the Ptolemaic kingdom; resources: royal treasury financing the expeditions, and customs officials who confiscated the returning cargo as crown property.
- **The unnamed shipwrecked guide** — a Tamil-speaking southern Indian sailor, sole survivor of his crew, who possessed crucial sailing knowledge (of the monsoon winds) that Greek navigators lacked.

## What Happened

Indian and Arab merchants had long monopolized the sea trade in spices between India and the Red Sea, jealously guarding their knowledge of the monsoon wind patterns that made the open-sea crossing possible. The shipwrecked Indian guide, once rehabilitated at the Ptolemaic court, offered to lead an expedition back to his homeland. Eudoxus made two successful round-trip voyages to India using this guide's knowledge, safely navigating open water beyond Arab-controlled coastal routes. On both occasions, however, his cargo of Indian spices was seized by Ptolemy's customs officials upon return to Egypt, apparently as a form of taxation or appropriation so severe it amounted to confiscation of his entire profit.^[extracted] Frustrated, Eudoxus organized a well-equipped new expedition — reportedly even bringing dancing girls aboard — to reach India by circumnavigating Africa entirely, bypassing Ptolemaic customs jurisdiction altogether. This first circumnavigation attempt got only as far as the Atlantic coast of Morocco before his crew mutinied and forced him to turn back. Undiscouraged, Eudoxus fitted out a second expedition and set off again — and was never heard from again.

## Outcome

Eudoxus's fate remains unknown; he is presumed to have died at sea or been lost somewhere along the African coast. His voyages nonetheless advanced Greek geographic knowledge of the Indian Ocean monsoon system and the East African coastline (he had previously landed, during his return from his second India voyage, far south on the African coast after misjudging the monsoon, making contact with local peoples near Cape Guardafui). The repeated confiscation of his cargo by royal customs officials stands as a specific case of crown economic predation discouraging private commercial initiative in the Ptolemaic trading system.

## Open Questions

- The exact circumstances of Eudoxus's final disappearance are unknown.
- Later classical sources (Strabo, Pliny) preserve varying and sometimes skeptical versions of the story; its full historicity has long been debated.
