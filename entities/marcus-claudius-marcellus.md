# Marcus Claudius Marcellus

Marcellus's sack of Syracuse (211 BC) is treated by Gruen as a landmark moment in Rome's acquisition of Greek art as war spoils — though Gruen is careful to situate it within a longer-running Roman religious practice rather than present it as an unprecedented rupture.

## Biography / Role

- Gruen's *Culture and National Identity* identifies Marcellus's sack of Syracuse in 211 BC as the event most commonly cited for the acquisition and display of captured art as war trophies, but argues this practice actually predates Marcellus, with religious precedents extending back to Camillus's *evocatio* of Juno from Veii in 396 BC.
- Gruen situates Syracusan plunder within a pattern in which cult statues from defeated cities (Veii's Juno, Praeneste's Jupiter Imperator, Volsinii's Vortumnus, Falerii's Janus and Minerva Capta) were ritually "absorbed" into Roman state religion as tokens of divine favor rather than treated as simple plunder — implying Marcellus's Syracusan spoils, however famous, extended an existing religious-political logic rather than inventing a new one. ^[inferred]
- The influx of Greek artists to Rome (the painter Metrodorus; sculptors Timarchides, Polycles, Dionysius, Philiscus, Pasiteles) is dated by Gruen as intensifying especially after later conquests — L. Scipio Asiagenus's victory over Antiochus III (190 BC) and L. Aemilius Paullus's conquest of Macedon (168 BC) — suggesting Marcellus's Syracusan spoils were an early, prominent instance within a broader multi-generational pattern of war-spoil art acquisition rather than its sole driver.

## Open Questions

- Gruen's single citing page does not detail Marcellus's military campaign itself or his political career beyond the Syracuse episode's role in the Roman art-and-religion narrative; a fuller biographical account would require sources outside this batch.
