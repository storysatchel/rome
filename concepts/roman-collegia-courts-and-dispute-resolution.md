# Collegia, Courts, and Commercial Dispute Resolution in the Bazaar

Bang's Chapter 5 ("Community: Cult, Courts, Credit and Collaboration in the Bazaar") argues that Roman merchants compensated for the imperial state's weak, discontinuous legal enforcement by building their own social infrastructure — professional associations (*collegia*), patron-client ties, and private arbitration — that did the practical work later associated with courts and guilds.

## Key Ideas

- **Collegia were social/religious bodies first, economic regulators only incidentally.** Unlike medieval guilds, Greco-Roman *collegia* almost never held formally recognized powers to fix prices, cap membership, or set quality standards. Their documented focus was burial of deceased members, convivial dining, and cultic ritual. Finley read this as evidence collegia had no real economic function; Bang follows Waltzing and van Nijf in rejecting that inference — absence of formal monopoly power did not mean absence of economic utility, it meant the economic function operated through different channels (community-building, credit access, dispute mediation). The comparison to Mughal merchant "castes" is instructive: these too were loose, non-hereditary-in-practice conglomerates of local sub-groups rather than rigid guild monopolies, yet still structured commercial life. ^[inferred]
- **The imperial state was structurally ambivalent toward associations.** Rome tolerated collegia "outside the law" as long as they stayed quiet — Trajan's comment to Pliny that "whatever name we give them... if people assemble for a common purpose they soon turn into brotherhoods" led him to deny Nicomedia a fire brigade for fear of setting a precedent, even after a request from Juliopolis for military protection against marauding troops was separately refused on identical grounds (the state's unwillingness, not just inability, to intervene). The jurist Marcianus (Dig. 47.22) permitted poor citizens to form burial societies without authorization only if they met no more than once a month; in practice, enforcement was looser than the law implied. A documented case shows an Alexandrian agitator, Isidorus, mobilizing several confraternities against the Roman governor Flaccus — exactly the mobilization risk the state feared.
- **A minority of collegia acquired quasi-corporate status through state service**, not commercial success — e.g. Egyptian merchant groups who bought the right to administer the state salt-trade monopoly, builders' associations converted into municipal fire brigades, and the *corpora naviculariorum* (shippers supplying Rome's grain *annona*), which the state eventually converted into a corporation of landowners obligated to supply ships — a transformation so binding that St. Augustine is recorded hesitating over whether to accept an inheritance tied to this obligation.
- **Patronage was the literal institutional expression of clientelization.** Collegia routinely elected patrons from the local or imperial political elite, who could intervene directly in commercial disputes, offer protection in social conflicts, and "soften the attitude of the authorities" — documented via inscriptions naming equestrian patrons of shipper and wine-merchant guilds, and via the Ostia material showing patrons actively defending collegia interests. In return, collegia could be mobilized to support their patrons' political contests. This is the mechanism by which a merchant's "clientelized" relationships converted into usable leverage against predatory officials, not just a vague social nicety.
- **Collegia mimicked city-state structure and language.** Professional associations were organized with their own popular assembly and magistrates, and members commonly addressed each other as "friends" or even "brothers" — a replication of civic form inside the trading world, used to disseminate norms of proper conduct (one ordinance bans raising grievances against fellow members during communal dinners, reserving disputes for the monthly assembly instead) and to punish failures with fines or expulsion.
- **Internal mediation was preferred to the official courts.** An ordinance from an association in Tebtunis (Roman Egypt, reign of Tiberius) fines any member who sues a fellow member rather than seeking internal resolution — paralleling Mughal caste councils, which served as judicial bodies of first instance before disputes ever reached a Mughal court. Roman state courts had weak enforcement tools of their own (self-help by the winning party was often required to make a verdict stick), making the "closest community's" backing nearly indispensable to a litigant regardless of venue.
- **Private arbitration ran on social capital: the *vir bonus* mediator.** Roman jurists discuss the *arbiter* in terms of the *vir bonus* ("good man") standard — parties agreed on a person of recognized standing within their community to mediate. The Sulpicii banking archive from Puteoli (wax tablets, *TPSulp.*) documents a real case: a dispute between banker C. Sulpicius Cinnamus and his associate C. Julius Prudens was formally filed before a judge in AD 55, then the parties agreed to transfer it to private arbitration before Marcus Barbatius Epaphroditus — a member of the same freedman business circle — with the case still unresolved over a year later, implying slow, negotiated progress rather than binding closure.
- **Filing a lawsuit was often a bargaining tactic, not an endpoint.** Both the Sulpicii dispute and the separate Babatha archive (a Jewish woman's inheritance dispute) show litigants filing to pressure a reluctant party into private settlement negotiations, rather than pursuing the suit to verdict — courts functioned as leverage within an ongoing mediation process as much as a venue for final judgment.
- **Courts were not neutral arbiters of abstract right — they functioned as "societal justice."** Ancient legal practice expected courts to weigh litigants' social standing and the standing of their witnesses; lawyers were expected to argue status and influence explicitly. Judges, dependent on the active cooperation of local communities to enforce rulings, tended to reinforce the existing distribution of local power — but Bang stresses this was not an absolute "done deal": occasional upsets were possible, which is precisely what gave the threat of litigation real bargaining value even for weaker parties.
- **Reputation functioned as a tradeable legal asset.** Witnesses "of high reputation in the bazaar" carried disproportionate weight in disputes; people of low standing struggled to secure such witnesses for their side. This "symbolic capital," built through participation in collegial and civic ritual life, directly fed back into the practical capacity to win a legal dispute.

## Open Questions

- Bang's own evidence base for collegia's *judicial* role (as opposed to their social/convivial role) is thin — he explicitly notes that the very few surviving collegial ordinances leave their conflict-resolution function "very much in the dark," so the Tebtunis and *TPSulp.* examples used here are best treated as illustrative rather than representative of a formally codified system. ^[ambiguous]
- Whether the comparative strength of Greco-Roman civic court structures (compared to Mughal India's weaker urban corporate articulation) meant Roman merchants relied relatively *less* on internal collegial jurisdiction than their Mughal counterparts is suggested by Bang but not conclusively demonstrated.
