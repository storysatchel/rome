# cities/municipia

Roman cities and municipia were urban communities in which local elites carried out administration and displayed their standing through houses as well as public activities. Domestic archaeology reveals aspects of this municipal society, although elite residences cannot stand for the whole urban population.

## Houses within an urban plan

A town house could benefit from access to an urban water supply, while the street system and assigned plots limited its expansion. At planned Timgad, such constraints help explain why some especially large and lavish houses stood outside the city’s grid.

Owners and residents could include members of the local elite involved in administering their native *civitates* and *municipia*, as well as wealthy Roman officials. The source considers the first group probably more numerous. Their position as patrons of local clients and clients of higher-ranking Romans affected the social requirements of their houses.

## An uneven archaeological record

Excavation has often concentrated on districts with elite residences. At many North African sites, early excavations also failed to collect stratigraphic evidence. The resulting record favours impressive houses while leaving building sequences and the dwellings of lower-status inhabitants less well understood.

## Municipal elites and cultural choices

The housing evidence also bears on how Roman municipal societies formed. De Haan’s discussion uses a model in which imperial administration relied heavily on local elites operating through urban institutions. Identification with Roman practices could reinforce those elites’ standing within their own communities.

Cicero’s formulation joins the usefulness of a house with convenience and *dignitas*, or social distinction. Domestic design could therefore both serve and display an owner’s position. Yet the adoption of a form conventionally labelled Roman cannot by itself measure submission to Rome, just as a local form cannot automatically prove resistance. The chapter treats cultural exchange and identity as variable, making houses evidence for negotiation rather than a simple scale of Romanization.
