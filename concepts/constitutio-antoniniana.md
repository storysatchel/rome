# Constitutio Antoniniana

The *Constitutio Antoniniana* was Caracalla’s collective grant of Roman citizenship in 212 CE. The source describes its beneficiaries cautiously as “all—or many” of the peoples within the empire. Its effect should not be simplified into the disappearance of every intermediate legal status.

## Citizenship and different forms of Latinity

Coloniary Latinity did not survive the grant, whereas Junian Latinity, associated with manumission, continued until Justinian’s time. The distinction matters for claims about marriage rights: extending *conubium* to freedperson families would not automatically include every Junian Latin unless they too had received full citizenship.

## Why full citizenship mattered to freedpeople

For Junian Latins, full citizenship brought important inheritance advantages. It offered the ability to make and take under wills and reduced the patron’s control over the freedperson’s eventual estate. Earlier routes to citizenship included qualifying service in the *vigiles*, specified contributions to grain transport or milling, and a procedure involving marriage before witnesses followed by a year-old child.

These routes reveal the stakes behind the distinction between Latin status and Roman citizenship. Patrons could also grant an upgrade through a second, formal manumission, *iteratio*. The survival of Junian status after 212 therefore preserved a relationship of dependence that a sweeping account of universal citizenship would miss.
