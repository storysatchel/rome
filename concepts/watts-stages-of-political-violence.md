# Watts's Staged Timeline of Political Violence

The parent page records Watts's one-sentence summary of his escalation thesis — murder, then intimidation, then civil war, then autocracy, each stage "a generation" apart. The underlying text gives this summary specific dated anchors and a chapter-by-chapter structure that the summary compresses away.

## Key Ideas

- **Stage 1 — the first political killing (133 BC).** Watts anchors the opening threshold precisely: the mob killing of Tiberius Gracchus (tribune 133 BC, son of [[entities/tiberius-sempronius-gracchus-cos-177-163|Ti. Sempronius Gracchus, cos. 177/163]]) and 200–300 of his supporters on the Capitoline, which Watts quotes Plutarch calling the "first outbreak of civil strife in Rome that resulted in the bloodshed and murder of citizens since the expulsion of the kings." Watts treats 133 BC as a hinge precisely because contemporaries (per Cicero, writing a lifetime later) recognized in retrospect that "the whole manner of his tribunate divided one people into two factions" — i.e., the killing, not just the land law, is what broke precedent.
- **Stage 2 — armed intimidation of assemblies and elections ("within a generation").** Watts dates this threshold to the 120s–90s BC: Gaius Gracchus's death under the first [[concepts/senatus-consultum-ultimum|senatus consultum ultimum]] (121 BC), then [[entities/lucius-appuleius-saturninus|Saturninus]] and Glaucia's armed gangs contesting the consular elections of 100 BC. Watts's own chapter titles mark this arc: "The Politics of Frustration" and "The Rise of the Outsider" cover the Gracchi-to-Marius period in which violence shifts from a single mob action to organized, recurring intimidation of voting.
- **Stage 3 — civil war ("within two generations").** Watts's chapter "The Republic Breaks" covers Publius Sulpicius Rufus's 88 BC recruitment of roughly 3,000 armed supporters to force through Marius's command against Mithridates, followed immediately by Sulla's unprecedented march of his legions on Rome itself that same year — the first time, per Watts, a sitting Roman commander turned his army against the city to reverse a domestic political defeat. This cascades through the Marius–Cinna reprisals of the mid-80s, Sulla's return and proscriptions (82–81 BC), and recurs with Spartacus's revolt (73–71 BC) and ultimately Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon and civil war against Pompey (49–45 BC) — Watts's chapters "Rebuilding amid the Wreckage," "The Republic of the Mediocre," "Stumbling Toward Dictatorship," and "The Birth and Death of Caesar's Republic."
- **Stage 4 — autocracy ("two generations after that").** Watts's final chapters, "The Republic of Octavian" and "Choosing Augustan Liberty," cover the post-assassination proscriptions of 43 BC under the second triumvirate, the battle of Philippi (42 BC), the defeat of Antony and Cleopatra at Actium (31 BC), and Octavian's assumption of the name Augustus (27 BC) — the point at which, per Watts, the citizenry's accumulated exhaustion with unpunished disorder converted into active demand for one-man rule rather than passive acceptance of it.
- Watts frames his own opening chapter ("Autocratic Freedom," set in 22–19 BC) as evidence that stage 4 was not a single clean transition: he cites a grain-shortage riot under Augustus during which the Roman population demanded Augustus accept the title of dictator outright, using Cassius Dio's remark that order held "because they were afraid" to argue that even fully "autocratic" Rome periodically re-litigated how much self-rule to surrender.
- The four-stage structure is Watts's own rhetorical device (stated explicitly in the passage the parent page already quotes) rather than a neutral periodization scheme independently verified by other sources in this batch — Mommsen, for instance, periodizes the same span around the dictatorship's institutional lapse (217 BC) rather than Watts's generational count from 133 BC. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- Watts's "generation" intervals (133→~100s→~88-44→27 BC) are rhetorically clean but not uniform in actual years (roughly 33, then up to 44, then 17–23 years), raising the question of whether "generation" functions as a literal demographic unit or a narrative device in his argument; the sampled text does not resolve this explicitly. ^[ambiguous]
- Whether Watts's stage-3 threshold is better dated to Sulpicius's private army (88 BC, domestic intimidation escalating to organized force) or to Sulla's march on Rome (88 BC, the same year, but military force entering the city) is not disambiguated in his own narrative, which treats them as two beats of one crisis. ^[ambiguous]
