# The Colossus of Memnon Snubs Hadrian but Speaks for Sabina

The "[[entities/colossus-of-memnon|Colossus of Memnon]]," a pair of massive seated statues of Amenhotep III at [[entities/thebes-egypt|Thebes]] in [[entities/egypt|Egypt]], had become one of the ancient world's premier tourist attractions after one of the statues developed a crack (following an earthquake) that caused it to emit a strange sound at dawn, popularly believed to be the legendary hero Memnon greeting his mother, the dawn goddess Eos. Visitors from across the empire traveled to hear it and left inscriptions recording their experience. When the emperor Hadrian visited with his wife Vibia Sabina and an entourage including the poetess Julia Balbilla in AD 130, the statue reportedly spoke for Sabina but not — on the day in question — for Hadrian himself.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Hadrian** ([[entities/hadrian]]) — the reigning emperor, touring Egypt as part of an extensive empire-wide journey; stakes: his personal and imperial prestige in a conspicuously public religious/touristic ritual.
- **Vibia Sabina** ([[entities/vibia-sabina]]) — Hadrian's wife; her own inscription records that "during the first hour she twice heard Memnon," a claim of a successful, auspicious encounter with the oracle.
- **Julia Balbilla** ([[entities/julia-balbilla]]) — a poetess accompanying the imperial entourage, whose surviving verse graffiti obliquely records that Memnon "had the bad taste to fail to perform" for Hadrian.

## What Happened

By Hadrian's reign the Colossus of Memnon had already drawn visitors for over a century, including several governors of Egypt and army officers, who left inscriptions (some in careful verse, in the style of Homer) commemorating having "heard Memnon" at dawn. During Hadrian's state visit in AD 130, his wife Sabina left an inscription stating she heard Memnon speak twice in the first hour after sunrise — an auspicious, successful result. Hadrian himself left no such inscription recording a successful hearing; the accompanying poetess Julia Balbilla's verses can be read as diplomatically implying that the statue failed to sound for the emperor on this occasion. Hadrian's reign nonetheless marked the high point of visits to the site — multiple governors, district officials, and self-styled poets left inscriptions during his time in Egypt — and after his visit, imperial visits and the graffiti tradition tailed off, with the last dated inscription around AD 205, shortly before Septimius Severus had the statue restored (after which it reportedly stopped "speaking" altogether).

## Outcome

Whatever the religious or psychological significance read into the incident by contemporaries, no inscription survives in which Hadrian himself claims success, in pointed contrast to his wife's recorded triumph. The episode illustrates both the degree to which Roman elites, up to and including the emperor, participated in popular religious tourism, and the awkward possibility of public "failure" even for an emperor at a site believed to reveal favor or disfavor from the gods.

## Open Questions

- Whether Hadrian made a second attempt, or whether the "snub" was simply bad luck with the statue's temperature-dependent acoustic mechanism (the sound was likely caused by expanding morning dew/heat in the statue's cracked stone), is unknown.
- Julia Balbilla's verses are allusive rather than explicit about Hadrian's failure, leaving the precise reading somewhat a matter of interpretation.^[ambiguous]
