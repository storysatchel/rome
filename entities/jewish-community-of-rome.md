# Jewish Community of Rome

The Jewish community of ancient Rome was the largest and oldest continuous Jewish settlement in the western Mediterranean. Beginning as diplomatic delegations under the Hasmonaeans in the second century BCE, the community expanded rapidly following Pompey’s eastern campaigns in 63 BCE. Residing primarily in the working-class quarter of *Trans Tiberim* (modern Trastevere) and around the Porta Capena and Subura, Roman Jews maintained a network of autonomous synagogues (*synagogai* or *proseuchai*), enjoyed distinct legal privileges (*religio licita*) established by Julius Caesar and Augustus, and navigated periodic political expulsions and the fiscal burden of the *Fiscus Iudaicus* following the First Jewish War.

## Origins and Demography

Diplomatic relations between Rome and Judaea commenced in 161 BCE when Judas Maccabaeus dispatched an embassy to the Roman Senate to conclude a treaty of mutual friendship (1 Maccabees 8; Josephus, *Ant.* 12.414–419). Further embassies followed under Jonathan (143 BCE) and Simon (139 BCE). Valerius Maximus (1.3.3) notes an early expulsion of Jews in 139 BCE by the praetor peregrinus Cornelius Hispalus for attempting to introduce foreign cult practices (*Sabazios* / *Sabbath*) to Roman citizens.

The substantial permanent Jewish population in Rome dates from 63 BCE, when [[entities/pompey|Pompey]] captured Jerusalem, ended the Hasmonaean civil war, and brought thousands of Jewish captives back to Rome to march in his 61 BCE triumph. Most of these captives were subsequently purchased by Roman masters, manumitted, and enrolled as Roman citizens with freedman status (*liberti*).

By the late Republic and Augustan period, the Jewish population of Rome is estimated at between 30,000 and 50,000 individuals, representing roughly 4 to 6 percent of the metropolitan population. When a Jewish delegation arrived from Judaea in 4 BCE to petition Augustus following the death of Herod the Great, Josephus (*Ant.* 17.300) records that over eight thousand resident Roman Jews accompanied the envoys to the Temple of Apollo on the Palatine.

## Legal Status: Ancestral Rights and Exemptions

Unlike many foreign religious groups that were subject to police bans against unauthorized associations (*collegia illicita*), the Jews of Rome were recognized as observing an ancient ancestral tradition (*mos maiorum gentium*), generally characterized in Roman jurisprudence as a recognized cult (*religio licita*):

1. **The Caesarian Charters:** In the 40s BCE, Julius Caesar issued a series of senatorial decrees and personal letters (preserved by Josephus, *Ant.* 14.190–216) rewarding Jewish loyalty during the Alexandrian war. Caesar granted Jews the unrestricted right to assemble, collect monetary contributions, observe Sabbath rest, and hold communal banquets. When the *Lex Iulia de collegiis* dissolved all political clubs and unauthorized associations, the Jewish community was explicitly exempted.
2. **Augustan Protections:** Augustus confirmed and expanded Caesar’s charters:
   - **Judicial Accommodations:** Jewish citizens could not be summoned into court on the Sabbath or on Friday afternoon after the ninth hour (*Ant.* 16.163).
   - **Grain Distributions:** If the monthly free public grain dole (*annona frumentaria*) fell on a Sabbath, distribution officials were legally required to reserve the Jewish recipient's share until the following Sunday (Philo, *Legatio ad Gaium* 158).
   - **Military Exemption:** Recognized exemption from legionary military conscription, since military service required bearing arms and traveling on the Sabbath and consuming non-kosher field rations.
   - **Temple Tax Transmission:** Permission to collect the annual half-shekel tithe (*sheqalim*) from all adult males and dispatch the sacred funds under official escort to the Temple in Jerusalem without seizure by provincial customs agents or Roman financial prefects.

## Synagogues and Organizational Structure

The Jewish community in Rome did not operate under a single unified municipal hierarchy or central rabbinical council (in contrast to the *ethnos* and ethnarch of Alexandria). Instead, it consisted of independent, self-governing local congregation cells (*synagogai* or *proseuchai*).

Epigraphic evidence from the Jewish catacombs of Rome—most notably the Monteverde Catacomb along the Via Portuensis, the Vigna Randanini on the Via Appia, and the Villa Torlonia catacomb on the Via Nomentana—records at least twelve distinct named synagogues:
- **`Synagoge Augustensium` (*Augustesioi*):** Likely named in honor of Augustus, who guaranteed their legal charters.
- **`Synagoge Agrippensium` (*Agrippesioi*):** Named in honor of Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa, a renowned personal patron and friend of Herod the Great.
- **`Synagoge Bolumnensium` (*Volumnesioi*):** Likely patronized by or named after Marcus Volumnius, a Roman procurator in Syria.
- **Topographical Synagogues:** Named after the neighborhoods where members lived, including the *Campenses* (Campus Martius), the *Suburenses* (Subura), and the *Elaias* (Olive Tree quarter).
- **`Synagoge Vernaclensium` (*Vernaclesioi*):** Composed of "vernaculars"—native-born Roman Jews who spoke Latin/Greek, distinguishing themselves from foreign-born immigrant captives.

Each synagogue was directed by:
- An **`Archisynagogos`:** The director responsible for religious services, Torah reading, and maintaining the sanctuary.
- A council of elders (**`Gerousia`**) presided over by a **`Gerousiarch`**.
- An **`Archon`:** An executive magistrate elected annually to manage finances, judicial arbitration, and external civic relations.
- A **`Pater Synagogae`** or **`Mater Synagogae`:** Honorary titles conferred upon prominent civic benefactors and donors who funded synagogue construction or oil distributions.

The primary language of the Roman Jewish inscriptions was Jewish Greek (roughly 75%), followed by Latin (roughly 24%), with occasional Hebrew formulas (such as *Shalom al Yisrael*) appearing toward late antiquity.

## Frictions, Expulsions, and the *Fiscus Iudaicus*

Despite their official legal protections, Roman Jews encountered recurrent frictions with conservative Roman magistrates:

### The Republican and Early Imperial Expulsions
- **Expulsion of 19 CE (Tiberius):** Following an incident in which four Jewish con artists swindled a noble Roman convert named Fulvia (wife of the senator Saturninus) out of purple textiles and gold intended for the Jerusalem Temple, Tiberius struck back. As reported by Tacitus (*Ann.* 2.85) and Suetonius (*Tib.* 36), the Senate conscripted four thousand freedmen-grade Jewish youths of military age and deployed them to Sardinia to suppress banditry (*"if they succumbed to the pestilential climate, it would be a cheap loss"*), while the rest of the community was ordered to destroy their religious vestments or leave Italy. The decree lapsed or was revoked following the fall of Sejanus in 31 CE.
- **Expulsion of 49 CE (Claudius):** Suetonius (*Claud.* 25.4) famously writes: *"Since the Jews constantly made disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus [Iudaeos impulsore Chresto assidue tumultuantes], he expelled them from Rome."* This notice—frequently linked to the arrival of early Christian missionaries preaching Jesus as Christ in the synagogues—resulted in temporary expulsions (including Aquila and Priscilla; Acts 18:2), though Cassius Dio (60.6.6) clarifies that because their numbers were so vast, Claudius could not easily drive them out without massive unrest, instead prohibiting their assemblies.

### The First Jewish War and the *Fiscus Iudaicus*
Following the destruction of the Second Temple by Titus in 70 CE, the Flavian dynasty fundamentally altered the fiscal and legal status of Jews throughout the Empire. Vespasian redirected the traditional half-shekel temple tax paid by all observant Jews into an imperial head tax: the *Fiscus Iudaicus*. Every Jewish male, female, and child aged three to sixty-two was required to pay two drachmas (denarii) annually directly into the temple treasury of *Jupiter Capitolinus*—a humiliating theological submission of Yahweh to the supreme god of Rome.

Under Domitian (81–96 CE), the collection of the *Fiscus Iudaicus* was enforced with draconian brutality. Imperial tax agents inspected circumcised individuals and targeted not only practicing Jews, but also those who lived "according to Jewish customs" without acknowledging their faith, and Roman aristocrats accused of "atheism" and Judaizing (such as Titus Flavius Clemens and Flavia Domitilla in 95 CE). Upon his accession in 96 CE, Nerva abolished the abusive denunciations, minting the famous commemorative coin inscribed *Fisci Iudaici calumnia sublata* ("The abusive extortion of the Jewish tax having been removed"), though the tax itself remained active until the third century.
