# The Post-Cannae Vestal Scandal: Opimia and Floronia (216 BC)

In 216 BC, after the Roman defeat at [[entities/cannae|Cannae]] and with Hannibal's army in Italy, two Vestals, Opimia and Floronia, were convicted of *incestum*. Theirs is the last Vestal case recorded between the founding of the Republic and the end of the Second Punic War, and the most discussed. It is the usual example cited for the view that Vestals were accused only in times of fear and upheaval, and for 216 that description fits.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Opimia** ([[entities/opimia-vestal-216-bc]]): a convicted Vestal. She died either by the customary burial alive at the [[entities/colline-gate|Colline Gate]] or by her own hand; no source says which.
- **Floronia** ([[entities/floronia-vestal-216-bc]]): a convicted Vestal, with the same uncertainty about her death. She is the one named as Cantilius's partner.
- **L. Cantilius** ([[entities/lucius-cantilius-pontifical-scribe]]): a *scriba pontificius*, "one of the pontifical scribes, whom they now call the lesser pontifices". He had committed the offence with Floronia and was beaten with rods in the [[entities/comitium|Comitium]] by the Pontifex Maximus until he died. He is one of the few named *scribae* from before the first century BC.
- **Q. Fabius Maximus**: in Livy's account, the senator who proposed keeping matrons off the streets and silencing public lamentation to restore order.

## What Happened

After Cannae, with hardly any magistrates left in the city, Rome filled with lamentation: the facts were not yet known, so households mourned the living and the dead alike. On Fabius's proposal the senators kept the matrons indoors and quieted the mourning. Mourning was later limited to one month, and the annual rites of Ceres were suspended because too few women were out of mourning to perform them. Later that year, when the senate debated ransoming the Cannae captives, women mingled with the men listening to the debate. When ransom was refused, they followed the messengers to the city gates, lamenting and protesting.

Among the prodigies of that year, the Vestals Opimia and Floronia were found guilty of unchastity. One was put to death under the earth at the Colline Gate, "as is the custom"; the other killed herself. Cantilius was flogged to death in the Comitium. In a Vestal case the customary penalty was burial alive in an underground chamber in the [[entities/campus-sceleratus|campus sceleratus]], and the lover was beaten to death.

The affair, which Livy calls a *nefas*, was treated as a prodigy requiring expiation:
- The senate had the *decemviri* consult the Sibylline Books.
- It sent Q. Fabius Pictor to Delphi to ask how the gods could be appeased.
- On the *decemviri*'s direction, a Gallic man and woman and a Greek man and woman were buried alive in the [[entities/forum-boarium|Forum Boarium]], a rite Livy calls hardly Roman.

Livy presents the *decemviri* as believing their expiation had worked and judges them mistaken, since the course of the war did not change. In 215 the lex Oppia restrained women's dress and behaviour.

## Interpretations

- **Religious crisis.** The Romans looked to their own conduct for the cause of the defeat. The Vestals' unchastity was one of the prodigies that had gone unrecognised and had disrupted the *pax deorum*, and the senate's measures were attempts to restore it.
- **Control of women.** Much of the turmoil in Rome came from women's public conduct: street mourning, attendance at the ransom debate, protest at the gates. On this reading, condemning two Vestals served as a symbolic correction of that conduct, as well as answering the city's fear. This is an interpretation of the setting, not evidence of what the Vestals did.

## Open Questions

- Which Vestal was buried and which killed herself. Livy's *altera … altera* does not say, and no work in the corpus assigns the deaths. ^[ambiguous]
- Whether any specific evidence implicated Opimia and Floronia. Livy records the convictions and Floronia's partner, but no accusation. ^[ambiguous]
