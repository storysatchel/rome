# Lugdunum Penalized for Backing Clodius Albinus, AD 197

The provincial capital of [[entities/gaul|Gaul]], Lugdunum (modern Lyon), backed the losing side in the civil war between Septimius Severus and the rival imperial claimant Clodius Albinus, governor of [[entities/britannia|Britain]]. The conflict culminated in a major battle fought at Lugdunum itself in AD 197, after which the city — having supported Albinus — was formally penalized by the victorious Severus.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Clodius Albinus** ([[entities/clodius-albinus]]) — Roman governor of Britain who had been proclaimed emperor by his troops and allied (initially) with Severus before the two fell out; stakes: his claim to the imperial throne, and the backing of Gallic provincial centers including Lugdunum.
- **Septimius Severus** ([[entities/septimius-severus]]) — rival claimant and eventual sole emperor; goal: eliminate Albinus as a rival and punish provincial centers that had supported him.
- **Lugdunum (Lyon)** ([[entities/lugdunum]]) — the provincial capital of Gallia Lugdunensis, whose local elite or administration evidently threw support behind Albinus; stakes: its civic standing and privileges within the empire.

## What Happened

Civil war broke out between the several claimants to the imperial throne following the assassination of Pertinax and the brief reign of Didius Julianus in AD 193. Clodius Albinus, commanding the legions of [[entities/britannia|Britain]], was initially recognized by Severus as a Caesar (junior colleague) to secure Severus's rear while he dealt with the eastern rival Pescennius Niger, but the alliance broke down once Niger was defeated. Albinus crossed to [[entities/gaul|Gaul]] and was backed by at least part of the Gallic provincial establishment, with Lugdunum emerging as a center of his support. The decisive battle of the war was fought at Lugdunum in AD 197, where Severus's forces defeated and killed Albinus.

## Outcome

Having supported the losing claimant, Lugdunum was penalized by Severus in the settlement that followed. The exact nature of the penalty (fines, loss of privileges, physical damage to the city) remains unspecified here. The episode marks the political and military instability beginning to affect Gaul in this period and can be understood as a prelude to the broader third-century crisis.^[ambiguous]

## Open Questions

- The precise form the "penalty" against Lugdunum took (financial, administrative, or physical destruction of the city) is not specified in the source and would require further research into Herodian or Cassius Dio's narrative of the Severan civil war.
