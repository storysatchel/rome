# Agrippina the Younger's Unprecedented Carpentum Privilege

Tacitus (*Annals* 12.42) records that Agrippina the Younger, wife of the emperor Claudius, was granted the right to ride up to the Capitol in a *carpentum* — the ceremonial carriage traditionally reserved for priests and sacred rites (*honos sacerdotibus et sacris antiquitus concessus*) — a privilege Tacitus frames as an alarming escalation of her personal power and a mark of Claudius's subjection to his wife. A related episode from the same period: when Claudius celebrated his British triumph in AD 44, his then-wife Messalina rode behind his chariot in a carpentum, a conspicuous public display of a wife sharing in a husband's triumphal honors (Suetonius, *Claudius* 17.3).

## Parties & Stakes

- **Agrippina the Younger** ([[entities/agrippina-the-younger]]) — goal: visibly elevate her status and that of her son (the future Nero) within the imperial household; resources: marriage to the reigning emperor, descent from Germanicus.
- **Claudius** ([[entities/claudius]]) — stakes: his public image as a traditionalist ruler respectful of Republican and religious forms, compromised by appearing to indulge his wife's ambitions.
- **Messalina** ([[entities/messalina]]) — a separate but comparable episode: rode in a carpentum behind Claudius's chariot during his AD 44 British triumph, an unusual public share in triumphal honor normally reserved for the triumphator alone.

## What Happened

Tacitus presents Agrippina's carpentum privilege as part of a broader pattern of her "raising her own eminence higher" (*suum quoque fastigium Agrippina extollere altius*), noting that the honor increased public veneration of her specifically because she was the daughter of a famous general (Germanicus) and sister, wife, and (soon) mother of emperors. The grant is treated by Tacitus as symptomatic of the erosion of normal constitutional and religious propriety under Claudius. Separately, Suetonius notes that during Claudius's earlier British triumph of AD 44, Messalina had ridden a carpentum following his chariot — itself an uncommon honor for an emperor's wife rather than a priest.^[extracted]

## Outcome

The episodes did not produce an immediate political crisis, but Tacitus uses them as evidence in his broader narrative of Claudius's household (and, by extension, the imperial office) being effectively governed by the ambitions of his wives — first Messalina, then Agrippina. Agrippina went on to secure the succession of her son Nero in AD 54, two years after this account.^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- Whether contemporaries in the 40s AD reacted to these honors with the alarm Tacitus retrospectively attributes to them, or whether this is colored by Tacitus's hostile portrait of Claudius's wives.
