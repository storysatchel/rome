# Catiline's Suspiciously Friendly Prosecutor (65 BC)

Two years before the conspiracy that made him infamous, L. Sergius Catilina stood trial for extorting the province of Africa during his governorship — and was acquitted in proceedings so collusive that Cicero himself, weighing whether to defend Catiline, described the prosecutor as acting with "the utmost goodwill toward the accused" (Alexander, case #212).

## Parties & Stakes

- **L. Sergius Catilina** ([[entities/lucius-sergius-catilina]]), pr. 68 — defendant, accused of extortion as governor of Africa (67–66 BC).
- **P. Clodius Pulcher** ([[entities/publius-clodius-pulcher]]), later aed. cur. 56 — prosecutor; his conduct of the case drew later suspicion of *praevaricatio* (collusive, deliberately weak prosecution).
- **L. Manlius Torquatus** (cos. 65) — appeared as a character witness for Catiline.
- The jury — split along class lines in its vote: senators voted to convict, while equites and *tribuni aerarii* voted to acquit.

## What Happened

Catiline's defense drew on African witnesses and a slate of consular character witnesses (*laudatores*), but the trial's most striking feature was the prosecutor's conduct. Cicero, in a letter written around the same time while weighing whether to defend Catiline himself (a defense he ultimately did not undertake), remarked that Clodius was prosecuting with conspicuous goodwill toward the man he was supposedly trying to convict — language modern scholars (and Alexander, citing Gruen's countervailing argument) have read as implying outright collusion, though Gruen argues the charge of *praevaricatio* against Clodius is not conclusively proven by the evidence.

## Outcome

Catiline was acquitted, with the jury split by social class (senators for conviction, equites and tribuni aerarii for acquittal) — itself a telling sign of how politically fraught extortion verdicts had become in this period. The acquittal cleared the way for Catiline's subsequent, ultimately unsuccessful runs for the consulship and the conspiracy that followed in 63 BC.

## Open Questions

- Whether Clodius's prosecution was genuinely collusive (*praevaricatio*) or merely incompetent/half-hearted is explicitly disputed between ancient implication (Cicero) and modern scholarly reassessment (Gruen), and Alexander does not resolve it. ^[ambiguous]
