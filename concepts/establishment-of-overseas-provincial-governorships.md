# Establishment of Overseas Provincial Governorships

[[concepts/roman-provincial-administration]]'s Open Questions flagged that none of its three sources traced how Rosenstein's informal mid-Republican "hegemony" over Italy became the formal provincial governorship system of the Late Republic. A closer read of *Rome and the Mediterranean* does supply part of that bridge — not as a single reform, but as a sequence of concrete institutional steps: new standing praetorships created specifically to govern annexed territory, a documented tax-administration scheme in Spain, a named extortion scandal and failed trial, and the 149 BC law that finally gave the system formal accountability.

## Key Ideas

- **Praetorian governors with permanent troops, unlike the Greek "liberation" model**: by 196–194 BC the senate had three live templates for controlling conquered territory — citizen/allied colonization, informal "hegemony" with no resident official, or "the dispatch of a praetorian governor, as in Sicily and Sardinia and now Spain," where (unusually) "troops accompanied the governor to enforce his will and secure Roman rule." When the senate evacuated Greece entirely in 194 BC rather than establish a fourth such governorship, Rosenstein attributes the choice explicitly to the political cost of creating yet another standing praetorship, since "each praetor hoped to advance to one of the two consulates" and more praetors meant intensified competition — a deliberate structural reason some conquered regions got governors and others (initially) did not.
- **The praetor count tracks the provinces directly**: per Rosenstein's cursus honorum table, praetors grew from one (to c. 242 BC) to four (to 197 BC) to six thereafter — the four-praetor stage corresponds to Sicily and Sardinia each receiving a resident governor, and the six-praetor stage to the division of Spain into two provinces (Hispania Citerior and Ulterior) in 197 BC. (See [[concepts/quaestorship-and-provincial-finance]] for how quaestor numbers rose in step with the same provincial expansion.)
- **A concrete named tax-administration system in Spain**: Ti. Sempronius Gracchus (the elder, father of the later tribunes — [[entities/tiberius-sempronius-gracchus-cos-177-163]]) put in place a settlement that required Spanish communities to supply grain to the Roman forces stationed there — the system functioning, in effect, as a standing provincial *stipendium*-in-kind levy administered by the governor rather than a Roman magistrate in Rome.
- **A documented governor extortion scheme and its failed prosecution (171 BC)**: Spanish delegations complained to the senate that several praetorian governors had been setting an arbitrarily high price on the grain Gracchus's system required, demanding payment in cash at that inflated price instead of the grain itself, then buying the grain they actually needed at the real market price and pocketing the difference — and additionally had taken to sending Roman officers to collect the money-tax directly from towns rather than letting local communities administer it themselves, "which invited abuse" (Livy 43.2.1–11). The senate did order trials, but structural conflicts of interest gutted them: provincials were barred from speaking for themselves and had to use senatorial patrons, the supervising praetor hand-picked an all-senatorial jury, one defendant was acquitted and the other two fled into voluntary exile to end proceedings, and the supervising praetor himself then abruptly departed for his own province, foreclosing further hearings. Rosenstein reads the episode as the senate directly choosing to protect its peers over its provincial subjects.
- **The 149 BC quaestio de rebus repetundis as the system's first formal accountability mechanism**: the trigger was a separate, more extreme atrocity — the propraetor Ser. Sulpicius Galba's 150 BC massacre and enslavement of Lusitanians who had surrendered to him under a promise of resettlement, which "outraged public opinion at Rome" and nearly produced his personal prosecution. The fallout led the senate to back legislation creating the Republic's first permanent criminal court, the *quaestio de rebus repetundis* (extortion court), whose purview was explicitly "abuse of magisterial power, particularly in the provinces and particularly in money matters." Rosenstein is clear that this was not primarily provincial-subject protection: the senatorial jurors who staffed the court failed to convict a single defendant in its first twenty-eight years. He reads the law instead as an attempt by the senate to assert control over its own governors — using the threat of post-tenure prosecution to deter commanders from ignoring senatorial instructions or provoking the kind of provincial unrest that could escalate into open revolt.
- **Timing bridges the mid-Republic and the system the Late Republic inherited**: the sequence — standing praetorian governors with garrisons (by 227–197 BC) → documented in-kind tax administration (Gracchus's Spanish settlement) → attested governor abuse of that system (171 BC) → a permanent, if toothless, accountability court (149 BC) — gives a concrete institutional chain running from Rosenstein's "hegemony" framing toward the extortion-prone, senatorially-supervised provincial governorships that dominate Late Republican politics (Verres's Sicily being the best-documented later case; see [[scenarios/verres-quaestorial-embezzlement-under-carbo]]).

## Open Questions

- Rosenstein's account explains *why* standing governorships appeared for Sicily, Sardinia, and Spain specifically and not (initially) Greece, but does not explain the later, further step of Greece and Macedonia eventually being converted to provinces themselves (146 BC) — that falls outside this book's 290–146 BC window at its very close.
- The 149 BC *quaestio de rebus repetundis*'s total failure to convict for 28 years means it functioned as a formal institutional step without functioning as effective provincial-subject protection; whether later reforms (e.g. the Gracchan-era jury reforms) changed this is not addressed in the sampled portion of this source. ^[ambiguous]
