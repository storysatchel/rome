# The Dilectus Refusal of 275 BC

After Pyrrhus of Epirus returned to southern Italy following his Sicilian campaign, the consul M'. Curius Dentatus held a levy (*dilectus*) to raise troops. Roman enthusiasm for the war had collapsed after the costly "Pyrrhic" victories at Heraclea (280 BC) and Ausculum (279 BC); when Curius called up the first citizen required to report, that man refused to respond. Curius declared the man's property forfeit as a penalty — and, according to the (possibly embellished) tradition, sold him into slavery outright when he tried to contest the ruling.

## Parties & Stakes

- **M'. Curius Dentatus** ([[entities/manius-curius-dentatus]]) — goal: fill the legions against a still-dangerous Pyrrhus despite collapsed popular enthusiasm for the war; resources: consular authority to hold the levy and impose penalties on non-compliant citizens, plus his own reputation as a proven, popular general from his 290 BC victories over the Sabines and Samnites.
- **The unnamed draft-dodging citizen** — goal: avoid conscription into what looked like "another forlorn hope" after Rome's run of defeats; resources: his civic status and property, both of which he lost in the confrontation.

## What Happened

Pyrrhus's 280–279 BC victories at Heraclea and Ausculum, won by his professional Hellenistic army against Roman levies, had badly shaken Roman confidence — the senate reportedly even considered a peace treaty, averted only by the hardline stance of Appius Claudius Caecus. When Pyrrhus departed for Sicily and then returned to Italy, Roman "fighting spirit evidently collapsed." Consul M'. Curius Dentatus's attempt to hold the dilectus for 275 met open resistance: the first citizen called up for enrollment refused to respond. Curius declared the man's property forfeit as punishment. Rather than "toeing the line," the man tried to challenge this verdict — and Curius responded by selling him into slavery. The source flags this harsh reported action as possibly embellished in transmission, but treats the underlying reluctance to serve as real and widespread. ^[ambiguous]

The broader recruitment crisis was severe enough that, for the first time, the state armed the *capite censi* — propertyless citizens normally excluded from legionary service — presumably because too few *assidui* (property-owning citizens liable for the regular levy) were presenting themselves. Curius's own political capital, built on his earlier victories and his practice of distributing conquered land to his soldiers, is credited with ultimately overcoming the recruitment crisis.

## Outcome

The specific draft-dodger lost his property and his freedom — an exemplary, deliberately public punishment meant to deter further refusals. More broadly, Curius's combination of harsh enforcement, personal popularity, and extraordinary measures (arming the *capite censi*, with the associated increase in *tributum* cost spread across the whole citizen body) successfully refilled the legions for the final phase of the Pyrrhic War. The arming of the poor also had a secondary political effect: it promoted some poorer citizens into the middle classes of the *comitia centuriata*, altering the composition of that assembly going forward. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- The individual's name is not preserved in the sources; it is unknown whether "selling into slavery" meant enslavement within Roman territory or deportation/sale abroad.
- The reported severity of the punishment (property forfeiture escalating to enslavement) may be a later rhetorical exaggeration used to illustrate Curius's severity as a moral exemplum, rather than a precisely transmitted legal outcome. ^[ambiguous]
