# Lex Licinia de Modo Agrorum (367 BC)

The *Lex Licinia de modo agrorum* is the first Roman *lex de modo agrorum* ("law on the amount of land") ancient sources know of — part of the Licinian-Sextian Rogations carried by the tribunes C. Licinius Stolo and L. Sextius Lateranus in 367 BC, traditionally capping an individual's landholding at 500 *iugera* (125 hectares) plus limits of 100 large and 500 small grazing animals. Roselaar's monograph (see [[references/roselaar-public-land-roman-republic]]) devotes an entire chapter section to reconstructing the law from fragmentary, contradictory, and much-later source testimony, since virtually every element of the traditional account — its date, its content, and even whether it concerned public land, private land, or both — is disputed in the modern scholarship she surveys.

## Key Ideas

- **The sources are nearly unanimous on the number but not the date.** Livy, Valerius Maximus, Pliny, Columella, *De viris illustribus*, Varro, Cato (via Gellius), Velleius Paterculus, and Plutarch's *Camillus* all cite the 500-*iugera* figure, but only Livy explicitly dates it to 367 BC; a long scholarly tradition (Niese, Tibiletti, Beloch) has argued the figure is anachronistic for the fourth century, since the Ager Romanus at the time (c. 700,000 *iugera* after accounting for unusable land) could barely have supplied 300 senators with 500 *iugera* each, let alone land for Appian's stated 600 animals per holder (which alone would require ~1,800 *iugera*). ^[extracted]
- **Roselaar's own resolution of the dating problem:** she rejects both the orthodox 367 BC date (unmodified) and the common alternative of a late-third/early-second-century origin, pointing out that no ancient source records any agrarian law being passed between 367 and the Gracchi — an implausible silence for "the one law which introduced the 500-iugera limit" — while independently corroborating that the limit was already in force by 167 BC, since Cato cites it in his *Pro Rhodiensibus* speech (preserved in Gellius, *NA* 6.3.37) and Livy records a lawsuit over excess land-holding as early as 298 BC (Livy 10.13.14). Her conclusion: the law is genuinely from 367 BC, and the second-century attribution theories arise from scholars trying to reconcile Appian's and Plutarch's Gracchan-era framing with the 500-*iugera* figure rather than from any positive evidence of a later law. ^[extracted]
- **The scope debate — Roselaar's "both public and private" thesis:** the dominant 19th/20th-century view (Niebuhr onward) held the law capped only *ager publicus*; Rathbone has recently revived the older, pre-Niebuhr reading that it capped only *private* land, using Cato's 167 BC reference (addressed to a non-Gracchan audience with no reason to assume public-land context) as his key evidence. Roselaar argues for a third position, following a largely-discarded 1835 theory of Huschke later revived by Rich: the law capped the *combined* total of public and private land held by one person, at a time (the 4th century BC) when no individual yet owned 500 *iugera* of private land outright — explaining both why the limit was workable in 367 and why it had become irrelevant (and ignorable) by 167, once elite private holdings alone routinely exceeded it. ^[extracted]
- **The *habere* / *possidere* terminology dead end:** Niebuhr's case for a public-land-only reading leaned on Livy's use of *possidere* rather than *habere*, on the theory that *possessio* was a technical term invented specifically for occupying *ager publicus*. Roselaar shows from Imperial jurists (Ulpian, Paulus, Macer) that both verbs were used interchangeably of full ownership and of lesser legal holds throughout Roman legal history, and that the terminological argument therefore cannot settle the scope question either way. ^[extracted]
- **Relationship to the Gracchan law:** Roselaar argues Tiberius Gracchus deliberately presented the *lex Sempronia agraria* of 133 BC as a revival of the Licinian limit (per Velleius Paterculus 2.6.3) to minimize the appearance of innovation, even though his own law — covering *ager publicus* only, granting 500 *iugera* plus 250 per child, and offering secure tenure the earlier law never had — was considerably more generous than the law of 367 as she reconstructs it. See [[entities/lex-sempronia-agraria]] for the later law's own provisions and commission. ^[inferred]
- Astin's biography of Cato independently registers the same scholarly uncertainty from the other direction: he notes that "earlier restrictions were modified" on the use of public land around the early second century BC without being able to date or source a specific law, and that the precise origin of the restrictions Cato invokes in 167 BC "is disputed in the scholarship." Astin's agnosticism corroborates Roselaar's starting observation that the 500-*iugera* limit's legislative history is a genuine scholarly crux, not a settled fact obscured only by one author's idiosyncratic reading. ^[extracted]

## Open Questions

- Roselaar's "both public and private land" reading is her own novel synthesis of a minority 19th-century position; it directly contradicts the majority view assumed by Astin and by most sources Astin cites, and this page does not adjudicate between them — flagged as a live disagreement rather than resolved. ^[ambiguous]
- Whether the number-of-animals clause (100 large / 500 small beasts) attributed to the law by Appian is original to 367 BC at all remains unresolved even within Roselaar's own account: she accepts the 500-*iugera* arable limit as genuinely fourth-century but does not commit to a date for the livestock clause, which would have required land far beyond what Rome possessed in 367 BC.
