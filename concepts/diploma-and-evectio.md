# Diploma and Evectio

In Roman administrative law, *diplomata* and *evectiones* were official travel credentials and requisition warrants issued under imperial authority to authorize access to the state transport and postal network (*vehiculatio*, later *cursus publicus*). Because state infrastructure, livestock, and lodging were provided through compulsory provincial levies (*angaria*), access was legally restricted to magistrates, military commanders, and official messengers carrying valid credentials.

## Terminology and Physical Forms

The legal terminology governing travel credentials evolved across the Principate and late antiquity:

- **Diploma (δίπλωμα)**:
  - Derived from the Greek verb meaning "to fold over" or "double."
  - Originally designated a pair of hinged, folding bronze or wooden tablets tied with cords and sealed with an official signet.
  - In postal administration, the *diploma* functioned as the primary passport and vehicle requisition permit under the early and high Principate, bearing the emperor's seal and personal authorization.
  - *Distinction*: Postal travel *diplomata* must be distinguished from military discharge diplomas (*diplomata militaria*), which were certified bronze tablets issued to auxiliary soldiers and praetorians confirming Roman citizenship and *conubium* (legal right of marriage) after twenty-five years of service.
- **Evectio**:
  - The standard statutory term in fourth- and fifth-century Roman law for a postal travel warrant.
  - Formally authorized the bearer to requisition draft animals, wagons, and grooms at designated roadside stations (*mutationes* and *mansiones*).
- **Tractoria**:
  - An expanded late antique travel warrant that authorized not only transport animals and vehicles, but also daily subsistence rations (*annona*) and lodging at state *mansiones*.
  - Issued to high-ranking provincial governors, ambassadors, and high ecclesiastical delegations traveling to imperial church councils.

## Issuing Authority and Administrative Control

Control over travel warrants was an imperial prerogative:

- **Imperial monopoly**: Ultimate authority to grant warrants belonged to the emperor. Under Augustus and the Julio-Claudians, every *diploma* required imperial validation.
- **Delegated quotas to governors**: To facilitate provincial governance, emperors issued fixed quotas of blank, pre-signed *diplomata* to provincial governors (*legati Augusti pro praetore*, *proconsules*) and praetorian prefects. Governors were permitted to issue these only for urgent public administrative business within their designated jurisdictions.
- **Expiration and route restrictions**: Every warrant specified:
  - The name and official capacity of the authorized traveler.
  - The exact permitted route (origin, transit highways, and destination).
  - The authorized vehicle type (such as a *rheda*, *carpentum*, or *cisium*).
  - The maximum contingent of animals (horses, mules, donkeys, or oxen).
  - A strict expiration date, after which the credential became void.
- **Pliny the Younger and Trajan (*Ep.* 10.120–121)**: The administrative sensitivity surrounding *diplomata* is illustrated by Pliny the Younger during his governorship of Bithynia-Pontus. Following the death of his wife's grandfather, Pliny issued an official *diploma* to his wife so she could travel rapidly to her grieving aunt in Italy. Pliny wrote immediately to Trajan to apologize for using state transport for private family reasons and to request retroactive confirmation. Trajan approved the exception on compassionate grounds, but reiterated the fundamental rule that warrants were not to be granted for private convenience.

## Statutory Allowances and Weight Regulations

In late antiquity, the Theodosian Code (Book 8, Title 5: *De cursu publico, angariis et parangariis*) codified comprehensive limits on postal vehicles and animal loads to prevent the destruction of draft stock and highway infrastructure:

- **Vehicle load limits**:
  - *Rheda* (four-wheeled travel carriage): Capped at 1,000 Roman pounds (*pondo*, roughly 327 kg).
  - *Angaria* (heavy four-wheeled freight wagon): Capped at 1,500 Roman pounds (*pondo*, roughly 490 kg).
  - *Carrus* (two-wheeled cart): Capped at 600 Roman pounds (*pondo*, roughly 196 kg).
- **Animal allocations**:
  - Standard travel warrants typically permitted four mules for a *rheda*, though in winter up to ten animals were temporarily permitted in rough weather.
  - In 382 AD, a provincial vicar (*vicarius*) was legally restricted to thirty donkeys and ten horses (CTh 8.5.38).
- **Passenger restrictions**: Imperial constitutions barred unauthorized entourages: a *carpentum* or *rheda* was limited to two or three persons, identified as the official traveler and security escorts (*custodes*).

## Fraud, Usurpation, and Legal Penalties

Because *diplomata* provided free travel, lodging, and draft animals at provincial expense, the forgery and usurpation of warrants was a chronic administrative problem:

- **Penalties for unauthorized travel**: Traveling via the state post without a warrant was penalized as public theft and usurpation of imperial majesty. The future emperor Pertinax, as a young equestrian officer commanding a cohort in Syria, utilized the postal service without an official *diploma*. The Syrian governor severely reprimanded him and forced him to walk across the province on foot as a public humiliation (*HA Pert.* 1.6).
- **Legislative suppression of warrant proliferation**: In the fourth century, emperors repeatedly enacted constitutions punishing provincial governors who issued *evectiones* to private friends, landowners, or unauthorized clients. Under Constantius II and Julian, governors who exceeded their annual warrant quotas were subjected to heavy fines, while the travelers were stripped of their carriages.
- **Inspection by the Agentes in Rebus**: The central imperial court deployed *agentes in rebus*—civil intelligence officers operating under the *magister officiorum*—to inspect *mansiones*, audit station registers, examine traveler credentials, and confiscate forged or expired warrants at river crossings and city gates.
