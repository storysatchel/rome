# Marius's Narrow Acquittal on Electoral Bribery Charges (116 BC)

Before he became the famous reformer of the Roman army and seven-time consul, Gaius Marius struggled to climb the *cursus honorum*. He failed in his bid for the aedileship, and in 116 BC barely won election as praetor — a victory that was immediately contested in court.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Gaius Marius** ([[entities/marius]]) — goal: secure and retain the praetorship against the bribery charge; resources: his narrow electoral victory, his legal defense.
- **The unnamed prosecutor(s)** — goal: void Marius's election by proving electoral corruption; resources: ambitus statute, evidence of alleged bribery.

## What Happened

Having barely won election as praetor for 116 BC, Marius was promptly charged with *ambitus* — illegal bribery of voters — a charge common enough in an era Marin describes as one where "the infamous bribery... seemingly characterized every election." Marius mounted a defense and secured an acquittal, though apparently by the narrowest of margins. ^[extracted] The episode came not long after Marius, as tribune of the plebs, had already alienated the powerful Metelli family by pushing through a law narrowing the passages used for voting, to prevent outside interference with voters at the polls — a reform the Senate had initially rejected before Marius took it directly to the people. ^[extracted]

## Outcome

Marius was acquitted and retained his praetorship. His political relationship with the Metelli, despite the earlier friction over his voting-reform law, was not permanently damaged: by 109 BC, Q. Caecilius Metellus took Marius on as his legate for the Jugurthine War — the campaign during which Marius would maneuver to replace Metellus in command (see [[scenarios/marius-gold-ring-sulla-seal-rivalry]]).
