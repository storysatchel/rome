# The Roman Assemblies from their Origin to the End of the Republic

George Willis Botsford, Professor of History at Columbia University, published *The Roman Assemblies from their Origin to the End of the Republic* with Macmillan in 1909. By his own account in the preface, it was "the first to offer in monographic form a detailed treatment of the popular assemblies of ancient Rome," written as a reaction against certain theories of Niebuhr and Mommsen concerning the *comitia*, and intended as "a book of study and reference" for institutional and comparative constitutional research rather than a polemic for one interpretive school.

## Core Argument / Thesis

Botsford sets out to follow "a conservative historical method as opposed to the radical juristic" approach — building generalizations up from the ancient evidence rather than fitting evidence to a preconceived legal theory of the constitution. The book traces the [[concepts/popular-assemblies-rome|Roman popular assemblies]] — the curiate, centuriate, and tribal *comitia* — from their roots in archaic clan society through their full republican development as electoral, legislative, and judicial bodies, and finally their decline into empty formality (in the case of the *comitia curiata*) or political irrelevance by the end of the Republic.

## Key Claims and Evidence

- The *populus Romanus* comprised patricians, plebeians, and clients from the earliest recoverable period; Botsford argues at length against the view that the primitive state was exclusively patrician, concluding "the Latin language gives no hint of an exclusively patrician populus."
- Ancient authors (Cicero, Livy, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Plutarch) uniformly derive the patriciate from Romulus's selection of a council of elders (the *patres*) who became the original [[concepts/roman-senate|senate]], with *patricii* denoting their descendants — though Botsford shows the etymological tradition deriving *patricius* from *patrem ciere* ("one who can cite a father") created lasting confusion by conflating the term with *ingenuus* (freeborn).
- The curiate assembly (*comitia curiata*), based on the 30 *curiae* subdividing the three archaic tribes (Tities, Ramnes, Luceres), is the oldest Roman voting body, predating the city's foundation in origin; by the Republic it survived chiefly to pass the *lex curiata de imperio* confirming the *imperium* of newly elected magistrates, along with certain adoption and testamentary formalities — eventually represented by 30 lictors rather than actual citizen attendance.
- Botsford's analysis of the *lex curiata de imperio* (building on his own 1908 article) surveys Cicero's *De lege agraria* extensively to argue that the centuries or tribes actually conferred *imperium* or *potestas* on magistrates, while the curiate assembly's role was merely to confirm or formally sanction a grant already made — a reading bearing directly on how the [[concepts/consulship|consulship]] derived its legal authority.
- The book distinguishes sharply between the *contio* (a meeting called for debate or announcement, with no formal decision) and the *comitia* proper (a voting assembly reaching a binding decision) — a terminological distinction later scholars (including Sandberg) treat as foundational.
- Drawing on comparative evidence from Homeric Greece, the Germanic tribes, the Celts, and the Slavs, Botsford situates the Roman assembly within a broader pattern of early political development in which assemblies originally ratified decisions of kings and councils rather than initiating policy, and majority rule was foreign to primitive practice. ^[inferred]
- The *comitia tributa* and *concilium plebis*, based on the citizenry's division into (eventually) 35 tribes, grew in political importance from 449 BC onward, with jurisdiction and legislative functions expanding markedly after the *lex Hortensia* of 287 BC gave plebiscites full force of law.

## Structure / Scope

The book is organized in two parts: Part I ("Elements of the Comitial Constitution," chapters I–V) covers the populus and its early divisions, the social composition of the primitive populus, the thirty-five tribes, the centuries and classes, and the auspices; Part II ("The Assemblies: Organization, Procedure, and Functions, Resolutions, Statutes, and Cases," chapters VI–XIX) covers *comitia* and *concilium*, the *contio*, the *comitia calata*, the *comitia curiata*, the organization and functions of the *comitia centuriata*, the rise of the *comitia tributa* (across two chapters spanning to 449 and then to 287 BC), its judicial functions, and comitial legislation in three successive chronological chapters (Hortensius to the Gracchi, the Gracchi to Sulla, Sulla to the end of the Republic), closing with comitial procedure/calendar and a historical summary. Evidence is drawn chiefly from Livy, Cicero, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, and the fragments of the annalists, supported by extensive comparative ethnographic and Indo-European philological scholarship (Mommsen, Niebuhr, Lange, Fustel de Coulanges, Willems) cited in detailed chapter bibliographies. ^[ambiguous] (thin direct sampling of the later chapters on comitial legislation, chs. ## Significance

Writing in 1909, Botsford positioned his work explicitly against Niebuhr's and Mommsen's constructions of the early Roman constitution, while still relying heavily on Mommsen's *Römisches Staatsrecht* as a reference point throughout. Kaj Sandberg's 2001 *Magistrates and Assemblies* cites Botsford's book as one of the few prior studies (alongside Guglielmo Nocera's 1940 work) to treat the popular assemblies as political institutions in their own right rather than subordinating them to prosopographical or Gelzerian analyses of aristocratic factions — evidence that Botsford's institutional framing remained a direct reference point for assembly scholarship nearly a century later.

## Open Questions

- Botsford's own treatment leaves open (and flags as genuinely contested in his sources) whether clients were, from the outset, simply a subset of plebeians or a formally distinct status; he judges the view that arrays clients against plebeians from early in the state's history as "historically better founded" than the alternative, without fully resolving it. ^[ambiguous]
- The precise constitutional mechanics distinguishing the *concilium plebis* from the *comitia tributa* are treated by Botsford and remained disputed into Sandberg's generation of scholarship, which devotes an entire subsection to re-litigating the question. ^[ambiguous]
