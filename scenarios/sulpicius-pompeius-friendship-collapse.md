# The Sulpicius–Pompeius Friendship Collapse of 88 BC

P. Sulpicius Rufus, tribune of the plebs in 88 BC, and Q. Pompeius Rufus, that year's consul, had lived in what contemporaries described as the closest and most loving friendship. The relationship disintegrated completely over the political question of how to integrate [[entities/rome|Rome]]'s newly enfranchised Italian allies into the voting tribes — a cause Sulpicius championed on behalf of the Marian faction.

## Parties & Stakes

- **P. Sulpicius Rufus** ([[entities/publius-sulpicius-rufus]]) — goal: force through the redistribution of new citizens across all thirty-five voting tribes (rather than confining them to a few, which diluted their electoral power); resources: tribunician office, armed supporters, alliance with Marius.
- **Q. Pompeius Rufus** ([[entities/quintus-pompeius-rufus-88bc]]) — goal: resist the measure as consul; resources: consular authority, Sullan backing.

## What Happened

Sulpicius, resorting to armed force to push his legislative agenda, turned his former close friend's own consulship into a battleground. The resulting street clashes between the two men's factions escalated into open tumults and violent confrontations in [[entities/rome|the city]]. In the fighting, a son of Pompeius Rufus was killed.

## Outcome

The rupture between the two former friends became a byword for how quickly intimate Roman political alliances could curdle into lethal enmity when personal ambition and factional loyalty collided; Cicero cites it in *De Amicitia* as a cautionary tale discussed by "everyone" in his own generation, with Atticus specifically remembered as having been close to Sulpicius at the time. The violence of 88 BC fed directly into the broader crisis that saw Sulla march on Rome that same year.

## Open Questions

- The precise sequence and scale of the street violence, and exactly how Pompeius Rufus's son died, remain unclear beyond the report of his death.
