# Servilia's Family Council at Antium

Less than three months after the Ides of March, Julius Caesar's assassins Brutus and Cassius faced a political problem: the ordinary people of Rome, stirred up by a theatrical funeral that Mark Antony staged for Caesar, had turned out to be far less grateful for Caesar's death than the "Liberators" had expected. The senate, seeking a face-saving way to get the two men out of Italy, had voted them a grain commission — an administrative post that could be read either as an honor or as a humiliating demotion.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Servilia** ([[entities/servilia]]) — goal: steer her son Brutus and his allies toward a safe, face-saving course of action; resources: her household as the meeting's venue, her closeness to Cicero and other senators, her promise to use her influence to get the grain commission rescinded by senatorial decree.
- **Marcus Brutus** ([[entities/marcus-junius-brutus]]) — goal: weigh the competing dangers of staying in a hostile Rome versus leaving Italy under what felt like a diminished posting; resources: legal standing as praetor, popular sympathy among some senators.
- **Gaius Cassius Longinus** ([[entities/gaius-cassius-longinus]]) — goal: refuse any arrangement that looked like an insult; resources: his own reputation and defiance.
- **Cicero** ([[entities/cicero]]) — goal: give practical advice while trying to stay personally safe; resources: his political experience, though he was not part of the original conspiracy and had not been consulted in advance.

## What Happened

Cicero arrived at Servilia's villa at Antium before noon and found the meeting already well underway, with Brutus, his mother Servilia, his wife Porcia, his sister-in-law Junia Tertia ("Tertulla," Cassius's wife), and Favonius present. Brutus, in front of this gathered company, publicly asked Cicero's advice. Cicero recommended accepting the Asiatic grain commission, arguing that Brutus's personal safety was now the only thing that mattered. Mid-speech, Cassius arrived and Cicero repeated his advice to him. Cassius, described as wearing a fierce, almost warlike expression, flatly refused to go to Sicily, demanding rhetorically whether he should accept an insult disguised as a favor, and said he would go to Greece instead. When Cicero asked Brutus the same question, Brutus said he wanted to return to Rome "if you agree" — to which Cicero bluntly replied that he did not agree at all, since Brutus would not be safe there. The group then turned to recriminations about missed opportunities in the assassination's aftermath, with Cassius and others sharply blaming Decimus Brutus. Cicero, pressed to say what should have been done differently, ventured that the conspirators should have summoned the senate and taken charge of the state immediately after the killing — a suggestion Servilia cut off with the exclamation "I never heard anyone say such a thing!"

## Outcome

Servilia promised to use her influence to have the grain commission formally cancelled by senatorial decree, sparing Cassius the indignity. Brutus was talked out of his vague wish to return to Rome and instead arranged for games to be held in his name in his absence. Cicero came away with the impression that both men intended to leave Italy, with his own plan being to depart from Antium for Asia.
