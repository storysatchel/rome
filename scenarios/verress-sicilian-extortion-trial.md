# Verres's Sicilian Extortion Trial

In 70 BC, Cicero prosecuted Gaius Verres for misconduct, especially extortion, during his governorship of Sicily in 73–71 BC. Sicilians seeking redress turned to Cicero, who had served as quaestor on the island and maintained connections with leading residents.

## The struggle to bring the case

Verres had wealthy and influential supporters, including the formidable advocate Hortensius. The defence sought to derail proceedings or postpone them until the following year. A rival application to prosecute by Verres’s former quaestor, Quintus Caecilius Niger, forced Cicero first to establish his own right to conduct the prosecution. Supporters of Verres also intimidated Sicilian witnesses.

## Gathering evidence under pressure

After winning the preliminary hearing, Cicero submitted a formal charge and obtained 110 days to investigate. He spent about sixty of those days travelling to Sicily to collect witnesses and documents. Speed mattered because delay served the defence’s strategy. The trial began on 5 August after the jury was selected in the second half of July.

## Exile and conviction

Soon after the first hearing, Verres withdrew into voluntary exile. He was convicted in his absence without a second hearing. Cicero subsequently published both speeches he had delivered and speeches prepared for the abandoned second stage. Their survival therefore does not mean that every published accusation was spoken before the jury. The prosecution strengthened Cicero’s reputation and challenged Hortensius’ standing as Rome’s leading advocate.

## Why delay mattered, and how Cicero prevented it

Postponement to 69 BC would have placed Hortensius and Verres’s supporter Quintus Caecilius Metellus Creticus in the consulship, while Marcus Caecilius Metellus would preside over the extortion court. Some jurors were also due to leave Rome for office, creating the prospect of a more favourable replacement jury. The defence’s timetable was therefore a strategy for changing the institutional setting of the trial.

Cicero countered by abandoning the customary long opening exposition followed by extended witness hearings. He outlined charges briefly and brought forward selected witnesses at once, making it harder for Hortensius to answer without developing accusations the prosecution had not yet fully argued. The first hearing ended on 13 August, before the Votive Games beginning on 16 August. The court never returned for the planned second hearing. The five undelivered speeches later published for that stage covered Verres’s earlier career, judicial abuses in Sicily, tax extortion, theft of artworks, and misuse of coercive authority.
