# Carcer and Tullianum

## Architectural and Topographical Context
The Roman state prison (*Carcer*, later known in Christian tradition as the *Mamertine Prison* or *San Pietro in Carcere*) is situated on the northeastern slope of the Capitoline Hill, overlooking the Comitium and the northwestern corner of the Forum Romanum, immediately adjacent to the Senate House (*Curia Hostilia* / *Curia Julia*) and the Temple of Concord. This central civic placement reflects its integration into public judicial procedures, criminal trials, and state triumph rituals.

## Architectural Bipartition and Mechanics
Topographical and archaeological analysis by T. J. Cadoux establishes that the complex comprises two superimposed, functionally distinct chambers:
1. **The Upper Chamber (*Carcer* Proper):**
   - Historically attributed to King Ancus Marcius (Livy 1.33), who constructed it to suppress rising crime in the expanding archaic city.
   - Built of massive rectangular blocks of Grotta Oscura and Monteverde tufa, remodeled in the Augustan period with a travertine facade bearing the names of the suffect consuls C. Vibius Rufinus and M. Cocceius Nerva.
   - Functioned strictly as a custodial detention facility (*ad continendos homines, non ad puniendos*). Roman criminal jurisprudence did not employ long-term imprisonment as a judicial sentence; citizens and captives were detained here only while awaiting trial, sentencing, or execution.
2. **The Subterranean Chamber (*Tullianum*):**
   - Attributed to King Servius Tullius or derived from the archaic Latin *tullus* (a natural water spring or jet).
   - A semi-circular subterranean vault constructed of dry-laid tufa blocks corbelled together and anchored with iron clamps, cutting into the living tufa of the Capitoline slope.
   - The chamber possessed no doors or staircases; entry was gained exclusively through a circular aperture cut into the stone ceiling.
   - Functioned as the supreme place of secret state execution for condemned traitors and conquered foreign kings following triumphs. Condemned prisoners were lowered through the hole into the damp, lightless chamber, where the public executioner (*carnifex*) executed them by ligature (noose strangulation).
   - Sallust (*Bellum Catilinae* 55) famously describes its horrifying interior: "Its appearance is repulsive and terrible from neglect, darkness, and stench."

## Associated Penal Adjuncts
- **The Scalae Gemoniae (Gemonian Steps):** A flight of steps descending from the Carcer past the Temple of Concord to the Forum. Following strangulation in the Tullianum, the corpses of executed traitors were cast out onto the steps to lie exposed to public view and popular mutilation, after which they were dragged with iron meat-hooks (*uncus*) into the Tiber. Notable exposures include Sejanus and his children under Tiberius (AD 31).
- **The Lautumiae:** Ancient tufa stone quarries located adjacent to the Carcer along the Clivus Argentarius. Used as an expansive auxiliary detention compound to confine large groups of military hostages, war captives, and rebellious slaves when the tiny Carcer was inadequate.

## Historic Executions
Notable figures executed within the Tullianum include:
- Q. Pleminius (204 BC), propraetor accused of sacrilege and cruelty at Locri.
- Supporters of Gaius Gracchus (121 BC), executed en masse under the *senatus consultum ultimum* of consul L. Opimius.
- King Jugurtha of Numidia (104 BC), starved to death following Marius's triumph.
- The Catilinarian conspirators (63 BC), including P. Cornelius Lentulus Sura and C. Cethegus, executed under consul Cicero's direct supervision following the Senate's debate.
- Vercingetorix (46 BC), the Arvernian leader executed after languishing six years in custody following Caesar's Gallic triumph.
