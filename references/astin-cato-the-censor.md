# Cato the Censor

Alan E. Astin's *Cato the Censor* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1978) is a comprehensive scholarly biography of Marcus Porcius Cato (234–149 BC), written largely during a 1974–6 study leave and a fellowship year at the Institute for Advanced Study, Princeton. The book's central purpose, stated in Astin's preface, is to provide a study "as complete and as coherent as the often fragmentary evidence permits," arguing against both the caricature of Cato as an eccentric reactionary and over-sophisticated revisionist readings — Astin's thesis is that Cato was "extraordinary yet in no small degree representative, a remarkable Roman but none the less a Roman of his time."

## Core Argument / Thesis

Astin reconstructs Cato's life chronologically across the first six chapters (birth at [[entities/tusculum|Tusculum]] in 234 BC through the censorship of 184 and later years), then devotes six thematic chapters (7–12) to oratory, Hellenism, writings (the *De agricultura* and *Origines*), the *De agricultura* as a social/economic document, and foreign affairs. His overarching claim is that Cato displayed "broad continuity and consistency" rather than contradiction: the man hostile to Greek philosophy and medicine was simultaneously demonstrably well-read in Greek literature, and this is not psychological paradox but a coherent, calculated stance. [[concepts/roman-identity-and-cultural-memory]]

## Key Claims and Evidence

- Cato was a Roman citizen by birth from a Tusculan family of at least moderate wealth; the "humble origins"/poverty narrative in some ancient sources (Velleius, Valerius Maximus) is rhetorical exaggeration for contrast with his later eminence, not literal fact.
- As censor in 184 BC (with L. Valerius Flaccus), Cato expelled seven senators in the *lustrum*, including the ex-consul L. Quinctius Flamininus (accused of a lurid execution to please a lover) and stripped several *equites*, including L. Cornelius Scipio Asiaticus, of their public horses — actions Astin treats as combining genuine moral censure with the normal technical grounds of unfitness (age, physical disability) rather than being purely personal vendettas.
- Cato possessed substantial, demonstrable knowledge of Greek literature (quotations from the *Odyssey*, parallels with Xenophon's *Symposium* and Demosthenes' *Philippics*, dicta about Socrates, Lycurgus, Solon) even while publicly denouncing Greek rhetoric, philosophy, and especially medicine — Astin treats this not as contradiction but as calculated positioning: Cato opposed the social and political effects of unmediated Hellenism on young Romans, not Greek learning as such. ^[inferred]
- Appendix 1 surveys Cato's ancient sources: his own speeches (over 200 fragments from ~80 speeches) and the *Origines* underlie much later tradition; Polybius knew Cato personally through Scipio Aemilianus; Livy, Cicero (anecdotal, rhetorical-critical, and the *De senectute* dialogue), Nepos, and Plutarch each used distinct, only partially overlapping, sources — Cicero's *De senectute* characterization of Cato is judged unreliable as biography since Cicero used Cato chiefly as "a dramatic mouthpiece."
- The *De agricultura* describes "investment farming": cash-crop estates of roughly 100–240 *iugera* (60–150 acres) producing wine or olive oil for absentee owners, with ancillary grain, osiers, and livestock for self-sufficiency rather than sale — Cato himself owned multiple such properties.
- This type of farm expanded in the early 2nd century BC largely by filling a vacuum left by war casualties and displaced peasants from the Second Punic War, and by exploiting the enlarged *ager publicus* (confiscated from Hannibal's Italian allies), not — at least not yet in Cato's lifetime — primarily by dispossessing smallholders; that dispossession intensified later and fed into the crisis of Tiberius Gracchus's tribunate in 133 BC. [[concepts/land-and-agrarian-conflict]]
- Legal limits on public-land use (traditionally 500 *iugera*, 100 large/500 small animals) were probably tightened or newly imposed in the early 2nd century as use of the *ager publicus* intensified, though the precise origin of the restrictions is disputed in the scholarship Astin surveys. ^[ambiguous]

## Structure / Scope

The book covers Cato's full lifespan (234–149 BC) using primarily literary sources — Cato's own fragments (speeches, *Origines*, *De agricultura*), Polybius, Livy, Cicero, Nepos, and Plutarch — supplemented by extensive secondary scholarship (Kienast, Della Corte, Fraccaro, Scullard) cited in the footnotes. Four appendices treat sources, chronology, and technical problems (e.g., the Spanish campaign, the lex Oppia debate). The book deliberately excludes the strictly agricultural/technical content of the *De agricultura*, treating it instead as social and economic evidence.

## Significance

Astin positions his study against earlier biographies (notably Kienast's *Cato der Censor*, 1954) and against a historiographic tendency either to subordinate one side of Cato's "paradox" (Hellenist vs. anti-Hellenist) to the other or to abandon reconciliation altogether. Astin's resolution — that Cato's attitudes were deliberate and consistent rather than internally contradictory — anticipates and is closely paralleled by Erich Gruen's later, more extended argument in *Culture and National Identity in Republican Rome* (1992), which treats Cato as "a subtle and sophisticated thinker" rather than a tortured or schizophrenic figure. ## Open Questions

- Astin's own footnotes flag persistent scholarly disputes over the origin and dating of *ager publicus* occupation limits, and over whether Cato was formally "dictator" or an improvised "acting dictator" equivalent in some traditions (a confusion Astin does not resolve, since this applies to Fabius Maximus Cunctator in other sources, not Cato himself — the general source-critical problem of inflated family/annalistic traditions is common to both).
- The extent to which Cato's Spanish campaign narrative in Livy reflects Cato's own lost writings versus later annalistic embellishment remains, by Astin's own admission, not fully resolvable from the fragmentary evidence. ^[ambiguous]
