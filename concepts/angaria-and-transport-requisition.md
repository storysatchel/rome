# Angaria and Transport Requisition

*Angaria* (Greek *angareia*, ἀγγαρεία, derived from Old Persian *angaros*, a royal mounted messenger) was the compulsory public obligation (*munus*) imposed on provincial communities, landowners, and peasants to supply draft animals, wagons, drivers, and roadside accommodation for Roman magistrates, military officers, and imperial couriers. Alongside the transport of official travelers and dispatches, the term applied to the requisitioning of draft animals and heavy wagons for military logistics, building materials, and state treasury shipments.

## Legal and Administrative Structure

Under the Republic and the Principate, the Roman state maintained virtually no permanent transport equipment or draft livestock of its own. When Augustus organized the state transport network (*vehiculatio*, later *cursus publicus*), the logistical foundation was laid upon the existing administrative obligation of provincial municipalities to support travelers on public business.

The obligation operated through several institutional mechanisms:

- **Municipal territorial liability**: Every city, town, or rural district through whose territory a designated military highway ran was legally responsible for maintaining the roadside stations (*mansiones* and *mutationes*) within its borders.
- **Civic liturgies (*munera*)**: Municipalities met these demands either by assigning personal or financial liturgies to local citizens—requiring landowners to deliver animals and drivers directly—or by imposing local levies to fund contracts with professional transport guilds (*cisiarii*, *iumentarii*).
- **Parangaria**: Requisitions for secondary cross-country journeys off the primary highway network were termed *parangariae*, obligating villages in the interior to provide pack animals and carts when officials traveled beyond paved postal roads.
- **Heavy freight (*angaria clabularis*)**: Transporting bulk military arms, uniforms, building materials, and copper coinage required heavy four-wheeled wagons (*angariae*) drawn by teams of two to four oxen.

## Agrarian Disruption and Economic Impact

Because transport requisitions were compulsory and took absolute priority over private business, *angaria* was among the most burdensome indirect taxes borne by the provincial population:

- **Agricultural interruption**: Requisitions frequently coincided with critical periods in the farming year—spring plowing and autumn harvest. When passing officials seized plowing oxen or draft mules, agricultural labor was disrupted, directly threatening crop production.
- **Livestock attrition**: The physical strain of long-distance transport on paved roads resulted in heavy livestock mortality. Administrative statutes in the Theodosian Code indicate that roughly one-fourth of station livestock had to be replaced annually due to exhaustion, injury, or disease.
- **Sudden ad hoc exactions**: Private documents demonstrate the arbitrary nature of transport demands. A third-century Egyptian papyrus (P. Fuad. Univ. 6) records a private sender unable to transport domestic gifts because imperial officials had suddenly commandeered all working camels in the district.
- **Fodder supply**: Provincial taxpayers were required to provide fodder (barley, chaff, and hay) for post-station livestock, either through physical deliveries in kind (*capita* as part of the *annona*) or through cash commutations (*adaeratio*).

## Epigraphic Petitions and Provincial Resistance

Numerous inscriptions from the Greek East, the Danubian provinces, and North Africa record desperate appeals by provincial communities against extortionate and violent requisitioning:

- **The Sagalassos Edict (Pisidia, reign of Tiberius, SEG XXVI 1392)**: Issued by the governor Sextus Sotidius Strabo Libuscidianus, this inscription set precise statutory ceilings on what travelers could claim from the town of Sagalassos. Highest-ranking magistrates were limited to ten carts or their equivalent (thirty mules or sixty donkeys), and travelers were required to pay established compensatory rental fees to the owners.
- **The Scaptopara Petition (Thrace, 238 AD, CIL III 12336)**: Inhabitants of the village of Scaptopara sent an embassy to Gordian III, complaining that soldiers and imperial officials regularly abandoned the main highway to exploit their village's thermal baths, demanding free hospitality and seizing draft animals until the village faced abandonment and financial ruin.
- **The Moesian Inscription (159/160 AD)**: Preserves the earliest attested Latin appearance of the word *angaria*, occurring in an official complaint against unauthorized transport seizures.
- **The Anosa-Antimacheia Dispute (Phrygia, 200–237 AD, SEG XVI 754)**: Documents a protracted legal dispute between two neighboring villages regarding the division of the obligation to supply animals and fodder for passing postal traffic, mediated by an imperial procurator.

## Imperial Legislation and Reform Attempts

Recognizing that unrestrained requisitions devastated provincial economies, several emperors attempted to reform or centralize the system:

- **Claudius (49/50 AD)**: Issued an empire-wide edict attempting to curb unauthorized transport requisitions and relieve municipal exhaustion, though recurring complaints demonstrate its limited long-term effect.
- **Nerva (96–98 AD)**: Enacted the reform commemorated on imperial coinage with the legend *vehiculatio Italiae remissa* ("the transport burden of Italy lifted"), transferring the fiscal cost of postal animals and wagons in Italy from local municipalities to the imperial treasury (*fiscus*).
- **Septimius Severus (193–211 AD)**: Nationalized postal transport funding across key provincial sectors, placing *praefecti vehiculorum* in charge of highway contracts, though inflationary pressures and subsequent civil wars shifted the burden back to municipal liturgists.
- **Theodosian Code (Book 8, Title 5)**: In the fourth century, sixty-six imperial constitutions sought to combat endemic abuse. Constantine in 315 AD (CTh 8.5.1) prohibited travelers from seizing agricultural oxen working in the fields, ordering them to wait for official station animals. Julian (CTh 8.5.8; 8.5.17) restricted the number of draft animals hitched to wagons, regulated load weights, and prohibited the use of knotted clubs or metal-tipped whips to protect livestock from fatal abuse.
