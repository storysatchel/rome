# Romans at War: Soldiers, Citizens, and Society in the Roman Republic

## Coverage Note
Comprehensive 17-chapter scholarly collection edited by Jeremy Armstrong and Michael P. Fronda (Routledge, 2020; honoring Nathan Rosenstein), integrating modern "war and society" methodologies to investigate the structural, fiscal, political, and cultural intersections of Roman Republican warfare.

## Sampled Claims and Historiographical Advances
- **Evolution of Archaic Command:** Fred Drogula's analysis demonstrates that Roman military *imperium* did not spring fully formed from the archaic kingship into the dual consulship in 509 BC. Rather, early Roman warfare was organized around private aristocratic warbands (*sodales*, as recorded on the *Lapis Satricanus*) and clan retinues (the Fabii at the Cremera), with unified public command crystallizing only in the 4th century BC.
- **The Dilectus-Tributum Fiscal System:** James Tan demonstrates that the mid-republican levy (*dilectus*) was structurally inseparable from citizen taxation (*tributum*). Because wealthy citizens paid direct tributum to fund legionary pay (*stipendium*), military mobilization created direct aristocratic financial stakes and democratic accountability in Roman foreign policy.
- **The Manipular Army c. 300 BC:** Jeremy Armstrong demonstrates that the transition from a rigid phalanx to flexible maniples (*manipuli*) was an adaptive structural response to warfare in the rugged Apennine terrain against the Samnites, requiring cooperative battlefield autonomy between Roman legionaries and Italian *socii*.
- **Systemic Late Republican Indiscipline:** Lee L. Brice catalogues dozens of recorded mutinies, commander assassinations, and camp rebellions, proving that legionary indiscipline was a continuous feature of Roman armies throughout the 2nd and 1st centuries BC, rather than an abrupt aberration caused by Sulla or Caesar.
