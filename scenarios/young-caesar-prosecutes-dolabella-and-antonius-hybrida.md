# The Young Caesar's Prosecutions of Dolabella and Antonius Hybrida

In 77 or early 76 BC, a Caesar in his early twenties — with no political office yet to his name — prosecuted Cn. Cornelius Dolabella, a Sullan ex-consul (81 BC), on a charge of extortion (*de repetundis*) arising from his provincial governorship. Shortly after, in 76 BC, Caesar brought a similar charge against C. Antonius Hybrida (later consul of 63 BC alongside Cicero, and uncle of Mark Antony). Both cases were calculated moves by a young noble with no safer route yet open to public attention: prosecution was the quickest way for an ambitious young man to get himself noticed.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Julius Caesar** ([[entities/julius-caesar]]) — goal: build a public reputation and oratorical name without yet holding office; resources: family connections, borrowed oratorical style of his relative Caesar Strabo, willingness to take political risk by targeting a powerful ex-consul.
- **Cn. Cornelius Dolabella** ([[entities/gnaeus-cornelius-dolabella-cos-81]]) — goal: defend his conduct as governor and avoid conviction; resources: top-tier advocates Hortensius and Cotta, Sullan political connections.
- **C. Antonius Hybrida** ([[entities/gaius-antonius-hybrida]]) — goal: avoid conviction for his own extortionate conduct; resources: similar elite advocacy and connections.

## What Happened

Caesar won the preliminary *divinatio* (the contest to be awarded the right to prosecute) against Dolabella, but lost the [[entities/forum-romanum|trial proper]]: Dolabella was acquitted, almost certainly for political rather than legal reasons, with the eminent orators Hortensius and Cotta defending him — a case prominent enough that Cicero personally attended to hear the defense speeches. Despite losing, Caesar's prosecution speech became one of his most celebrated, cited by Velleius Paterculus, Asconius, Tacitus, Suetonius, Valerius Maximus, Plutarch, and Aulus Gellius. Unusually for an unsuccessful speech, Caesar circulated a written version in several books — an aggressive act of self-promotion, since circulating failed speeches was rare even for Cicero. Surviving fragments show Caesar invoking nostalgia for stricter ancestral standards and complaining, with calculated self-deprecation, that the "best case" was being wrested from him by Cotta's eloquence.

Caesar followed up against Antonius Hybrida in the same year on similar extortion charges; Antonius also escaped conviction. The two cases together made Dolabella and Antonius stock examples of extortionate governors, cited even decades later by the satirist Juvenal.

## Outcome

Caesar lost both cases outright but achieved exactly what he seems to have wanted: a reputation as a brilliant, fearless young orator, without the lasting political liability of having actually destroyed a powerful senator's career. By prosecuting a prominent Sullan supporter as the son-in-law of Sulla's enemy Cinna, Caesar also signaled his political allegiance early and clearly.

## Open Questions

- Whether Caesar genuinely wanted to win is disputed; some scholars read the acquittals as a deliberately low-risk way to gain fame without making a permanent enemy.
- The exact content of the speech against Antonius Hybrida is almost entirely lost.
