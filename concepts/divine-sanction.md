# divine sanction

Imperial authority could be expressed in sacral language and communicated through approved images. The examples assembled by Clifford Ando concern political recognition, portraits, and public display; they do not all describe gods independently granting a ruler the right to govern.

## Recognition through a colleague’s portrait

In 467 CE Leo received the portrait of Anthemius, the new western emperor, in Constantinople. After formal reception and panegyrics, Leo ordered their portraits to be displayed together and the new image sent to cities throughout the empire. His declaration presented this recognition as his “divine assent” and proclaimed the shared rule of the two halves.

The political act was concrete even though the language was elevated. Earlier, Theodosius had publicized recognition of Magnus Maximus through consular dating, coinage, and an order to exhibit Maximus’ portrait in Alexandria. Images made a change in legitimate government publicly legible.

## Images as carriers of authority

Ando argues against assuming that ceremonies addressed to portraits were transparently hollow to ancient viewers. Portraits could embody the emperor’s protection and oversight in places such as markets and courts; their arrival could be commemorated much like an imperial visit.

Painted panels also presented religious interpretations of victory. Eusebius describes Constantine displayed with a cross above him and the defeated Licinius as a dragon below. Panegyrists could repeat an approved visual message by describing it, thereby speaking within the relative safety of an already sanctioned representation.

## Different meanings of sanction

Imperial approval of an image, sacral descriptions of the emperor’s assent, and legal penalties against a religious group are distinct uses of authority. Tertullian’s description of artwork mocking the Christian God belongs to the last context, not evidence that the mockery itself demonstrates divine approval. Keeping these meanings separate prevents a shared word from creating a false religious argument.
