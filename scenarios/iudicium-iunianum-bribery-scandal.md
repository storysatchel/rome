# The Iudicium Iunianum: A Bribed Jury and Its Cascading Prosecutions (74–70 BC)

The *iudicium Iunianum* ("Junian trial," named for its presiding magistrate) of 74 BC convicted Statius Albius Oppianicus of attempted poisoning — but became notorious because at least one juror, G. Aelius Paetus Staienus, was bribed by both the prosecution and the defense simultaneously, and the resulting scandal spawned a string of separate prosecutions against individual jurors and the presiding official over the following four years (Alexander, cases #149, #153–154, #159–162, #170, #172, #175). Cicero's *Pro Cluentio* (69 or 66 BC) is the chief surviving source, defending the original prosecutor, Cluentius, against a later charge that he himself had corrupted the jury.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Statius Albius Oppianicus** ([[entities/statius-albius-oppianicus]]) — defendant, accused of repeated poisoning attempts (including against his stepson Cluentius); convicted by a narrow margin (one more vote for acquittal would have saved him).
- **A. Cluentius Habitus** ([[entities/aulus-cluentius-habitus]]) — the nominal prosecutor (*nominis delator*) of Oppianicus, later himself accused of having bribed the jury to secure the conviction; defended by Cicero in 66 BC.
- **G. Aelius Paetus Staienus** ([[entities/gaius-aelius-paetus-staienus]]), q. 77 — juror in the original trial, who (per Cicero) took money from both sides and voted to convict; later separately convicted (between 74 and 70 BC) for an unrelated military mutiny charge.
- **G. Iunius** ([[entities/gaius-iunius-aedile-75]]), aed. 75 — the presiding *iudex quaestionis* for whom the trial is named; later himself prosecuted and condemned for irregularities in how he swore in and seated the jury.
- **G. Fidiculanius Falcula** ([[entities/gaius-fidiculanius-falcula]]) — a juror accused of taking bribes to convict; prosecuted twice (73 and 74 BC) on related charges, acquitted the second time.
- **L. Quinctius**, tr. pl. 74 — tribune who drove several of the retaliatory prosecutions against individual jurors and against Iunius himself.

## What Happened

Oppianicus stood trial in 74 BC for a series of poisoning attempts, principally against his own stepson Cluentius, whose mother Oppianicus had married. The thirty-two-man jury split sharply: roughly half voted to convict, including several jurors later accused of having taken bribes from the prosecution, and at least one juror, Staienus, was separately accused of having accepted money from both sides and then voting to convict regardless — a wholly cynical double-cross later held up as emblematic of the court system's corruption. Oppianicus was convicted by the narrowest possible margin. In the years that followed, the scandal metastasized: the tribune L. Quinctius brought a succession of prosecutions against individual jurors from the panel (including Falcula, convicted once and acquitted once, and others convicted of related corruption, mutiny, and electoral-bribery charges under various statutes), and even against Iunius, the presiding magistrate, for procedural failures in how he had conducted the trial. By the time Cicero defended Cluentius in 66 BC against a belated accusation that *he* had bribed the original jury to convict his stepfather, the entire episode had become a byword in Roman forensic rhetoric for the murkiness of jury corruption under the Sullan court system.

## Outcome

Oppianicus's conviction stood, but the court that produced it was discredited for years afterward: at least half a dozen separate individuals connected to the original jury or its presiding magistrate faced follow-on prosecutions between 74 and 70 BC, with mixed results (convictions, acquittals, and at least one case where the jurors themselves refused to proceed). Cluentius himself was acquitted of the belated bribery charge in 66 BC with Cicero's help.

## Open Questions

- The exact number of jurors who took bribes, and from which side, is contested even within Cicero's own highly partisan retelling in the *Pro Cluentio*. ^[ambiguous]
- Several of the individual juror-prosecutions' verdicts are recorded only as "C" (convicted) or "A" (acquitted) with no surviving account of the arguments or evidence.
