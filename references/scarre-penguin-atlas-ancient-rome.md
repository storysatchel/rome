# The Penguin Historical Atlas of Ancient Rome

Chris Scarre's reference atlas (Penguin), over 60 full-color maps plus timelines and short narrative sections, organized into five chronological "parts" from early Rome through "Restoration and Fall." This source file is a scanned (image-only) copy with no text layer — read via vision on a representative sample of spreads rather than full linear extraction.

## Core Argument / Thesis

Not an argumentative monograph but a reference atlas: its "thesis" is organizational — presenting Roman history as a series of synchronized tracks (political/military events, building/construction, literature/philosophy, and parallel developments in Asia & Africa) alongside spatial/cartographic data (trade routes, provincial boundaries, military campaigns). ^[extracted]

## Key Claims and Evidence

- **Parallel timeline format:** the sampled "Timeline: 100–540 AD" spread tracks five simultaneous columns — Roman State, Building & Construction, Literature & Philosophy, and Asia & Africa (splitting into Western/Eastern Empire columns after the 364 AD division) — letting a reader see, for example, that Huns were defeating Goths on the Dnieper (375 AD) in the same years Rome's Aurelian Wall was already decades old and Ammianus Marcellinus was writing his history. ^[extracted]
- **Trade and Transport spread (2nd century AD):** details the Roman road and sea-trade network at its height — major roads from Britain to Syria, named sailing times between ports (e.g. Alexandria–Puteoli, 15–20 days), and a commodity-production map (wine, oil, grain, metals) overlaid on the empire's provinces. A second map traces "Roman trade with the East" — the Silk Route through Central Asia to China (Serica/Changan/Luoyang) and sea routes to India (Muziris, Taprobane/Sri Lanka) and the incense-producing regions of Arabia. Cites Diocletian's Edict of Prices (301 AD) as evidence it was cheaper to ship grain by sea from Spain to Syria than move it 75 miles overland — direct corroboration of the cost asymmetry between land and sea transport that [[references/casson-travel-ancient-world|Casson]] and [[references/hudson-rhetoric-roman-transportation|Hudson]] also address from literary angles. ^[extracted]
- **Augustan succession and imperial crisis patterns:** the sampled "growth of empire" spread covers Augustus's 40-year reign, Egypt's unique status as the emperor's personal domain (no senator could enter without permission), and the Year of Four Emperors (69 AD) as the first major test of the imperial succession system — noting the pattern that adoption, not biological descent, determined the Julio-Claudian succession (no emperor from Augustus to Nero was succeeded by his own biological son). ^[extracted]
- **Fall of the Western Empire narrative:** traces the sequence from the Battle of Adrianople (378 AD, Valens killed) through the formal East/West division under Valentinian I and Valens, the Gothic sack of Rome (410 AD, after Alaric's forces spent years negotiating with Honorius's "vacillating government"), to Romulus Augustulus's abdication (476 AD) — explicitly marking 476 as the symbolic end of the Western Empire while noting the Eastern/Byzantine empire persisted, with Justinian I (527–565) briefly reconquering much lost western territory, until Constantinople's fall to the Ottomans in 1453. ^[extracted]

## Structure / Scope

Five parts (implied by page headers sampled): early Rome through Republic, "The Imperial Regime," and "Restoration and Fall" as the final part, organized around the standard periodization (Monarchy → Republic → Principate → Late Antiquity). Each spread pairs a map with a short narrative essay and image captions drawing on coins, mosaics, and archaeological photographs.

## Significance

A chronological and geographic reference spine for the whole wiki rather than an argumentative source — its Trade and Transport spread is the first source in this wiki to show the *physical* trade-route geography underlying [[references/bang-roman-bazaar|Bang's]] economic analysis and [[concepts/roman-provincial-administration]], and its timeline format is a useful cross-check for dating claims made by narrative sources elsewhere in this wiki (e.g. the exact dates bracketing the Gothic invasions and Western Empire's collapse). ^[inferred]
