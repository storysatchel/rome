# Caesarism/populism

Caesarism and populism belong to a tradition of explaining the collapse of republics through the rise of charismatic strongmen. In this account, leaders gain power over an allegedly gullible populace and turn democratic participation into a means of establishing personal rule. *How Republics Die* examines this explanatory tradition critically rather than accepting it as a law of political development.

## From demagoguery to Caesarism

The book traces a recurring argument expressed through terms such as demagoguery, Caesarism, and Bonapartism: democratization supposedly produces the leaders who destroy democracy. It connects the modern reappearance of this argument to a “spectre of populism” first articulated by Roman elites in the late Republic.

This is a history of a political interpretation, not a claim that the Roman Republic and modern democracies shared identical institutions or causes of crisis.

## The problem of defining populism

The terminology is not neutral. Drawing on Paulina Ochoa Espejo, the source observes that even apparently universal definitions depend on prior judgements about what “the people,” “the elite,” and “popular will” ought to mean. Such assumptions can build an explanation into the categories used to describe events. The book therefore questions how useful populism is as a framework for understanding democratic crises, ancient or modern.

## Political consequences of the frame

In “From Caesarism to Populism,” Annelien de Dijn identifies a further step in this argument: if democracy supposedly generates its own destroyers, political power must be channelled through an elite to protect democracy from itself. The framework is therefore a prescription about who should govern as well as an explanation of collapse.

Her Roman example is Cicero’s defence of a mixed constitution. He opposed rule by a king or an unchecked aristocracy as forms of subjection, but also feared that excessive popular freedom would lead to tyranny. Consuls, Senate, and people were presented as balancing elements. The later populist frame inherited this suspicion of unrestrained popular power, without necessarily reproducing the specific Roman constitutional arrangement.
