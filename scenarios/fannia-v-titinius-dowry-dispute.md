# Fannia v. Titinius: A Dowry Dispute Judged by Marius (100 BC)

A civil suit from the small Latin town of [[entities/minturnae|Minturnae]], over the return of a dowry after divorce, survives only because the presiding juror was the future six-time consul [[entities/marius]] — and because his verdict became a byword for a judge publicly signaling that both parties to a divorce were somewhat at fault.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Fannia** ([[entities/fannia-of-minturnae]]) — divorced wife, suing her ex-husband for retention of her dowry under the action for a wife's property (*actio rei uxoriae*); sought full repayment.
- **C. Titinius** ([[entities/gaius-titinius-of-minturnae]]), of [[entities/minturnae|Minturnae]] — ex-husband, who had invoked a provision (*retentio propter mores*) allowing a husband to keep a portion of the dowry if the wife's own misconduct caused the divorce.
- **C. Marius** (cos. 107, 104–100, 86) — served as juror (*iudex*) in the case, years before his military and political prominence; his verdict here is one of the few glimpses of his pre-command career.

## What Happened

Titinius had divorced Fannia and sought to retain part of her dowry under *retentio propter mores*, a legal mechanism permitting a husband to keep money if the wife's bad conduct (not the husband's) had caused the marriage's end. Fannia sued to recover the dowry in full, implying that Titinius, not she, bore the moral responsibility for the divorce. Marius, serving as the private juror (*iudex*) assigned to hear the dispute, had to decide not simply who owed what but, implicitly, who was to blame.

## Outcome

Marius split the moral difference: he ordered Fannia to recover her entire dowry except for a single token sestertius. The nominal retention signaled that Fannia's own conduct was not entirely blameless even as the ruling overwhelmingly favored her financially — a verdict ancient sources (Valerius Maximus, Plutarch) preserved precisely because of its pointed, almost theatrical use of a trivial sum to make a moral statement.

## Open Questions

- The specific conduct alleged against either party is not recorded in the surviving sources; only the structure and outcome of the verdict survive.
