# Roman Oratory and Political Persuasion

Public speech — delivered chiefly at the *contio*, in the senate, and in the law courts — was a principal tool of Republican political persuasion, but van der Blom's study argues it was one ingredient among several (ancestry, wealth, patronage, military achievement, personal charisma) in building a credible "public profile," rather than an absolute prerequisite popularized by Cicero's own self-presentation as orator-statesman.

## Key Ideas

- Tacitus's *Dialogus de oratoribus* presents eloquence as a near-absolute necessity for public success under the Republic, a view van der Blom traces largely to Cicero's own self-presentation as the orator-statesman *par excellence*. She argues this is unrepresentative: successful careers combined ancestry, wealth, patronage/networks, military achievement, personal charisma, *and* oratory, with no fixed hierarchy among these factors — oratory mattered less for technical brilliance than as part of a credible, consistently communicated public profile.
- Marius, who won an unprecedented seven consulships, relied on military exploits and politicking rather than oratory, has no entry in Malcovati's *Oratorum Romanorum Fragmenta*, and is excluded from Cicero's own history of orators (the *Brutus*) — yet could be an effective speaker "when put on the spot," as when he silenced a riotous crowd after the murder of Memmius in 100 BC. Pompey, Piso Caesoninus, and Mark Antony were likewise "reactive" rather than active seekers of oratorical occasions, using visual spectacle, military reputation, or patronage instead to build their profiles.
- Cicero's self-presentation in the *Catilinarian* orations — always wakeful, always anticipating hidden plots — exemplifies the "revelatory" rhetorical mode Morstein-Marx identifies as characteristic of [[concepts/contio|contional]] speech, by which orators claimed privileged knowledge the audience could not independently verify.
- Credibility before a contional audience followed a reciprocal-exchange logic: a politician's prior offices (*beneficia* granted by the People) obligated continued demonstrated gratitude; consulars could invoke an accumulated record of such *beneficia* as *auctoritas*, as in M. Aemilius Scaurus's famous rebuttal of an accusation ("Whom, Citizens, should you believe?") that reportedly ended the confrontation on the spot.
- Cicero's own *De Amicitia* has Laelius call the *contio* itself a kind of stage (*scaena*) that can nonetheless judge who is a true and steady citizen versus a mere crowd-pleaser (§95) — a passage Morstein-Marx quotes directly, and which the *Institutions and Ideology* volume situates within its broader analysis of *amicitia* as a political institution operating alongside, and sometimes blurring into, patronage. The dialogue's definition of friendship as requiring virtue and mutual goodwill in both parties supplies the ethical vocabulary (trustworthy character, steadiness) that contional audiences, per Morstein-Marx, were asked to judge orators against. ^[inferred]
- Even speeches delivered in the senate (closed to non-senators) could leak out and shape public perception of an orator indirectly, meaning no venue — senate, courts, or *contio* — was fully insulated from a politician's broader public image.
- Van der Blom's closing claim: across her six non-Ciceronian case studies (Gaius Gracchus, Pompey, Caesar, Piso Caesoninus, Cato the Younger, Mark Antony), the single most important factor for political success was not any one career ingredient but "the willingness and ability to communicate the various elements in a politician's public profile... in a credible, consistent and appealing manner" — an openness of criteria that helps explain how *novi homines* could climb the *cursus honorum* despite elite advantages in wealth and networks. ^[inferred] (van der Blom's own explicit synthesis)
- Gaius Gracchus's case illustrates the limits of a single public profile: his broad "champion of the people" image concealed real divergence in interest between urban plebs (favored the grain law), rural plebs (favored the agrarian law but opposed Italian enfranchisement), equestrians (favored jury reform), and Latins/Italians (wanted citizenship) — his failure to differentiate messaging to these sub-groups contributed to his political collapse, and is central to how his rhetoric is read within [[concepts/optimates-and-populares]].
- Increasing attention to rhetorical training in the 90s–80s BC (the first Latin rhetoric handbooks, the closure of the first Latin rhetoric school, the rise of study trips to the Greek East) coincides with, but is not conclusively shown to cause, a rise in oratory's political importance; van der Blom notes this could instead be a parallel but independently caused trend, possibly tied to the consulship's shift from a military to a civil magistracy after Sulla (per Pina Polo). ^[ambiguous]

## Open Questions

- Van der Blom acknowledges the "bad orator" (*malus orator*) designation was subjective and worked on a sliding scale, making it genuinely difficult to determine how poor an orator's technical skill had to be before it measurably hurt his political prospects. ^[ambiguous]
- Whether rhetorical training's rising prominence in the late Republic reflects oratory's genuinely growing political weight, or an independent cultural/educational trend coinciding with it, is left open by van der Blom's own account. ^[ambiguous]
