# Roman House

The Roman house — whether the urban domus or the working rural villa — was simultaneously an architectural artifact, grounded in excavated evidence from Pompeii and Herculaneum, and a functional space that organized the household's labor, religious observance, and even its healthcare. ^[inferred] This page covers the physical structure itself; for the house as a social institution (the *familia*), see [[concepts/roman-household]], and for the daily routines it hosted, see [[concepts/roman-domestic-life]].

## Key Ideas

- **Pompeii/Herculaneum as the architectural evidence base.** Marquardt's chapter "La Maison" ("The House") grounds its reconstruction of Roman domestic architecture heavily in the Pompeii and Herculaneum excavations (beginning 1748), arguing — against contemporary skeptics — that despite their provincial/municipal (non-senatorial) character, Pompeian houses are fundamentally representative of Italic, not purely Hellenic, domestic architecture.
- **The house and garden as medical space.** Draycott's study treats the house and its garden as one of the central active sites of Roman "domestic medicine," the second of her book's four thematic movements (after concepts of health/regimen, before the household's human members and the transmission of medical knowledge). She draws on archaeological house plans, gardens, and medical instrumentaria from Pompeii alongside literary and epigraphic evidence to argue the house was an under-recognized node in the Roman "medical marketplace," functioning alongside — not subordinate to — professional physicians.
- **The rural working house/villa.** Cato's and Varro's farm-management treatises describe the rural steading as a built environment sized and organized around production: Cato gives specific staffing ratios tied to farm size (e.g., an olive farm of 240 *jugera* stocked with thirteen hands plus draft animals) and treats the *pater familias*'s periodic inspection tours of the estate's buildings and workforce as the core mechanism of estate management. Varro's structure separately treats field crops, livestock, and "the steading" (buildings) as distinct domains of agricultural knowledge, and describes his own elaborate aviary, fishponds, and game preserves at Casinum — showing that by the late Republic, elite villa architecture had expanded beyond pure production into aristocratic leisure and display.
- Across these three sources, "the house" functions on a spectrum from austere working farm building (Cato) through elaborate pleasure-villa (Varro) to excavated urban domus with gardens doubling as medical space (Draycott) and as architectural evidence for Italic building tradition (Marquardt) — a single architectural category serving very different social functions depending on context and period. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- How directly do Cato's and Varro's rural steading descriptions (2nd–1st c. BC) map onto the excavated urban domus evidence (Pompeii, 1st c. BC–79 CE) that Marquardt and Draycott rely on? None of the three sources directly bridges rural farm architecture and urban house archaeology.
- Draycott herself flags that conclusions drawn from well-preserved elite/middle-tier urban sites (Pompeii, Herculaneum) may not generalize to the empire-wide or rural population. ^[ambiguous]
