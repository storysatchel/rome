# Vestal Virgins

The Vestal Virgins were Rome's order of priestesses tending the sacred, perpetually-burning fire of Vesta — full members of the Roman citizen class who were nonetheless structural outsiders to any individual family's cult, a paradoxical dual status (per Wildfang) that let them represent the Roman state as a whole in religious matters without personal religious entanglement. Across the citing sources, the order appears in three registers: as subjects of a distinctive religious ritual system (Wildfang), as individual political agents in the late Republic (Wildfang, DiLuzio), and as defendants in Rome's judicial system (Alexander).

## Key Ideas

- Wildfang argues the Vestals' core rituals (preparing *mola salsa* and *muries* from scratch, using only fresh running water from the spring of Juturna, incorporating the October Horse's ashes into the Parilia) were purification and storage rites, not fertility rites as older scholarship assumed — ancient sources consistently describe Vesta's fire as sterile/barren rather than generative, in explicit contrast to masculine fertile-fire imagery like Vulcan's.
- Their most distinctive legal features — independence from a father's or husband's legal authority, exclusion from marriage, and unique dress and hairstyle (the *sex crines*, shared with brides) — are read by Wildfang as consequences of their liminal status: the *captio* initiation rite paralleled, but never completed, the *cum manu* marriage rite's removal of a woman from her birth family's cult, leaving Vestals permanently outside any family-cult structure. ^[inferred: Wildfang's synthesis across ritual evidence, not a single stated ancient claim]
- Vestals' legal trials for *incestum* (violation of their vow of chastity) were handled through a distinct religious procedure, the *quaestio apud pontifices*, rather than Rome's standing criminal courts (*quaestiones perpetuae*). Alexander's catalog of late-Republican trials treats this religious jurisdiction as methodologically separate from ordinary criminal process, retaining only the 114 BC incestum prosecutions of Aemilia and Licinia (cases #38–40) as background to four related subsequent cases, since his catalog otherwise excludes religious-jurisdiction trials.
- In the Regal and early Republican periods, Vestals in Roman historical narrative appear mostly as passive figures; incestum accusations (nine episodes catalogued by Wildfang before the end of the Second Punic War) cluster at moments when the accused Vestal's own family or faction was embroiled in political turmoil the state needed to address, rather than at any generalized moment of crisis.
- By the first century BC, individual Vestals become markedly more active political agents. In 73 BC, Fabia (half-sister of Cicero's wife Terentia) was accused of incestum with Catiline and Licinia was separately accused with her cousin M. Licinius Crassus; both were acquitted, which Wildfang reads as both factional (Optimate accusers against both women) and as evidence of the order's growing "sophistication" as independent legal and economic actors able to marshal elite advocates.
- DiLuzio's recovered chapters (per *A Place at the Altar*) foreground a different late-Republican episode as central: during the Catiline conspiracy of 63 BC, Vestals including Fabia interpreted a fire/flame omen during sacrifice and sent word via Terentia urging Cicero to act against the conspirators, which DiLuzio reads as the Vestals actively orchestrating a portent to shape the political crisis.
- Both Wildfang and DiLuzio independently treat the Vestals' collective intervention with Sulla to spare Julius Caesar's life (after Caesar, appointed *flamen Dialis* by Marius and Cinna, refused to divorce Cinna's daughter) as a landmark instance of unified Vestal political action — Suetonius's account places the Vestals' appeal ahead of Caesar's other advocates in rhetorical emphasis. The two books converge on this episode's significance despite being developed independently.
- DiLuzio also recounts Claudia (daughter of the consul of 143 BC) physically blocking a tribune from obstructing her father's unsanctioned triumph, and Licinia's unauthorized dedication of a Bona Dea shrine on the Aventine (challenged by the urban praetor and senate, then ruled invalid *sacrum* by the pontifical college) — both read as calculated political acts enabled by Vestal status, with the Aventine location tied to the plebeian-cause tradition.
- During the triumviral period, Vestals safeguarded important legal documents and valuables — including, per Plutarch and Cassius Dio, Mark Antony's will, later forcibly seized by Octavian — which DiLuzio situates within a broader pattern of triumviral-era violations of the temple's sanctity.

## Open Questions

- **Source-reliability gap:** the DiLuzio (*A Place at the Altar*) source file supplied for this wiki was severely corrupted — roughly 93% of its PDF bytes were zeroed, and only chapters 6–7 (on Vestal ritual and politics) survived in readable form; the book's treatment of its other stated subjects (the *flaminica Dialis*, priestly couples, Salian virgins) and its own introduction/conclusion are unrecoverable. Where DiLuzio's and Wildfang's accounts of the same episodes overlap, this page weights Wildfang's claims as the more solidly attested, per the citing pages' own cross-comparison. ^[ambiguous]
- Whether DiLuzio frames the Caesar/Sulla rescue as part of a general trend toward growing Vestal political "sophistication," the way Wildfang explicitly does, could not be determined from the surviving DiLuzio text — this specific interpretive framing is attested only in Wildfang. ^[ambiguous]
- The historicity of the Regal/early-Republican incestum episodes (per Wildfang) is fundamentally unverifiable beyond the bare fact that an accusation and punishment probably occurred; details and even some Vestal names may be later authorial invention. ^[ambiguous]
- Whether DiLuzio directly engages with or cites Wildfang's 2006 monograph could not be determined, since the recovered DiLuzio bibliography fragment covers only authors alphabetically "G" through "S." ^[ambiguous]
