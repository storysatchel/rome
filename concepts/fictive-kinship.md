# fictive kinship

Fictive kinship describes close relationships that resemble familial bonds without being biological or legally sanctioned. Gaia Gianni uses the term to study relationships formed around children and caregiving in the Roman world. “Fictive” marks an analytical distinction; it does not mean that the affection or obligations involved were unreal.

## Kinship and relatedness

Gianni distinguishes these bonds from biological relations and legally recognized ones, including marriage and adoption. The distinction is a working definition rather than a universal model of family. Anthropological approaches such as Janet Carsten’s “cultures of relatedness” instead question whether biological proximity should have priority in defining belonging.

Within that broader approach, fictive kinship remains useful for identifying roles that conventional genealogical or legal accounts leave outside the family.

## Caregiving and flexible family roles

The book treats a family as a flexible system of interdependent members. Its discussion of ecological theory highlights how someone outside the nuclear family can fill a missing caregiving role. A surrogate parental relationship need not reproduce a legal parent–child bond to become socially important.

This flexibility is especially significant for studying infancy and socialization. It directs attention to what people did for children and how relationships developed, rather than assuming that every meaningful family role followed from ancestry.

## Roman legal language

The Latin *familia* does not map neatly onto modern “family.” Gianni’s discussion of Ulpian emphasizes the jurist’s definition through a governing rule, including subjection to an ancestor’s power. Comparing that legal structure with lived relationships helps explain why legal membership alone cannot exhaust the study of Roman family life.
