# “‘Quibus permissum est iura condere’: Some Ideas on the Origin of the *Ius Respondendi*”

## Coverage note

This staged reference consolidates title-page, contents, introduction, and representative sampled evidence only; it does not claim full-book coverage. ^[ambiguous]

## Sampled claims

- On one interpretation, Gaius 1.7 does not require a securely attested Augustan license called *ius respondendi*: *quibus permissum est iura condere* denotes authority to explain or interpret law, not legislative power. In this reconstruction, juristic authority traditionally derived from reputation and court recognition; Justinian retrospectively reframed it through the Institutes and Digest, while the 426 Law of Citations, rather than an Augustan license, supplied later statutory authority for named jurists. Masurius Sabinus, under Tiberius, is the clearest explicit case of imperial authorization. These are disputed questions of legal-historical interpretation. ^[ambiguous]
