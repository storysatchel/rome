# The Portorium — Roman Customs Collection and Tax Farming

Bang's Chapter 4 ("A Thin Line: Portorium, Protection and Predation") reconstructs the mechanics of the *portorium*, the Roman system of passage tolls and customs duties, using Egyptian papyri, the Asian Customs Law inscription from Ephesos, the Palmyrene tax law, and the Digest's jurisprudence on *publicani*, read throughout against a parallel Mughal Indian customs apparatus.

## Key Ideas

- **The portorium was a passage toll, not a trade barrier.** Duties were charged on goods crossing a customs line on both entry *and* exit, at nodal points where traffic concentrated — city gates, harbours, river crossings, bridges, highways, bazaars — not as a single boundary tariff between "Rome" and "outside." Because consignments often had to pass several stations, customs duties accumulated with distance travelled; an Egyptian wheat shipment on a short journey paid three separate duties (leaving the nome, using the desert road, and the Memphis harbour tax).
- **Rates were low on ordinary goods, punitive on luxuries.** Ordinary customs and excise generally ran 2–5% ad valorem in both the Roman and Mughal empires (e.g. the *quadragesima*, "the fortieth" = 2.5%). The conspicuous exception was the 25% duty on Eastern luxury goods (spices, silk, incense) entering via Egypt, Syria, and Arabia — Strabo explicitly frames this as the Roman administration maximizing revenue from an unusually rich trade, not as a mercantilist barrier against importing luxuries. The empire was never organized around protecting domestic producers from foreign competition; grants of customs privilege (e.g. Augusta Troas, Pergamon/Elaea) were political/ideological rewards, not trade policy.
- **Layered, segmentary administration, not one coherent regime.** Large imperial customs districts (the Asian *portorium*, the Gallic/Illyrian *portorium*) only loosely tracked provincial boundaries — the Asian district was built around the old Attalid kingdom core but also absorbed tolls in the Bosporus strait, outside Asia's formal borders. Beneath this imperial layer, individual cities kept their own local *vectigalia* (tolls on market trade, bridges, pasturage) which Rome tolerated rather than abolished at conquest — a structure Bang compares directly to Mughal Gujarat, where the central customs-cum-sales tax coexisted with numerous aristocrat-controlled local tolls that central authorities tried, and largely failed, to suppress. ^[inferred]
- **Collection was farmed out, mostly to private contractors.** Most portorium revenue was collected by *publicani* ("tax farmers") under contract to the state for a fixed sum or share of proceeds — ranging from large Republican-era companies (*societates publicanorum*) farming whole districts down to small local contractors at village gates (there is direct epigraphic evidence of a middling Pompeian citizen and even an Antiochene councillor contracting to collect customs outside his home town). Contractors had to pledge property to the state as security, meaning they needed real assets but not necessarily political-elite status; the *Lex Irnitana* barred local councillors (and kin) from bidding on their own town's *vectigalia*, though this rule was not universal. Brunt's analysis shows no empire-wide shift to direct state collection even into the 2nd–3rd centuries; by the 4th century tax-farming is unambiguously dominant.
- **Surcharges and discretion were structural, not incidental corruption.** Because duties were assessed as a percentage of a subjectively judged value, collectors had wide latitude to estimate high. A Memphis harbour-toll receipt (c. 1st century AD) shows an official customs rate of 44 drachmas inflated to 91 drachmas via add-on charges (wharfage, examination fee, banker's cut, exchange fee, interpreter's fee, guard fee, clerks' fee, a libation fee) — effectively doubling the bill through "customary" accretions. Tacitus records Nero banning the "fortieth," "fiftieth," and other invented surcharges, but Bang reads the reform's survival as patchy at best (confirmed by contemporaneous Egyptian papyri still showing heavy surcharging).
- **The law tilted sharply toward the collector, not the merchant.** The Digest's special title on *publicani* (39.4) shows a publican sued after judgment liable only for double the amount wrongfully exacted — but could escape *all* liability simply by voluntarily returning the goods or money before the case concluded, even after a trial had been granted. By contrast, an ordinary private person caught stealing property faced quadruple damages. This asymmetry gave publicans a powerful stalling tactic: hold a merchant's goods, draw out negotiations, and test the merchant's stamina and resources before an "amicable" last-minute settlement — a tactic especially effective given trade's seasonal urgency. Falsely declared or undeclared goods, by contrast, were simply confiscated, with the burden of proof entirely on the merchant (the publican had no duty to advise on correct declaration). Only later legal changes (from Constantine) toughened penalties (death, exile) for abusive collectors, with old lenient procedures still running alongside the new ones. ^[extracted]
- **Merchants were not powerless: a fragile modus vivendi.** Because merchants and goods were mobile, they could reroute around the most abusive or heavily taxed stations — overreach by a collector risked losing the trade altogether, so trade flows acted as a soft constraint on exaction (Bang cites a parallel from Mughal Surat, where a governor tried to lure traders back by promising lighter taxation and better protection). Documented concessions include postponed payment for regular, "known" merchants (settled from sale profits on return), a lower export duty on Anatolian slaves versus slave imports (likely a courtesy discount to a favored trade), and re-export rebates at Palmyra (25 denarii import vs. 13 denarii on departure for unsold unguents) intended to draw goods to market in the first place.
- **F.C. Lane's protection/tribute model frames the overall economics.** Bang adopts historian F. C. Lane's distinction between the genuine *cost of producing protection* and a further *tribute*, a monopoly profit extracted on top by threat of force. Roman conquest did produce a real "peace dividend" — reduced large-scale war inside the Mediterranean let the army concentrate on frontiers while cheap, irregular local militias (e.g., Palmyra's own desert patrols against bedouin raiders) handled everyday banditry — but the state and local elites pocketed the savings rather than reinvesting them in further-improved trading conditions, in contrast to early modern European states competing for "shares of trade." The combined, competing revenue demands of the central fiscus and local aristocratic *vectigalia* produced a slow, piecemeal but real upward trend in the overall tax burden on trade from Augustus to the 4th century (confiscation of local tolls by emperors like Tiberius and Constantine; new levies like the *chrysargyron*). ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- The precise administrative mechanics of how an imperial customs district's revenue target was set, audited, or renegotiated across a tax-farming contract term are not detailed in the sampled material — Bang documents the bargaining dynamics at the point of collection but not the upstream contracting process between the state and a *societas publicanorum*.
- Whether the shift from large Republican tax-farming companies to smaller, more localized contractors in some provinces (the "Brunt thesis") represents genuine administrative decentralization or merely reflects uneven epigraphic survival remains explicitly contested in Bang's own footnotes. ^[ambiguous]
