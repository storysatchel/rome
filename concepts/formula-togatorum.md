# Formula Togatorum

The *formula togatorum* was the treaty-based administrative framework through which Rome mobilized Italian allied manpower without direct taxation or formal bureaucratic annexation.

## Key Ideas

- **Legal basis**: each allied community (*socius*) was bound by an individual treaty of alliance (*foedus*) with Rome; no example of one survives verbatim, so the specific terms are reconstructed from scattered references and are known to have varied in detail depending on how the alliance arose (a defeated enemy accepting terms, a weaker state seeking Roman protection, or active Roman courtship of a potential ally). All such treaties nevertheless bound the ally to "have the same friends and enemies as the Roman people" (*hostes eosdem habeto quos populus Romanus*, Livy 38.11.3) — placing control of the ally's foreign relations entirely in the senate's hands and precluding independent wars or alliances.
- **The quota formula itself**: the senate fixed each ally's annual troop contribution according to the *formula togatorum*, which — on the scant evidence of Livy 34.56.3–7 — took into account a community's number of men of military age and set its required contingent as a proportion of that figure. The exact arithmetic of the formula is not preserved in any source; the exact formula calculating quotas from citizen censuses remains unrecorded in surviving sources.
- **Scale, reconstructed from Polybius's 225 BC data**: in the one year for which Polybius gives hard numbers, the ratio of allied to Roman infantry was roughly 3:2, and allied cavalry outnumbered Roman cavalry three to one. Extrapolating this ratio across the half-century before the Hannibalic War, Rome's four annual consular legions required roughly 25,200 allied infantry a year — under 8% of all allied men of military age — and 3,600 allied cavalry, about 12% of allied men wealthy enough to serve mounted; assuming six years of eligible service per man, around 22% of all allied men would ever have been called up before Hannibal's invasion. ^[inferred]
- **Allied obligations and autonomy:** Italian allies paid no direct monetary tribute to Rome, maintaining municipal autonomy in exchange for supplying equipped military contingents.
- **Structural origin as the alternative to citizen dilectus-tributum**: Tan's chapter reframes the *formula togatorum* as one half of a deliberate two-track extraction system Rome built in the 4th century. Full citizens (*cives optimo iure*) were conscripted through the *dilectus* and taxed through *tributum* — a system that only worked because the two obligations were in rough balance (too many men drafted left too few taxpayers to fund their pay, and vice versa). Not every conquered community could sustain that balance, so Rome built parallel categories defined by what could efficiently be extracted from each:
  - *Non-Latin peregrine socii* and *Latini* (allies and Latin colonies): troop contributions only, via the *formula togatorum*, no *tributum* — "allies were low cost and low reward; citizens were high cost and high reward" (Tan).
  - *Cives sine suffragio* ("citizens without the vote"): both a mandated troop contribution *and* an apparent *tributum*-like financial obligation, without full citizen rights — "the worst of both worlds," per Tan, a status that fell out of use after the 270s.
  - *Coloniae maritimae* (coastal citizen colonies, e.g. Antium, and the 500 colonists sent to Sardinia in 378 BC): granted full citizenship but with *vacatio militiae* (exemption from conscription) because their tiny land allotments made them poor taxpayers and their garrison role kept them geographically fixed; since they never received *stipendium* either, their absence from both the *dilectus* and *tributum* cost Rome nothing.
- **Fiscal interchangeability of Latins and other allies**: despite different legal rights of interaction with Roman citizens, Latin colonies and non-Latin *socii* were treated identically on the extraction side — both paid no tax and received no pay, a point Tan notes is reflected in the later legal phrase *socii nominisve Latini* ("allies or those of the Latin name") in the agrarian law of 111 BC.

## Open Questions

Surviving records do not preserve the exact mathematical formula by which allied manpower rosters were converted into annual legionary quotas.
- Tan's reconstruction of the 4th-century dilectus-tributum/formula togatorum split is explicitly built on Polybius's (2nd-century) description of the *dilectus* projected backward onto the 4th century as "the best available model," not on direct 4th-century evidence — he states this as a deliberately conservative assumption rather than a demonstrated fact. ^[ambiguous]
