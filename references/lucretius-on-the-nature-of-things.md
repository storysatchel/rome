# On the Nature of Things

*De Rerum Natura* is a didactic poem in six books of Latin dactylic hexameter by Titus Lucretius Carus (c. 99-55 BCE), the fullest surviving exposition of Epicurean philosophy from antiquity, composed as an extended argument addressed to one Memmius urging acceptance of Epicurean physics as the cure for religious fear and anxiety about death.

## Core Philosophy / Narrative

Per the extracted section headers (see *Structure* below), the poem's argument proceeds systematically: Book I establishes that "substance is eternal," that void exists alongside matter, that nothing exists except atoms and the void, and surveys and refutes rival philosophers before arguing for an infinite universe. Book II covers atomic motion and the combination of atomic forms into the objects of experience, extending to the claim of infinite worlds beyond our own. Book III addresses the nature and composition of the mind, argues the soul is mortal (dissolving with the body), and draws the central ethical payoff: the "folly of the fear of death." Book IV treats sensory images, perception, and bodily/psychological functions including an extended treatment of sexual passion. Book V argues the world itself is not eternal and narrates the origins of plant and animal life, the "savage" early condition of humanity, and the beginnings of civilization. Book VI covers meteorological and geological phenomena and closes with an account of the Athenian plague. This progression — physics first, ethics (freedom from fear of death and the gods) as the payoff — is consistent with Epicurean method generally. ^[inferred; the overall shape is read from section headers and general knowledge of the work, not from body text in this file — see Open Questions]

## Key Claims and Evidence

- The poem's six-book division and its headnote structure (PROEM, then topical headings per book) are extracted directly from this edition's table of contents and in-text section headers.
- Book I's headers ("SUBSTANCE IS ETERNAL," "THE VOID," "NOTHING EXISTS *per se* EXCEPT ATOMS AND THE VOID," "CHARACTER OF THE ATOMS," "CONFUTATION OF OTHER PHILOSOPHERS," "THE INFINITY OF THE UNIVERSE") indicate the poem opens with a case for atomism as the only tenable physics, rejecting predecessor theories (e.g., those of Heraclitus, Empedocles, Anaxagoras) before the canonical atomist account.
- Book III's headers ("THE SOUL IS MORTAL," "FOLLY OF THE FEAR OF DEATH") confirm the poem's signature ethical argument: since the soul is material and dissolves at death exactly as the body does, death is "nothing to us" and fearing it is irrational — the Epicurean *tetrapharmakos* principle applied to mortality. [[concepts/roman-philosophy-schools]]
- Book V's headers on the "origins and savage period of mankind" and "beginnings of civilization" indicate the poem includes a naturalistic (non-divine) account of human cultural development, a point of contrast with providentialist accounts such as those found in Stoic sources like [[references/seneca-stoic-philosophy]]. ^[inferred]

## Structure / Scope

Six books of hexameter verse (rendered here, per the visible headers, in a metrical English translation), each opening with a proem before its topical sections. Book I: Substance Is Eternal, The Void, Nothing Exists *per se* Except Atoms and the Void, Character of the Atoms, Confutation of Other Philosophers, The Infinity of the Universe. Book II: Proem, Atomic Motions, Atomic Forms and Their Combinations, Infinite Worlds. Book III: Proem, Nature and Composition of the Mind, The Soul Is Mortal, Folly of the Fear of Death. Book IV: Proem, Existence and Character of the Images, The Senses and Mental Pictures, Some Vital Functions, The Passion of Love. Book V: Proem, The World Is Not Eternal, Origins of Vegetable and Animal Life, Origins and Savage Period of Mankind, Beginnings of Civilization. Book VI: Proem, Great Meteorological Phenomena etc., The Plague of Athens.

## Significance

Lucretius is the principal surviving Roman vehicle for Epicurean philosophy, standing opposite the Stoic tradition represented in this batch by Seneca: where Seneca's Stoicism locates freedom in aligning the self with a providential, rational cosmos, Lucretius's Epicureanism locates freedom in recognizing a universe of blind atomic mechanism with no governing providence and no post-mortem survival of the soul, removing the grounds for fear of gods or death ^[inferred]. Both figures converge as source material for [[concepts/roman-philosophy-schools]] as the two dominant Hellenistic-derived ethical systems current in Roman intellectual life.
