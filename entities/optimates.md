# Optimates

The *Optimates* (Latin for "the best men," also frequently styling themselves *boni*, "the good citizens," or the *factio nobilium*) was the conservative, traditionalist senatorial political bloc that dominated the Roman Republic from the mid-second century BCE until the establishment of the Principate. Not an institutionalized political party in the modern sense with programmatic platforms or formal memberships, the Optimates represented an ideological alignment and tactical consensus among the core senatorial oligarchy (*nobilitas*). Their primary political mission was the preservation of senatorial supremacy (*auctoritas senatus*), strict adherence to ancestral constitutional precedent (*mos maiorum*), the protection of aristocratic property rights, and the uncompromising defense of traditional magistracies against the popular assemblies and radical tribunician reform.

## Ideological Foundations: *Mos Maiorum* and *Auctoritas*

The core ideological principle of the Optimates was that the Roman Republic was an aristocratic commonwealth governed by the collective wisdom and moral gravity (*gravitas*) of the Senate. In their worldview, stability and imperial dominion (*imperium*) depended upon maintaining the ancestral constitutional equilibrium established by the founding fathers:
- **`Auctoritas Senatus`:** While acknowledging the constitutional reality that the sovereign Roman people (*populus Romanus*) formally voted on laws and elected magistrates, the Optimates maintained that popular assemblies were inherently volatile, irrational, and susceptible to demagogic manipulation. Therefore, no magistrate had the moral or political right to introduce legislation or declare war without the prior authorization and guidance of the Senate (*senatus consultum*).
- **`Mos Maiorum` (Ancestral Custom):** The unwritten behavioral code and traditional precedents governing public life. Any structural innovation—such as consecutive re-election to the tribunate of the plebs, bypass of senatorial debate, or granting multi-year proconsular commands—was denounced as tyrannical (*adfectatio regni*, aiming at monarchy).
- **The Concept of the `Boni`:** In Cicero’s philosophical defense of the Optimates (notably in *Pro Sestio* 96–103), the *optimates* were defined not merely as high-born patricians, but as all "sound, honest, and prosperous citizens" across Italy—including wealthy equites, respectable municipal magistrates (*decuriones*), and conservative landowners—who sought public tranquility accompanied by aristocratic dignity (*cum dignitate otium*).

## Institutional Weapons and Defensive Tactics

To neutralize reformist tribunes and populist generals, the Optimates deployed a sophisticated array of constitutional and religious mechanisms:
1. **The `Senatus Consultum Ultimum` (SCU):** The "Final Decree of the Senate" (*videant consules ne quid res publica detrimenti capiat*), first formulated in 121 BCE to eliminate Gaius Gracchus. The decree suspended standard constitutional protections, authorizing the consuls to use military force inside the sacred *pomerium* and execute citizen-reformers without appeal (*sine provocatione*). The SCU was subsequently deployed to kill Saturninus (100 BCE), crush the Catilinarian conspirators (63 BCE), and outlaw Julius Caesar (49 BCE).
2. **The Weaponization of the Auspices (`Obnuntiatio`):** Working in close alliance with the [[entities/collegium-augurum|College of Augurs]], Optimate consuls and magistrates routinely declared adverse omens or announced that they were watching the skies (*servare de caelo*), legally dispersing popular assemblies and rendering any reform bills passed under such conditions null and void due to ritual flaws (*vitia*). Marcus Calpurnius Bibulus used this tactic continuously throughout 59 BCE to contest Julius Caesar's agrarian legislation.
3. **Suborning the Tribunician Veto (*Intercessio*):** The Optimates routinely co-opted or bribed at least one conservative member of the ten-man College of the [[entities/tribunes-of-the-plebs|Tribunes of the Plebs]] to veto the legislative proposals of his populist colleagues, as seen when Marcus Octavius blocked Tiberius Gracchus in 133 BCE, and Lucius Caecilius Metellus attempted to bar Caesar from the public treasury in 49 BCE.
4. **Court Control (*Quaestiones Perpetuae*):** The Optimates fiercely defended senatorial exclusivity on juries trying provincial governors for extortion (*quaestio de repetundis*). When Gaius Gracchus transferred jury seats to the equestrian order, and when the equestrian juries infamously convicted the incorruptible Optimate Publius Rutilius Rufus in 92 BCE, the senatorial elite viewed it as a direct assault upon their judicial prerogative.

## Economic and Social Agenda

The domestic policy of the Optimates was fundamentally defensive, aimed at preserving the existing agrarian and financial hierarchy:
- **Resistance to Agrarian Distribution (*Leges Agrariae*):** The senatorial nobility held vast tracts of state-owned public land (*ager publicus*) which they farmed using large gangs of enslaved captives, creating massive wealth. The Optimates fiercely opposed all agrarian reform bills (from Tiberius Gracchus to Servilius Rullus in 63 BCE) that proposed measuring, reclaiming, and parceling out public land to impoverished Roman citizens, denouncing them as state-sponsored theft of private improvements.
- **Opposition to Grain Subsidies (*Leges Frumentariae*):** Beginning with Gaius Gracchus’s *Lex Frumentaria* (123 BCE), the Optimates denounced state-subsidized grain distributions as an intolerable drain on the treasury (*aerarium*) and a corrupt bribe that transformed independent citizens into an idle urban mob (*faex Romuli*) dependent upon demagogues. Sulla completely abolished the grain dole in 81 BCE, though the Optimates were eventually forced to restore it under pressure from food riots in the 70s BCE.
- **Suppression of Urban Guilds and Clubs (*Collegia*):** Recognizing that neighborhood wards (*vici*) and artisan guilds were easily mobilized into radical street factions, the senatorial Senate dissolved all unauthorized collegia in 64 BCE, a decree that was violently undone by Publius Clodius Pulcher in 58 BCE before being permanently reimposed by Caesar and Augustus.

## Key Optimate Figures and Historical Phases

### The Graccan Backlash (133–121 BCE)
The Optimate faction crystallized into a militant political force during the tribunate of Tiberius Gracchus. Led by Publius Cornelius Scipio Nasica Corculum (Pontifex Maximus), senators broke up wooden benches in the Curia and bludgeoned Tiberius and three hundred of his followers to death on the Capitoline. A decade later, the consul Lucius Opimius used the first SCU to slaughter Gaius Gracchus and three thousand plebeian supporters on the Aventine.

### The Sullan Restoration (82–80 BCE)
Following the bloody civil war against Gaius Marius and Lucius Cornelius Cinna, Lucius Cornelius Sulla enacted the ultimate Optimate constitutional counter-revolution:
- Tripled the size of the Senate to 600 members, packing it with equestrian allies.
- Stripped the tribunes of the plebs of their legislative initiative, making tribunician bills subject to prior senatorial approval.
- Barred ex-tribunes from ever running for higher curule magistracies, rendering the tribunate a political dead-end for ambitious young aristocrats.
- Restored exclusive control of the standing jury courts (*quaestiones*) to senators.

### The Catonian Stance and the Late Republic (60–49 BCE)
During the final decades of the Republic, the moral and intellectual leader of the Optimates was **Marcus Porcius Cato the Younger** (Cato of Utica). Renowned for his unbending Stoic integrity, Cato led the conservative senatorial core (including Quintus Hortensius Hortalus, Quintus Lutatius Catulus, and Lucius Domitius Ahenobarbus):
- Refused all compromise with Pompey, rejecting land grants for Pompey’s eastern veterans.
- Blocked Crassus’s tax-farming rebates for the equestrian syndicates.
- Stood firm against Julius Caesar’s request to stand for the consulship in absentia while retaining his Gallic army.

This rigid policy pushed Pompey, Caesar, and Crassus into the informal alliance known as the First Triumvirate (60 BCE). In 49 BCE, the Optimate leadership drove the Senate to pass the SCU declaring Caesar a public enemy, provoking Caesar’s crossing of the Rubicon and the outbreak of the Great Civil War. Following crushing defeats at Pharsalus (48 BCE) and Thapsus (46 BCE), Cato chose ritual suicide at Utica rather than live under Caesar’s monarchical clemency (*clementia*), solidifying his posthumous reputation as the ultimate martyr of Republican senatorial liberty.
