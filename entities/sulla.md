# Lucius Cornelius Sulla

Sulla's march on Rome in 88 BC brought a Roman army into the city under its own general. His later dictatorship and proscriptions escalated the use of military force and organized vengeance in Republican politics, providing a precedent against which Caesar's civil war and clemency would be judged.

## Biography / Role

- The march of 88 BC marked a decisive escalation from political violence to military intervention in the city. As dictator, Sulla used proscriptions to identify enemies for killing and confiscation. The death toll is uncertain; an estimate of "as many as nine thousand people" lacks a specific ancient citation in the available account, and ancient tallies vary. The line attributed to him — "I have executed everybody I can think of. Those I have forgotten about, I will execute at some time in the future." — is likely apocryphal. ^[ambiguous]
- An early source of rivalry with [[entities/marius]] may have been the Jugurthine War: serving as Marius's quaestor, Sulla secured Bocchus's betrayal of Jugurtha, creating competing claims to credit for ending the conflict. This is a retrospective explanation of the later rivalry, not evidence that civil war was already inevitable. ^[inferred]
- In one broad interpretation, Sulla's proscriptions belong to a continuous conflict running from the Gracchan land question and Tiberius Gracchus's death in 133 BC through Marius, Sulla, and Caesar to Octavian's victory at Actium. This reading makes Sulla an escalation point between tribunician violence and outright civil war.
- Caesar's later pardons for former enemies, including Pompey's supporters, contrasted with Sulla's proscriptions. They can be interpreted as an ultimately unsuccessful attempt to break the cycle of vengeance.

## Open Questions

- The precise death toll of the proscriptions remains uncertain; divergent ancient tallies and an uncited high estimate do not establish a reliable total.
