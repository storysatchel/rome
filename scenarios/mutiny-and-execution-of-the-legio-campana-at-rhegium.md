# The Mutiny and Execution of the Garrison at Rhegium

At the end of the Pyrrhic War, around 280 BC, Rome stationed a garrison at Rhegium, the Greek city at the toe of Italy, likely to protect it (and guard the strait to Sicily) during the conflict with Pyrrhus. The garrison — probably composed largely of Campanian troops under a commander Polybius names as Decius — instead seized the city for itself. Rome eventually recaptured Rhegium and executed the surviving garrison soldiers by 270 BC, an act contested in Rome itself: a tribune of the plebs complained that year that the executions had violated the condemned men's rights as Roman citizens.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Decius** ([[entities/decius-campanian-garrison-commander]]) — goal: control of Rhegium and its resources for his garrison, independent of Roman authority; resources: command of the garrison troops stationed in the city.
- **Rhegium** ([[entities/rhegium]]) — stakes: the city's own population and autonomy, caught between the mutinous garrison and the besieging Roman force sent to retake it.
- **Rome / the Roman state** — goal: reassert control over a strategically vital city on the strait to Sicily and punish the mutiny; resources: a besieging force sufficient to retake and hold the city.
- **An unnamed tribune of the plebs (270 BC)** (part of [[entities/tribunes-of-the-plebs]]) — goal: contest the legality of the executions on citizen-rights grounds; resources: tribunician power to raise formal complaint.

## What Happened

A garrison, probably comprising Campanian troops, is attested at Rhegium at the end of the Pyrrhic War. Polybius insists the garrison's leader was a Campanian named Decius, though Valerius Maximus gives a contradictory account; neither source claims the entire garrison was Campanian. The garrison turned on the city it was meant to protect and seized control of it — a mutiny-into-usurpation parallel to the roughly contemporary Mamertine seizure of Messana in Sicily (itself carried out by another mercenary garrison of Campanian origin), though the sources treat the two episodes separately. ^[inferred]

Rome eventually recaptured Rhegium, and the mutineers who survived were executed. In 270 BC — years after the initial seizure — a tribune of the plebs raised a formal complaint that the executions had been carried out "in violation of citizen rights," implying that at least some of the executed garrison troops held some form of Roman citizenship. This bears on the broader, contested question of whether Campanians generally held *civitas sine suffragio*, citizenship without the vote, by this period. ^[ambiguous]

## Outcome

Rhegium was restored to Roman control. The garrison that had seized it was destroyed. The affair left a political residue at Rome itself: the tribune's 270 BC complaint became one of the few surviving pieces of evidence bearing on whether Campanians possessed Roman citizenship in this period, since the complaint's premise — that executing the men violated "citizen rights" — only makes sense if their status as citizens was asserted or assumed.

## Open Questions

- The precise date of the initial mutiny/seizure (the source situates it only broadly "at the end of the Pyrrhic War") and the length of the garrison's independent control of the city are not given.
- Whether the entire garrison was executed, or only ringleaders, is not specified; neither Polybius nor Valerius Maximus claims the whole garrison was Campanian, leaving the makeup and fate of non-Campanian garrison members unclear. ^[ambiguous]
