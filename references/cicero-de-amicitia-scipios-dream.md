# Cicero — De Amicitia, Scipio's Dream

Marcus Tullius Cicero (106–43 BC) wrote *De Amicitia* ("On Friendship") probably early in 44 BC, during his retirement after Caesar's assassination and before the conflict with Antony; it is paired here with *Scipio's Dream*, the only substantial surviving portion of Book 6 of his earlier dialogue *De Republica* (54–51 BC). This edition uses the public-domain translation, introduction, and notes of Andrew P. Peabody. Unlike the three secondary-scholarship monographs in this batch, this is a primary philosophical/political text of the kind that later scholars of Roman oratory and political culture (e.g. [[concepts/roman-oratory-and-political-persuasion]]) cite as source evidence rather than analyze as historiography.

## Core Argument / Thesis

*De Amicitia* argues that true friendship (*amicitia*) can exist only between good men bound by shared virtue, not by calculation of mutual utility; it is framed as a dialogue in which Gaius Laelius Sapiens discusses friendship with his sons-in-law Fannius and Quintus Mucius Scaevola a few days after the death of his lifelong friend [[entities/scipio-aemilianus|Scipio Aemilianus]] (Africanus the Younger) in 129 BC. *Scipio's Dream* depicts a vision in which the elder Scipio Africanus and Scipio Aemilianus's own father appear to him, foretelling his future consulships and death, and urging him to find the only reward truly commensurate with virtuous public service in the celestial afterlife rather than in statues or triumphs.

## Key Claims and Evidence

- Cicero frames the dialogue as reported speech: Scaevola the Augur recounted to the young Cicero what Laelius had said to Fannius and Scaevola himself; Cicero then wrote it up "as the most loving of friends to a friend about friendship," addressed to Atticus.
- Friendship is defined as "entire fellow feeling as to all things human and divine with mutual good-will and affection," second only to wisdom among gifts given to man (§6).
- True friendship requires virtue in both parties; Laelius explicitly rejects the rigid Stoic position that only the "truly wise" (a standard no one has yet met) can be good, preferring a common-sense standard exemplified by men like Fabricius, Curius, and Coruncanius (§5).
- Friendship is distinguished from kinship: natural kinship persists even when goodwill is withdrawn, but friendship dissolves the instant goodwill ends (§5).
- The dialogue's interlocutors are themselves senior Republican statesmen — Laelius (consul 140 BC, augur), Fannius (historian, served under Scipio in Africa and Spain), and Scaevola "the Augur" (tribune, praetor, consul, augur) — situating the treatise's ideal of friendship within the lived political culture of the Roman elite rather than as abstract philosophy alone. ^[inferred]
- *Scipio's Dream* survives independently of the rest of *De Republica* because it was preserved and commented on by the fifth-century grammarian Macrobius, while the bulk of *De Republica* was lost until Angelo Mai recovered portions from a Vatican palimpsest in 1821.
- The Dream stages Scipio Aemilianus being told that earthly fame is brief and "worthless" compared to the reward reserved in the heavens for those who serve the *res publica*; the universe is described as nine concentric spheres producing the "music of the spheres."
- Peabody's introduction argues that the dialogue shows no anachronistic intrusion of Cicero's own post-129 BC political views into Laelius's mouth — e.g. Cicero (unlike his own later experience) has Laelius treat Epicureanism as a still-unfamiliar novelty, consistent with Laelius's actual lifetime. ^[ambiguous] (Peabody's claim of perfect historical consistency is itself a 19th-century scholarly judgment, not something the text proves on its own.)

## Structure / Scope

Two short works bound together in this public-domain edition: *De Amicitia* (27 sections of dialogue) and *Scipio's Dream* (9 sections, transmitted via Macrobius's commentary). The edition includes Peabody's introduction and biographical sketches of the three interlocutors (Laelius, Fannius, Scaevola), plus extensive footnotes identifying historical figures and events referenced in passing (e.g. the killing of Tiberius Gracchus's rival Papirius Carbo's era politics, Scaevola's defiance of Sulla).

## Significance

As a primary source, *De Amicitia* and *Scipio's Dream* function as foundational evidence for the Republican ideology of elite *amicitia* — the networks of political friendship and reciprocal obligation that the secondary scholarship in this batch treats as a structural feature of Republican politics. The *Institutions and Ideology in Republican Rome* volume's Part II on "political alliances" ([[references/vanderblom-gray-steel-institutions-ideology]]) explicitly analyzes *amicitia* as a political institution operating alongside, and sometimes blurring into, patronage — the same conceptual territory Cicero's Laelius stakes out philosophically here. Morstein-Marx's *Mass Oratory* ([[references/morsteinmarx-mass-oratory]]) likewise quotes *De Amicitia* §95 directly, where Laelius calls the *contio* itself a kind of stage (*scaena*) that can nonetheless judge who is a true and steady citizen versus a mere crowd-pleaser — a passage modern scholars treat as Cicero's own commentary on the theatricality of public political life. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- The extent to which the dialogue's content reflects the historical Laelius's actual views versus Ciceronian literary invention remains, by nature, unanswerable from the text alone.
- Because most of *De Republica* is lost, *Scipio's Dream*'s original argumentative context (what immediately preceded Scipio's narration of the dream in Book 6) cannot be fully reconstructed from this fragment alone. ^[ambiguous]
