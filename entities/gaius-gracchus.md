# Gaius Sempronius Gracchus

Gaius Gracchus is treated by both Morstein-Marx and van der Blom as a genuine innovator in Republican mass oratory — the tribune who gave the plebeian tribunate its lasting template as a vehicle for ideologically committed, performatively confrontational popular politics.

## Key Ideas

- Morstein-Marx credits Gaius Gracchus with pioneering a distinctive confrontational contional body language — *concitatio invidiae* ("stirring outrage"): stamping the foot, striking the forehead, and pacing the Rostra — and with being the first orator to free his arm from the toga for gesture and the first to pace while speaking. Tribunes generally, lacking the consular *auctoritas* available to established statesmen, instead drew credibility from the symbolic "protector of the People" function of the office itself, reinforced by invoking the martyrdom of the Gracchi.
- Van der Blom's case-study chapter on Gracchus is organized around the theme of tribunician oratory and family inheritance — his career opens her six-case-study structure (123–122 BC) and serves as the volume's starting point for charting routes to political success beyond Cicero's own oratory-centric model.
- Van der Blom classes Gracchus, alongside Caesar and Cato the Younger, among the politicians who actively sought out oratorical occasions, in contrast to Pompey, Piso Caesoninus, and Mark Antony, who treated public speech as a reactive obligation of office rather than an instrument of self-promotion.
- Van der Blom's volume illustrates the limits of a single public profile through Gracchus's own case: his broad "champion of the people" image concealed real divergence of interest between urban plebs (who favored his grain law), rural plebs (who favored the agrarian law but opposed his Italian enfranchisement bill), equestrians (who favored jury reform), and Latins/Italians (who wanted citizenship rights) — his failure to differentiate messaging to these sub-groups is identified as a contributing factor in his political collapse.
- The tribunate's structural appeal — never part of the formal *cursus honorum*, barred to patricians, but offering near-unlimited access to oratorical occasions via the right to call a *contio* and present bills — is cited by van der Blom as explaining why politicians like the Gracchi were drawn to it out of genuine ideological commitment to popular causes, rather than merely as a career stepping-stone. ^[inferred] (synthesized across van der Blom's discussion of tribunician motive)
- Aulus Gellius's *Attic Nights* is identified as a key non-political-focus source preserving apparently verbatim fragments of Gracchus's oratory (alongside Cato the Elder and Caesar) that would otherwise be lost.

## Open Questions

- Neither citing source directly addresses Gracchus's death in 121 BC in detail (noted in other references in this batch, e.g. Campbell), leaving the precise mechanics of his political collapse — beyond the fractured-coalition explanation — outside these two pages' scope.
