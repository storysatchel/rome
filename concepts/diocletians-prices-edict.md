# Diocletian’s Prices Edict

Diocletian’s Prices Edict of 301 CE attempted to impose maximum prices across the empire in response to high prices and inflation. Its preamble claimed a universal reach, and its list included more than a thousand prices, sometimes distinguishing several grades of a product. Alongside goods, it priced labour.

## Prescription rather than a price survey

The Edict blamed excessive prices on greed and sought to impose ceilings; it did not simply describe ordinary transactions. Groen-Vallinga and Tacoma stress this distinction and the difficulty of relating its figures directly to actual prices. The regulation was soon abandoned, but its failure does not make every relationship within the schedule meaningless.

Their approach is to examine the document internally. Differences between listed occupations may preserve approximate hierarchies of remuneration even when the ceilings cannot be treated as observed market rates.

## Labour and remuneration

More than sixty occupations appear, though the selection covers only part of the known occupational world. Payment takes several forms: daily wages, payment per action or product, payment per pupil per month, and payment according to the quantity of material worked. Daily wages include *pastus*, food, reminding readers that remuneration could combine cash and maintenance.

The list does not draw a sharp boundary between rural and urban work or between occupations performed by slave and free workers. Slaves also appear separately among purchasable goods. The authors interpret this combination as evidence for the importance of hired labour and short-term arrangements, not as proof that legal status was irrelevant to workers’ lives.

## Wage differences and household work

The wide spread of listed wages suggests substantial hierarchy within the labour force. Low individual earnings need not imply that a household relied on one male wage alone: the authors propose an adaptive family economy in which women and children also contributed labour. This is an interpretation of the wage pattern, not a household budget recorded by the Edict.
