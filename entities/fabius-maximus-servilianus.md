# Quintus Fabius Maximus Servilianus

Biological son of (likely) Gnaeus Servilius Caepio, consul of 169 BC, and brother of the consul of 140 BC, Quintus Servilius Caepio. Adopted into the [[entities/clan-fabius|Fabii Maximi]] as a second rescue-adoption of the family's near-extinct line in the 180s BC, making him the adoptive — not biological — brother of [[entities/fabius-maximus-aemilianus]]. See [[concepts/roman-elite-adoption-practices]].

## Biography / Role

- His adoption indicates that the older generation of the Fabii Maximi adopted at least two sons rather than one to rescue the failing line, hedging dynastic risk across multiple aristocratic houses.
- Won the consulship for 142 BC; made proconsul in 141 BC and sent to relieve the Roman commander Quinctius in the Spanish war against the Lusitanian leader Viriathus, bringing two additional legions (totaling roughly 18,000 infantry and 1,600 cavalry) plus ten war elephants requested from Rome's Numidian ally King Micipsa.
- Survived a near-disastrous ambush in which his army, after initially routing Viriathus's forces, was broken by a Spanish counterattack and fled in panic to camp; Servilianus personally rallied his tribunes and soldiers to hold the camp until darkness saved them.
- Earned a reputation for brutality against Rome's enemies: he had the hands severed from captured Roman deserters to deter further desertion, and — per the fourth-century writer Orosius — lured roughly 500 Lusitanian leaders in under a pledge of alliance before severing their hands as well; Such brutality drew no recorded domestic censure, falling within accepted Roman conduct toward external enemies.
- Ultimately failed to defeat Viriathus outright: while the historian Appian gives the most favorable account (an orderly withdrawal to winter quarters), Diodorus and Livy's summarizer describe an agreement with Viriathus on equal terms, regarded in those traditions as a humiliating concession rather than an orderly campaign. ^[ambiguous]
- Separately achieved note as a historian, reportedly authoring a now-lost history of Rome beginning with Aeneas, cited approvingly by Dionysius of Halicarnassus as a source Romans themselves respected.
- His son, Fabius Maximus Eburnus (consul 116 BC, censor 108 BC), later executed his own son during his censorship for an unspecified "grievous lapse in sexual mores," was convicted in court for it himself, and was subsequently exiled — effectively ending the Servilianus branch of the Fabii Maximi.

## Open Questions

- Whether Servilianus's Spanish command ended in a creditable withdrawal or an embarrassing capitulation to Viriathus remains uncertain due to contradictions between Appian and Diodorus/Livy. ^[ambiguous]
- The precise offense behind Eburnus's execution of his son is unrecorded in surviving sources, leaving the episode's severity and the public reaction to his exile only partially explained.
