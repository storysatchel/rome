# The Murder of Valerius Flaccus and the Fimbrian Mutiny

In 86 BC, during the Mithridatic War, the consul L. Valerius Flaccus was sent east — a political rival command to Sulla's, since the senate had assigned Flaccus the task of relieving or superseding Sulla against Mithridates while Sulla was formally an enemy of the Cinnan regime at Rome. Flaccus's own quaestor, C. Flavius Fimbria, became the proximate cause of his death: after Flaccus dismissed Fimbria, accusing him of criminality and of undermining his commander's authority, Fimbria incited the army to mutiny against Flaccus. The mutiny killed Flaccus, and Fimbria then assumed command of the legions himself.

## Parties & Stakes

- **L. Valerius Flaccus** ([[entities/lucius-valerius-flaccus-cos86]]) — goal: assert consular authority over the eastern legions and prosecute the war against Mithridates (or supersede Sulla); resources: consular command, the loyalty of the legions — which proved insufficient once his quaestor turned them against him.
- **C. Flavius Fimbria** ([[entities/gaius-flavius-fimbria]]) — goal: avoid the consequences of Flaccus's accusations against him and seize independent command; resources: his position as quaestor (giving him direct access to and credibility with the troops) and whatever personal following he had cultivated in the ranks.

## What Happened

Flaccus accused his quaestor Fimbria of criminal conduct and of undermining his authority as commander, and dismissed him. Rather than accept the dismissal, Fimbria turned to the legions and incited them to mutiny against Flaccus. The mutiny succeeded in killing Flaccus. The underlying dynamic remains disputed: one interpretation blames Flaccus's own reputation for tough discipline combined with weak leadership for making the army receptive to mutiny; a competing interpretation holds that Flaccus was in fact a qualified and capable commander, and that it was Fimbria who actively undermined his authority and engineered the mutiny for his own benefit. ^[ambiguous] Either way, Fimbria stepped into the vacuum and took command of the legions in Flaccus's place, continuing the campaign against Mithridates under his own authority.

This incident belongs to a broader pattern of officer-driven conspiracies and mutinies that proliferated during the civil-war decades of the 80s BC, when the instability of the central Roman state made it unusually unlikely that mutineers or usurping officers would face punishment. ^[inferred]

## Outcome

Flaccus was dead and replaced, irregularly, by his own former subordinate. Fimbria went on to command the army against Mithridates in his own right for a time, an extraordinary case of a quaestor parlaying a successful mutiny into independent generalship — though his later command ended in failure and his own death. The episode illustrates how thin the line between legitimate subordination and outright usurpation had become for Roman field armies once central political authority at Rome itself fractured during the Cinnan period.

## Open Questions

- The precise nature of the "criminality" Flaccus accused Fimbria of remains unclear here.
- Whether Flaccus's death was planned by Fimbria in advance or was an escalation he did not fully anticipate remains disputed between the two interpretations noted above. ^[ambiguous]
