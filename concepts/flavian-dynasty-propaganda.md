# Flavian dynasty/propaganda

Flavian propaganda made the suppression of the Judean revolt of 66–70 CE into a prominent claim of dynastic achievement. Vespasian and Titus celebrated the victory through *Iudaea Capta* coinage, a triumph, and arches, presenting it as the conquest of a foreign country rather than simply the recovery of an existing Roman possession.

## Conquest as a dynastic claim

This publicity contrasts with the more usual Roman tendency to avoid extensive celebration of victories over provincial revolts. One explanation for the exceptional emphasis is the new dynasty’s need to establish its position at Rome.

The recorded inscription of Titus’ arch in the Circus Maximus claimed that Jerusalem had resisted or escaped earlier generals, kings, and peoples before Titus subdued it. This presentation ignored earlier Roman captures, particularly Pompey’s in 63 BCE and Sossius’ in 37 BCE. The triumphal story consequently displaced the longer history of Roman domination.

## The afterlife of the Flavian narrative

This emphasis may have helped make 70 CE overshadow the earlier loss of Judean independence in both historical memory and scholarship. Flavian commemoration offers one possible cause among others, alongside the scale of Josephus’ narrative and later religious interpretations of the Temple’s destruction.

## A separate Pompeian argument

An earlier account of a Judean delegation inviting Pompey’s intervention may itself have been Pompeian propaganda, intended to justify his eastern settlement before a difficult Roman Senate. That is an earlier, distinct hypothesis, not evidence about Flavian policy; it should not be merged into the dynasty’s commemorative programme.
