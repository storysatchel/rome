# Vestal Virgins

The Vestal Virgins were Rome's order of priestesses tending the sacred, perpetually-burning fire of Vesta. Their combination of citizenship with independence from an individual family's cult can be understood as a liminal status that enabled them to represent the state in religious matters. Their distinctive rituals and legal position also created opportunities for political action, while accusations of *incestum* exposed them to a separate religious jurisdiction.

## Key Ideas

- Their core rituals included preparing *mola salsa* and *muries* from scratch, using fresh running water from the spring of Juturna, and incorporating the October Horse's ashes into the Parilia. One interpretation treats these as purification and storage rites rather than fertility rites: ancient descriptions of Vesta's fire as sterile or barren contrast with masculine fertile-fire imagery such as Vulcan's.
- Their independence from a father's or husband's authority, exclusion from marriage, and distinctive dress and hairstyle (*sex crines*, also worn by brides) may reflect this liminal position. On one reconstruction, *captio* paralleled but did not complete the *cum manu* marriage rite's removal of a woman from her birth family's cult, leaving Vestals outside any family-cult structure. This is an interpretation of the ritual evidence, not a single explicit ancient explanation. ^[inferred]
- Trials for *incestum*, violation of their vow of chastity, normally belonged to the religious procedure of the *quaestio apud pontifices*, not the standing criminal courts (*quaestiones perpetuae*). The 114 BC pontifical proceedings against Aemilia, Licinia, and Marcia were followed by related cases under a special public inquiry; religious and ordinary criminal jurisdiction should not be conflated.
- Regal and early Republican narratives often present Vestals as passive figures. In nine accusation episodes before the end of the Second Punic War, a possible pattern links charges to turmoil involving an accused woman's own family or faction rather than to generalized crisis alone. The historicity of the individual episodes remains uncertain.
- By the first century BC, some Vestals appear as more active political agents. In 73 BC, Fabia, half-sister of Cicero's wife Terentia, was accused of *incestum* with Catiline, while Licinia was separately accused with her cousin M. Licinius Crassus. Both women were acquitted. These outcomes can be read both as factional struggles involving Optimate accusers and as signs of growing political "sophistication": independent legal and economic actors could marshal elite advocates.
- During the Catiline conspiracy of 63 BC, Vestals including Fabia interpreted a fire or flame omen at a sacrifice and sent word through Terentia urging Cicero to act against the conspirators. One interpretation sees this as the active orchestration of a portent to shape the crisis, rather than a politically neutral ritual response.
- The Vestals collectively appealed to Sulla to spare Julius Caesar after he refused to divorce Cinna's daughter. Caesar had been appointed *flamen Dialis* by Marius and Cinna. Suetonius places the Vestals' appeal before those of Caesar's other advocates, giving their unified intervention particular rhetorical prominence.
- Claudia, daughter of the consul of 143 BC, physically blocked a tribune from obstructing her father's unsanctioned triumph. Licinia's unauthorized dedication of a Bona Dea shrine on the Aventine was challenged by the urban praetor and senate and ruled invalid *sacrum* by the pontifical college. Both episodes may represent calculated political uses of Vestal status; the Aventine setting also invites comparison with its association with plebeian causes.
- Vestals safeguarded important legal documents and valuables in the triumviral period. Plutarch and Cassius Dio report that these included Mark Antony's will, later forcibly seized by Octavian. This seizure can be understood as one of the period's violations of the temple's sanctity.

## Open Questions

- Were the Caesar–Sulla intervention and other late Republican episodes part of a sustained growth in collective political capacity, or do they reflect distinct opportunities created by particular crises? ^[ambiguous]
- The Regal and early Republican *incestum* stories cannot be independently verified in detail. Accusation and punishment may preserve historical events, while names and narrative particulars may include later invention. ^[ambiguous]
