# Religious Pluralism in the Roman Empire: Civic Cults, Isis, Mithras, and Christianity

The religious landscape of the High Empire was polytheistic, competitive, and highly diverse. Public authorities maintained traditional ancestral rites (*sacra publica*) while accommodating, regulating, or occasionally suppressing private devotional associations and non-Italian deities across the Mediterranean basin.

## Beyond "Oriental Religions": Modern Historiographical Consensus

Nineteenth-century scholarship, popularized by Franz Cumont, categorized cults with roots outside Italy (such as Isis, Cybele, Mithras, and Atargatis) as "Oriental religions"—alleging that they introduced an alien, emotional mysticism that undermined traditional Roman civic values.

Modern scholarship (notably John North and Beard-North-Price) decisively rejects this dichotomy:
- Non-Italian cults were not foreign invaders eroding Roman society; they were thoroughly naturalized elements of an integrated imperial culture.
- Their spread followed established Mediterranean commercial, urban, and military corridors rather than a coordinated missionary campaign.
- Traditional Roman religion was fundamentally incorporative, continuously adopting foreign deities through *evocatio* and private civic vows.

## Public Rites and the Imperial Cult

At the municipal and state level, civic religion revolved around collective performance rather than personal dogma:
- **The Capitoline Triad:** Temples to Jupiter Optimus Maximus, Juno, and Minerva anchored civic loyalty in Rome and provincial colonies.
- **The Imperial Cult:** The worship of deceased divinized emperors (*divi*) and the genius of the reigning *princeps* functioned as a universal civic test of political loyalty. Magistrates, provincial assemblies (*concilia*), and military units offered regular sacrifices to the gods for the emperor's safety (*salus Augusti*). Participation was a civic obligation; refusal was interpreted as sedition (*maiestas*).

## Mysteries and Integrated Deities: Isis and Mithras

Among the most visible non-Italian deities in the western empire were Isis and Mithras:

### The Cult of Isis
Originating in pharaonic Egypt and Hellenized under the Ptolemies, the cult of Isis experienced its greatest expansion under the Principate in Italy, Gaul, Spain, and Britain:
- In Rome, major sanctuaries operated on the Aventine, Caelian, and Campus Martius (the *Iseum Campense*).
- As dramatized in Apuleius's second-century novel *Metamorphoses* (Book 11), Isis was worshipped as a universal saviour goddess offering personal redemption, moral purity, and post-mortem protection. Her public processions (*Navigium Isidis*) were spectacles celebrated across port cities like Ostia and Corinth.

### The Mysteries of Mithras
Although claiming Persian antiquity, the mysteries of Mithras were essentially an imperial Roman creation, developing inside the borders of the empire during the late first and second centuries CE:
- **Devotional Core:** Centered on small, windowless, subterranean sanctuaries (*mithraea*) designed to resemble caves, featuring side dining benches and an iconic cult relief of the *tauroctony* (Mithras slaying the cosmic bull).
- **Social Composition:** The cult admitted only men and was organized into seven hierarchical grades of initiation (Raven, Bridegroom, Soldier, Lion, Persian, Sun-Runner, Father). It enjoyed intense popularity among legionary centurions, frontier auxiliary soldiers (along the Rhine and Danube), imperial freedmen, and customs officials.

## Christianity and Official Persecution

Unlike Isis or Mithras—whose adherents happily participated in civic sacrifices and honored Rome's public gods alongside their private devotions—Judaism and Christianity practiced exclusive monotheism.

- **Judaism's Ancestral Immunity:** Rome exempted Jewish communities from civic and imperial sacrifices because Jewish worship was ancestral tradition (*mos maiorum*), permitting Jews instead to pray and sacrifice *to* their God *on behalf of* the emperor.
- **Christianity as *Superstitio*:** Because Christianity broke with traditional ethnic bounds and forbade members from offering incense, wine, or sacrifice to civic gods or the emperor's image, authorities classified it as an illicit, obstinate superstition (*superstitio illicita*).
- **High Empire Persecution Policy:** During the reigns of Trajan and Hadrian, persecution was reactive rather than systematic. In the famous exchange between Pliny the Younger (governor of Bithynia-Pontus) and Trajan (*Epistulae* 10.96–97, c. 112 CE), the emperor laid down the official imperial policy: Christians were not to be sought out (*conquirendi non sunt*); anonymous accusations were forbidden; but if accused and refusing to demonstrate repentance by sacrificing to the Roman gods, they were to be executed for contumacy.
