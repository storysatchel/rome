# Popular assemblies

Roman Republican popular assemblies gave citizens a role in collective decisions, but their procedures did not distribute political initiative or voting weight equally. Voting was openly hierarchical, and the presiding officeholder controlled the agenda.

## Voting through centuries and tribes

The *comitia centuriata*, *comitia tributa*, and assemblies of the plebs were divided into corporate voting units, each of which cast one vote. In the centuriate assembly, organization by census classes and centuries favored the uppermost classes, which possessed a majority of the 193 voting units.

The tribal assembly and plebeian assemblies used territorial divisions: thirty-one rural tribes and four urban tribes. A reform of the centuriate assembly in the middle of the third century BCE combined tribal and centuriate criteria, although its details remain disputed. Across these arrangements, an individual citizen’s influence depended on the unit in which his vote was counted and on his social position.

## Control of proposals and procedure

Assemblies did not meet according to a fixed annual schedule. They had to be summoned and formally opened by an authorized magistrate or tribune, who also presided and controlled the procedure. Consuls and praetors presided over the *comitia*; tribunes presided over the *concilia plebis*.

Only magistrates and tribunes could put forward new laws and decrees. At the final voting stage, citizens accepted or rejected a motion without further debate; they could not introduce their own proposal or amend the text. In elections, the presiding magistrate also controlled the formal admission of candidates, and the result acquired validity through the magistrate’s proclamation. Popular decision-making therefore operated within strong procedural limits rather than through an unrestricted right of initiative.

The magistrate could also refuse the *renuntiatio*, the formal proclamation of an electoral result, and order another vote. Closing an assembly remained the prerogative of the official who had summoned it. These powers make the assemblies difficult to treat as autonomous decision-making bodies.

## Legislative reform without equal initiative

In the second century BCE, procedures increasingly became subjects of legislation. The *leges tabellariae* introduced secret ballots successively in electoral, judicial, and legislative assemblies, while laws on *ambitus* sought to regulate campaigning. This resort to statutes can be interpreted as a response to pressure on customary rules. Such changes altered political practice without removing the corporate organization of voting or officeholders' control of proposals.
