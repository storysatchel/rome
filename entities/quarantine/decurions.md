# decurions

Decurions were members of municipal councils whose public standing brought substantial financial responsibilities. Their obligations extended beyond attending meetings: managing public assets, appointing officials, collecting taxes, and securing the performance of civic tasks could expose their property to claims.

## Personal liability for civic administration

Ulpian’s discussion of a father consenting to his dependent son’s appointment shows the range of possible liabilities. These included public money, public works appointments, the nomination of successors, leasing public revenues, and appointments of guardians or curators. Decurions could also face liabilities arising from colleagues’ arrears or the enforcement of legacies left to the town.

A case discussed by Modestinus involved a civic loan inadequately secured by land. Liability fell not on the original lender but on an official who transmitted the obligation without a suitable guarantee. Public service could thus make an officeholder answerable for a transaction he had not initiated.

## Protecting the property behind public service

Imperial policy restricted transfers designed to evade liturgical obligations and barred certain potentially conflicting activities. Decurions could not ordinarily lease their own town’s revenues. A law of 382 forbade them from managing another person’s property, threatening exile for the councillor as well as punishment for the employer. Kehoe reads this partly as an attempt to keep their resources directed toward their own estates and civic duties.

The system was not entirely inflexible. Councils short of members could recruit men of less prestigious occupations, and impoverished decurions might receive maintenance, especially where they had spent their resources on their town. These accommodations reveal efforts to sustain the municipal institution as well as to compel its members.
