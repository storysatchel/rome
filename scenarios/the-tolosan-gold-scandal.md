# The Tolosan Gold Scandal and the Prosecution of Caepio

As proconsul in [[entities/gaul|Gaul]], Quintus Servilius Caepio seized a vast hoard of sacred gold and silver from the Gallic sanctuary city of [[entities/tolosa|Tolosa]] (modern Toulouse). He was widely, if never formally, believed to have embezzled much of this "Tolosan booty" for himself. Shortly afterward, his refusal to cooperate with his co-commander Gnaeus Mallius Maximus against the invading Cimbri contributed to the catastrophic Roman defeat at [[entities/arausio|Arausio]] in October 105 BC, in which an entire army was destroyed.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Quintus Servilius Caepio** ([[entities/quintus-servilius-caepio]]) — goal: escape accountability for both the embezzlement and the military disaster; stakes: his property, his Senate seat, his life
- **Lucius Appuleius Saturninus and Gaius Norbanus (tribunes)** ([[entities/lucius-appuleius-saturninus]], [[entities/gaius-norbanus]]) — goal: secure Caepio's condemnation; resources: a proposed extraordinary judicial commission empowered to investigate "the embezzlement and treason perpetrated in Gaul"
- **Gnaeus Mallius Maximus** — Caepio's co-commander at Arausio, also condemned in the same wave of prosecutions
- **The Senate's government party** — goal: shield Caepio and other elite defendants; resources: tribunician intercession, ultimately overwhelmed by mob violence

## What Happened

By unconstitutional popular decree, Caepio was first stripped of his proconsular command and had his property confiscated; a second decree later expelled him from the Senate. Still unsatisfied, Saturninus and Norbanus proposed an extraordinary judicial commission to investigate the Tolosan embezzlement and the treasonous conduct at Arausio. When the Senate's allied tribunes tried to block the measure through intercession, they were violently driven from the assembly, and senators present were pelted with stones by the crowd. The investigation proceeded regardless.

## Outcome

Caepio, his co-commander Mallius Maximus, and numerous other prominent men were condemned in the resulting wave of prosecutions in 103 BC. A friendly tribune managed, at the cost of his own political career, to save Caepio's life, though not his property, office, or standing. Norbanus himself was later put on trial for having set the prosecutions in motion in the first place. The affair became legendary in Roman memory as the origin of the proverbial "Tolosan gold," said to bring ruin on whoever touched it.

## Open Questions

- The embezzlement charge remains unproved; its plausibility does not establish the precise facts of the theft.
- The identity of the tribune who saved Caepio's life "by the sacrifice of his own civil existence" is not given.
