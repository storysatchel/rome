# Fausta and Flaccilla

Fausta and Flaccilla illustrate two ways of representing an empress’s contribution to imperial rule. Fausta's coinage celebrated the production of dynastic heirs, whereas Flaccilla's emphasized an authority expressed through imperial dress and Christian imagery of victory. The contrast concerns public representation rather than a simple disappearance of dynastic concerns.

## Fausta: motherhood and dynastic legitimacy

Fausta’s coins associate her with the virtues of an imperial mother. A double solidus depicts a seated female figure holding a child, using imagery modeled on a nursing Isis alongside the legend *pietas augustae*, the augusta’s sense of duty. A bronze issue of 325 presents her holding two children and calls her the “hope of the state.” These images connect the well-being of the empire to her ability to supply heirs.

The connection between Fausta and imperial power was not exclusively maternal. A panegyric of 307 describes a picture of her offering Constantine a jeweled helmet. This depicted imperial victory and the political legitimacy Constantine gained through marriage into the imperial family.

Fausta’s jeweled headband introduces a further ambiguity. Scholars have sometimes treated the headbands of Fausta and Helena as ornaments rather than insignia. Their appearance on coins only after the women became *augustae* in 324 supports a formal political significance. Their integration into women’s hairstyles, unlike the ribbon-fastened male diadem, need not make them merely decorative.

## Flaccilla: the empress as sovereign

Flaccilla’s elevation to augusta in 383 coincided with her son Arkadios’ promotion to augustus. Despite this dynastic occasion, her portrait did not explicitly celebrate childbearing. Flaccilla was the first *augusta* portrayed wearing a jeweled diadem and the imperial *paludamentum*, fastened at the right shoulder by a jeweled fibula.

Her attire resembles that of Theodosios I and suggests a degree of partnership in imperial authority. The accompanying Christian language of victory reflects a shift from the empress as symbolic mother of victory to the empress as victorious sovereign. This is an interpretation of the images, not evidence that Flaccilla held the same formal powers as an emperor.

After Flaccilla, the diadem and imperial mantle became standard features of the augusta’s image. This development was reinforced by Christian accounts of Helena as Constantine's partner in establishing Christian monarchy. Ambrose’s story of Helena turning the nails of the Cross into imperial equipment provided a religious explanation of female participation in imperial victory. It is evidence for the ideological framework of later representations, not a contemporary record of Flaccilla’s political decisions.
