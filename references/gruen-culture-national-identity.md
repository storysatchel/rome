# Culture and National Identity in Republican Rome

Erich S. Gruen's *Culture and National Identity in Republican Rome* (Cornell Studies in Classical Philology, Duckworth, 1993; based on Cornell's 1991 Townsend Lectures) examines how the Roman aristocracy of the third and second centuries BC confronted the cultural legacy of the Greek East and used that confrontation to construct a distinctive Roman identity. It extends Gruen's earlier *Studies in Greek Culture and Roman Policy* (1990) beyond episodes of state manipulation of Hellenism toward the broader question of Rome's "groping toward cultural maturity and self-definition."

## Core Argument / Thesis

Gruen's central thesis, stated in the Introduction, rejects the conventional characterization of Roman-Hellenic relations as "a love-hate relationship, the working out of an inferiority complex, a creative tension" — labels he calls misleading oversimplifications. Instead, he argues the Roman elite's engagement with Hellenism was "an evolving process through which the Romans shaped their own values and gained a sense of their distinctiveness," driven by confident appropriation and adaptation rather than anxiety. ## Key Claims and Evidence

- The canonical Trojan-origin legend (Aeneas, via Lavinium and Alba Longa) was not simply inherited but actively shaped and deployed by Romans in international diplomacy; accepting a Trojan rather than Greek derivation signaled a deliberate claim to a special place among Mediterranean peoples rather than passive borrowing. [[concepts/roman-identity-and-cultural-memory]]
- [[entities/cato-the-elder]] is reinterpreted as "a subtle and sophisticated thinker" rather than a figure of irreconcilable contradiction: his attacks on Greek oratory (the gibe that Isocrates' pupils "grew old" studying rhetoric only fit to plead before Minos in Hades), on philosophy (branding Socrates a "babbler" who undermined law and tradition), and especially on Greek medicine were a "deliberate design" to assert Latin cultural superiority, not evidence of a divided personality — even though Cato quoted Homer and Xenophon with ease, joked with Polybius in Greek, and modeled a fragment of the *Origines*' opening on Xenophon's *Symposium*.
- Cato's hostility to luxury (*lex Oppia* repeal, sumptuary laws, the speech *de signis et tabulis* against private display of art) and to Greek doctors (he retails the tale of Hippocrates refusing to treat "barbarian" enemies of Greece to argue Greek physicians were hostile to Romans by extension) forms a consistent program rather than isolated outbursts.
- The acquisition and display of captured art as war trophies predates [[entities/marcus-claudius-marcellus|Marcellus]]'s sack of Syracuse (211 BC), with religious precedents going back to Camillus's *evocatio* of Juno from Veii (396 BC): cult statues from defeated cities (Veii's Juno, Praeneste's Jupiter Imperator, Volsinii's Vortumnus, Falerii's Janus and Minerva Capta) were ritually "absorbed" into Roman state religion as tokens of divine favor rather than simple plunder, and generals like C. Maenius (prows mounted on the Rostra, 338 BC) and Sp. Carvilius (a colossal bronze Jupiter cast from captured Samnite armor, with a smaller statue of himself at its foot) used captured material for explicit self-commemoration. [[concepts/art-and-political-propaganda]]
- Roman sculptors are essentially unattested in the literary and epigraphic record for the 3rd–2nd centuries BC; Greek artists (the painter Metrodorus, sculptors Timarchides and his sons Polycles and Dionysius, the Rhodian Philiscus, the south Italian Pasiteles) migrated to Rome in increasing numbers from the early 2nd century, especially following L. Scipio Asiagenus's victory over Antiochus III (190 BC) and L. Aemilius Paullus's conquest of Macedon (168 BC) — meaning Roman "art" of this period was substantively produced by Greek hands directed toward Roman commemorative and ideological purposes.
- Later second-century theories that a "Scipionic circle" or "philhellene party" opposed a "Catonian"/anti-Hellenic party do not survive scrutiny: numerous examples from the mid-2nd century show no consistent factional alignment on Hellenism, and the idea of Romans modeling themselves on Hellenistic monarchy or suffering a collective "inferiority complex" are both judged inadequate as general explanations.
- The satirist Lucilius (2nd century BC), despite mocking philhellenic affectation (e.g., Albucius's pretentious Greek mannerisms), shows no anti-Greek bias himself: he quotes Homer, Archilochus, Euripides, Plato, and Xenophon, alludes to Epicurean atomism and Academic philosophy, and was addressed a philosophical treatise by the Academic Clitomachus — Gruen reads Lucilius as deflating pretentious Hellenizers, not Hellenism itself, making him "no denigrator of things Greek" and an apt closing figure for a book about genuine (not merely affected) cultural absorption.

## Structure / Scope

Organized in seven chapters moving roughly chronologically and thematically: the Trojan legend; Cato and Hellenism; art and civic life (acquisition of art as spoils); art and ideology (artists and patronage); theater and aristocratic political culture; the appeal of Hellas among the nobility; and Lucilius as closing case study. Gruen draws on literary sources (Livy, Plutarch, Cicero, Pliny the Elder's *Natural History*, Polybius) and the modern archaeological/epigraphic scholarship on sculptors' signatures and coinage. The book's scope runs essentially from the early Republic through the late 2nd century BC (Gracchan/Marian era), with the rise of satire read as a symptom of the nobility's cultural posture becoming "increasingly anomalous and antique."

## Significance

Gruen explicitly positions the book against both older "political faction" readings of Roman cultural history (Scipionic vs. Catonian parties) and psychologizing explanations (inferiority complex, love-hate ambivalence), arguing both approaches collapse under close examination of the primary evidence. ^[inferred] The treatment of Cato converges closely with Astin's *Cato the Censor* (1978) — both reject the "paradox" framing of Cato as self-contradictory — though Gruen pushes further toward reading Cato's Hellenism-critique as calculated rhetorical strategy aimed at elevating Roman self-esteem specifically, rather than Astin's more cautious emphasis on biographical consistency. ## Open Questions

- Gruen's own survey of rival explanations (political faction, "good Greeks vs. bad Greeks," public/private compartmentalization, imperial pragmatism, monarchic emulation, inferiority complex) is presented as a critique of inadequate alternatives, but his positive replacement thesis — gradual, confident self-definition — is itself acknowledged as difficult to pin to precise causal mechanisms or turning points. ^[ambiguous]
- The degree to which earlier (4th–3rd century) Roman aristocratic adoption of Greek cognomina (Philo, Sophus, Philippus, Philus) reflects genuine philhellenism versus isolated naming fashion is explicitly left open by Gruen as a matter on which "it would be prudent to avoid sweeping inferences."
