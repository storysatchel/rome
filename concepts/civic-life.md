# civic life

Roman civic life combined public honours with financial responsibilities and practical work. The evidence for substitute officials and female city patrons shows why an honorific title should not automatically be treated as either an empty distinction or proof of direct imperial supervision.

## Honorary office and substitutes

Greek cities had a Hellenistic tradition of assigning eponymous offices and responsibility for civic expenditure to gods, heroes, and rulers. Under Roman rule, prominent Romans and members of imperial families could receive similar appointments; an inscription even records the deified Roman Senate as a civic office-holder.

Such appointments raised the question of who performed the work. Prefects carrying out ordinary municipal tasks in place of imperial office-holders must be distinguished from curators with extraordinary duties. Substitute prefects need not have supervised cities more rigorously than ordinary officials.

Concrete substitutes are visible in Greek civic life. At Sparta, P. Memmius Pratolaus was honoured for acting four times for the eponymous patronomos, the god Lykourgos. At Mytilene, Agrippina the Elder held the gymnasiarchy for life, with duties apparently performed by a hypogymnasiarch. The uneven naming of substitutes in inscriptions should not be confused with proof that no substitute existed.

## Women as city patrons

Women’s civic roles have sometimes been dismissed as ceremonial or reduced to financial benefaction. An alternative interpretation is that women were selected as city patrons in their own right and, within limits, were expected to fulfil the same duties as male patrons. Their presence cannot simply be explained by the influence of powerful Severan women, because some patronesses predated that dynasty.

Patronage must also be distinguished from benefaction. Julia Memmia’s statue inscription at Bulla Regia praises the baths she financed and also names her as patroness, but many benefactresses were never elected patrons. Bronze *tabulae patronatus*, which record a council’s decision to co-opt a patron and its reasons, provide a different kind of evidence from statue bases erected mainly to thank a donor.
