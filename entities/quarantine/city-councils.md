# city councils

Roman city councils were municipal bodies drawn from landowning elites and responsible for basic local government, including fiscal administration. Their obligations linked the maintenance of civic institutions with the collection of public revenue from land.

## Taxes on uncultivated land

Aurelian made councils liable for the taxes on uncultivated lands in their territories. Constantine modified that obligation by allowing a three-year immunity before customary dues became payable. Where councils could not sustain the charges, the burden could be spread among other lands in the territory.

## Claims on elite property

Municipal responsibilities also constrained the property of the landowning elite. In the later empire, councils exercised a degree of corporate control over land belonging to decurions. Kehoe argues that, in theory, much elite land was therefore subject to claims by both the imperial treasury, or *Fiscus*, and local councils. These overlapping claims limited the freedom of owners to sell land or use it in credit transactions.

## Land transactions and the security of revenue

The councils’ burden formed part of a wider policy linking tax liability to productive land. A purchaser normally acquired both the fruits of land and its fiscal obligations. Constantine required purchasers to acknowledge outstanding taxes as well as current assessments, preventing bargains that purported to confer tax-free possession of indebted farms.

Julian later rejected agreements by which a seller retained the tax obligation while transferring the land. Such a separation left the treasury without the property to seize if taxes went unpaid. These rules explain why councils operated within a system of claims attached to land: the government sought a durable revenue base even when ownership changed. The resulting obligations could also make purchases more costly or uncertain.
