# exposed or mutilated body

Exposure and abuse of a condemned person’s body could extend Roman punishment beyond execution. At the Scalae Gemoniae, near the prison and Rome’s political centre, the displayed corpse linked state judgement to public humiliation and sometimes to crowd violence.

## Punishment after death

Denial of burial carried religious and commemorative consequences. A widespread belief held that the unburied dead could not immediately enter the underworld. A corpse abandoned in the Tiber also lacked a grave through which survivors could offer sacrifices or preserve a public record of achievements.

Exposure thus attacked the dead person’s relationship with both the community and the afterlife. It was not merely a practical disposal of remains.

## Sejanus and the crowd

In Cassius Dio’s account of Sejanus’ fall in 31 CE, senators struck his exposed face as he was led to prison, while the crowd jeered and pulled down his statues. The Senate later condemned him to death, and his body was thrown onto the Stairs. Dio also reports the strangling and exposure of his two children.

In Dio’s account, the crowd abused Sejanus’ corpse for three days and then cast it into the river. This can be interpreted as a significant transition: spectators moved from destroying images to attacking the body and finally assumed control of the last stage of execution. Tolerated violence around the prison could spill beyond the ritual space sanctioned by the state.

## An uncertain beginning

The exact beginning of exposure on the Stairs under imperial authority is uncertain. No early “first victim” can be identified confidently, and the Stairs must be distinguished from the older practice of exposing bodies in that area of the Forum. Later examples, including Decebalus’ head under Trajan, show the site’s continuing association with enemies of the state.
