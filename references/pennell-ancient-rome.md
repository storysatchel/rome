# Ancient Rome: From the Earliest Times Down to 476 A.D.

Robert F. Pennell's *Ancient Rome* is a revised-edition companion volume to his earlier *History of Greece*, written for American "high schools and academies" and first revised at Riverside, California in July 1890. It is an unabashed reference-and-drill textbook rather than an interpretive history: Pennell states in his preface that the book aims to give "a short and clear statement of the rise and fall of Rome, with a biography of her chief men, and an outline of her institutions, manners, and religion," and includes appended "Examination Papers" reflecting contemporary college entrance requirements.

## Core Argument / Thesis

Pennell advances no historiographical thesis; the book's organizing logic is chronological completeness and institutional clarity for students memorizing Roman history. Its implicit framework treats Roman history as a sequence of constitutional stages — monarchy, patrician-plebeian "Conflict of the Orders," republic, and empire — each defined by enumerable laws, offices, and dates rather than by causal argument.

## Key Claims and Evidence

- Rome's geography and ethnography open the book: Italy's three early races (Iapygian, Etruscan, Italian), the Etruscan twelve-city confederacy "between the Arno and the Tiber," and the Latin Confederacy of thirty towns under Alba's religious leadership at the Latin festival to [[entities/jupiter]] Latiaris.
- The regal period's political structure is laid out schematically: the three tribes (Ramnes, Tities, Luceres), each split into ten curiae and each curia into ten gentes (3 tribes × 30 curiae × 300 gentes), with the king, Senate (originally all patres, later fixed at 300), and the Comitia Curiata as the three constitutional pillars.
- The "Conflict of the Orders" (patricians vs. plebeians) is tracked law by law: the Publilian Law (471 BC) moving tribune elections to the Comitia Tributa, the Terentilian Rogation and Twelve Tables (451–450 BC) following the Decemvirate and the Virginia affair, the Valerio-Horatian laws (449 BC) establishing appeal rights and binding plebiscita, the Canuleian Law (445 BC) permitting intermarriage, and finally the Licinian Rogations (367 BC) reopening the consulship to plebeians and capping public-land holdings at 500 iugera — culminating in full legal parity by 286 BC via the Hortensian law.
- [[concepts/ager-publicus]] (public land) is explained as land seized from conquered cities, nominally rentable at 10–20% of produce but increasingly treated as de facto private property (*possessio*) by the patrician occupants who held it — the structural grievance Pennell identifies as underlying Spurius Cassius's failed 486 BC land law and, by extension, the later Gracchan crisis.^[inferred]
- Military history is treated through named set-piece wars: the Gallic sack of Rome and the Battle of the Allia (390 BC), the three Samnite Wars including the humiliation at the Caudine Forks (321 BC), and the Latin War (340–338 BC) that dissolved the Latin Confederacy and absorbed its cities into the Ager Romanus.
- The index shows the book's full chronological range continues through the Punic Wars, the Gracchi, Marius and Sulla, Caesar, the Triumvirates, the Julio-Claudian and Flavian emperors, the "Five Good Emperors," and the third-century crisis down to Constantine, Theodosius, and the Gothic/Vandal invasions ending in 476 AD, though the sampled text concentrated on the earliest periods.^[ambiguous]

## Structure / Scope

The book proceeds strictly chronologically across roughly 400+ pages plus an extensive index, organized into short numbered chapters (geography, early inhabitants, early government, external history, the Conflict of the Orders, the Samnite/Punic/civil wars, the Empire) each written as dense, fact-dense prose amenable to recitation and testing rather than narrative argument. Pennell draws on standard classical sources (Livy, Sallust, Polybius) implicitly rather than citing them in-text, consistent with its textbook genre; maps and plans were "specially drawn and engraved" for the edition.

## Significance

As a 19th-century American secondary-school text, Pennell's book exemplifies the genre of condensed constitutional-and-military Roman surveys that circulated alongside works like [[references/greenidge-history-of-rome-later-republic]] for more advanced students — Pennell explicitly markets the book as filling the gap at the high-school level that works like Greenidge's assumed as prior knowledge.^[inferred] It offers no original research; its value here is as a terse cross-reference for dates, offices, and law names across the full span of Roman history.

## Open Questions

- The sampled text covered the regal period through the early Samnite Wars in depth but not the imperial sections in narrative form (only via the index); claims about later-period content rest on index entries alone and should be treated cautiously.^[ambiguous]
- Pennell gives Spurius Cassius's agrarian law a tentative date of "486?" himself, signaling his own uncertainty about early Republican chronology that modern scholarship treats as even more unreliable.^[ambiguous]
