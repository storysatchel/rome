# Consulship

The two annually elected consuls held the Roman Republic's supreme magistracy: absolute military command abroad and headship of the civil administration at Rome, legitimated by the centuries' or tribes' grant of *imperium* and only formally confirmed, not conferred, by the archaic curiate assembly.

## Key Ideas

- In Polybius's constitutional analysis, the consuls held the "monarchical" element of Rome's mixed constitution: absolute military command abroad (recruiting, appointing military tribunes, punishing subordinates, controlling funds) and headship of the civil administration at Rome (presenting embassies to the senate, executing senatorial decrees, summoning and presiding over [[concepts/popular-assemblies-rome|popular assemblies]], and executing statute law).
- Botsford argues — reading Cicero's *De lege agraria* against the ancient sources — that the centuries or tribes actually *conferred* imperium or *potestas* on consuls, while the archaic curiate assembly's *lex curiata de imperio* role was merely to confirm or formally sanction a grant already made elsewhere; this bears directly on how the consulship derived its legal authority. See [[concepts/popular-assemblies-rome]] for the assemblies' side of this relationship.
- By the late third century BC, Rome's public life had grown around the two consuls to include a growing number of praetors (one from the start, two by c. 244, four by c. 227, six by 197), curule and plebeian aediles, ten tribunes of the plebs, quaestors, and irregular boards — the consulship sat atop this expanding structure; see [[concepts/roman-magistracies]] for the broader magistracy system the consulship headed.
- The *comitia centuriata* — presided over by a consul — was the body that elected consuls, praetors, and censors, tying the office's own renewal each year to the assembly it in turn presided over once in office.
- The consular year's start date varied considerably before stabilizing: irregular dates in the 5th–4th centuries BC, likely the Calends of May through much of the 3rd century, the Ides of March from (at latest) 217 BC, and finally 1 January from 153 BC onward — a shift Hans Beck and others connect to military exigencies (the Second Punic War, the Celtiberian rebellion in Hispania) rather than calendrical reform. ^[inferred]
- Inauguration followed an invariant ritual sequence: pre-dawn auspices, donning the toga praetexta before the household Penates, a *salutatio*, a procession to the Capitol with senators and friends, sacrifice of a white ox to Jupiter by each consul, vows for the republic's welfare (*vota pro salute rei publicae*), and a first senate meeting on the Capitol, at which both consuls were expected to speak and a debate on the state of the republic was held.
- The first senatorial session of the year typically — though not invariably — addressed the *sortitio* or *comparatio* (allotment or negotiated division) of provinces between the two consuls and the size of the levy each would command, though Livy shows urgent matters (tax doubling in 215 BC, scheduling the censorial election in 214 BC) could take precedence depending on the year's circumstances.
- Rather than deducing consular functions from an abstract, formalist definition of *imperium* (*Staatsrecht*), consular practice developed empirically through customary precedent, ritual procedure, and shifting political consensus.

## Open Questions

- Whether *imperium domi* (civil power within the *pomerium*) and *imperium militiae* (military power outside it) represented a single territorially-bounded power, or whether *imperium* was strictly a military concept with civil authority inside the city resting on a separate *potestas*, remains an unresolved constitutional dispute. ^[ambiguous]
- The book's own synthesis of the pre-153 BC consular year's starting dates concedes that evidence is sparse and sometimes contradictory for the 5th and 4th centuries, leaving the earliest chronology of the office uncertain. ^[ambiguous]
