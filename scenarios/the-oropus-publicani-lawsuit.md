# The Oropians' Lawsuit Against the Publicani Over the Land of Amphiaraus

During Sulla's campaigns in Greece in the 80s BC, he vowed a gift of land to the temple of Amphiaraus at Oropus in Boeotia and confirmed it as inviolable territory exempt from taxation. A generation later, Roman tax-farming companies (*publicani*) operating in the province began assessing dues on that same land, prompting the townspeople of Oropus to bring a formal complaint to Rome. The resulting dispute and its resolution are preserved on an inscription recording a letter sent to Oropus by the consuls of 73 BC.

## Parties & Stakes

- **The people of Oropus** ([[entities/oropus]]) — goal: protect the tax-exempt, sacred status of the temple land granted by Sulla; resources: Sulla's prior grant, an inscribed senatorial precedent, and standing to petition Rome directly.
- **The publicani** (tax-farming companies) — goal: maximize taxable territory and revenue under their contracts in the province; resources: contracts (*locationes*) with the Roman state, financial leverage, political connections among Roman equestrians.
- **The consuls of 73 BC, M. Terentius Varro Lucullus** ([[entities/terentius-varro-lucullus]]) **and C. Cassius Longinus** ([[entities/gaius-cassius-longinus-cos73]]) — goal: adjudicate and communicate a binding ruling; resources: consular authority and the senate's backing.

## What Happened

Oropus's representatives argued that the land in question had been granted inviolable, tax-exempt status by Sulla as a fulfillment of his vow to the god Amphiaraus upon his victory, and that the publicani's assessments violated that grant. The matter went before the senate, which issued a senatus consultum in Oropus's favor; the consuls then wrote directly to the Oropians confirming the decision, a letter that the town had carved into stone alongside the senatorial decree itself, preserving the whole correspondence as proof against future challenges by the tax-farmers. ^[extracted]

## Outcome

The senate upheld Oropus's claim and the land retained its tax-exempt status, at least for the time being; the episode illustrates how a small provincial community could successfully litigate against the politically powerful publicani by invoking a prior grant from a Roman magistrate, and how such victories were memorialized epigraphically to deter renewed attempts at collection.

## Open Questions

- Whether the publicani continued to contest the ruling after 73 BC is not recorded in the surviving evidence. ^[ambiguous]
