# Roman Population Growth Debate (High Count vs Low Count)

The [[concepts/ager-publicus]] and [[concepts/land-and-agrarian-conflict]] pages both cite Roselaar's claim that the second century BC was a period of population *growth*, following "De Ligt and Lo Cascio," as the load-bearing demographic premise behind her population-pressure thesis. The shallow pass treated this as a settled citation; the underlying debate is in fact a genuinely unresolved, decades-long dispute about how to read the Augustan census figures against earlier Republican ones, and Roselaar's own chapter (see [[references/roselaar-public-land-roman-republic]]) lays out at least three competing reconstructions rather than a single consensus model.

## Key Ideas

- **The trigger: a discontinuity in the Augustan census.** The first Augustan census (28 BC) records 4,063,000 citizens — far higher than Republican-era figures extrapolated forward would predict under the traditional "low count" reading, which assumed the Republican census (like earlier ones) counted only adult male citizens. The question of how to reconcile this jump defines the whole debate. ^[extracted]
- **Lo Cascio's "high count":** argues the Augustan figure, unlike earlier Republican censuses, counted the *entire* citizen population (men, women, children), not just adult males. Applying a low multiplier of 3 retroactively to Republican figures on this assumption implies the citizen population grew from roughly 2 million (225 BC) to over 12 million by 28 BC — extremely rapid growth (0.5-0.8% per year sustained over two centuries) that would also require importing an enormous, otherwise-unattested number of slaves to explain growth beyond natural increase. Roselaar judges this scenario's demands on Italy's carrying capacity (requiring a population density not matched again until the late 19th century, and food imports with no archaeological or literary trace) too severe to accept. ^[extracted]
- **Brunt and Hopkins's older "low count":** reads the Augustan figures as consistent in method with Republican ones (adult males only, multiplied by a higher factor), implying only modest growth across the second and first centuries BC — the dominant view before Lo Cascio's challenge, but one De Ligt argues overestimated the starting population in 225 BC (Brunt's 4.5 million for all of Italy versus De Ligt's revised 3.5-4 million), depending heavily on whether Polybius's 225 BC troop-census figures (2.24) represent all adult males of both Romans and allies, or only *iuniores* for the allies specifically. ^[extracted]
- **De Ligt's intermediate model, which Roselaar adopts:** keeps the "low count" assumption that growth was modest (around 0.3% per year net, after accounting for emigration) but lowers Brunt's starting population estimate and raises the ending one slightly (assuming roughly 1 million citizens lived outside Italy by the Augustan period), arriving at about 5 million people in Italy by 28 BC via gradual natural increase of the free citizen population rather than mass slave importation or hidden women-and-children in the census base. Roselaar explicitly follows this reconstruction, while noting that the Republican census figures would then represent an *increasing* degree of real under-representation over time (undercounting a genuinely growing population) rather than "showing" decline. ^[extracted]
- **Roselaar's own caveat — none of the models is secure:** she states plainly that "the problems connected with the 'high count' scenario are too serious to accept... at this moment," but adds that "the 'low count' scenario is not free from problems either," singling out the core unverified assumption (that the Republican census counted all adult males consistently) as one scholars should keep testing against a possible "middle count." This page treats her adoption of the De Ligt model as a considered but explicitly provisional choice, not an established fact the wiki should repeat uncritically. ^[extracted]
- **Downstream economic calculations built on the model:** Roselaar uses De Ligt's population figures to argue the market for basic foodstuffs in Rome was small relative to Italy's total arable land (as little as 10-20% of peninsular arable land could have fed a 750,000-1,000,000-person Rome in 133 BC), which in turn supports her broader thesis that elite market-production pressure on land was limited and that population growth, not elite land-grabbing, squeezed small farmers. Slave-number estimates are revised downward in parallel: using ancient agronomic ratios (one slave per 6.25-10 *iugera* of vines, one per 18.5 *iugera* of olives, one per 20 *iugera* of grain), she estimates perhaps 250,000 slaves working market-oriented land in Italy around 133 BC — far below the 2-3 million figures older scholarship (Toynbee, Brunt, Hopkins) assumed for the early Empire, a figure the "high count" model requires but the "low count"/De Ligt model does not. ^[inferred]
- **Independent convergence from a different angle:** Rosenstein's *Rome and the Mediterranean* (see [[references/rosenstein-rome-and-the-mediterranean]]) reaches a structurally similar anti-dispossession conclusion via demography rather than population-growth modeling specifically — he calculates that supplying Rome's wine and oil needs required only about 0.5% of Italy's arable land and 35,000-70,000 slave workers, "far too small a labor footprint to explain mass displacement" of smallholders, and attributes urban population growth to employment pull (construction, festivals) rather than fleeing dispossessed farmers. The two scholars do not use the same model, but both independently conclude that the traditional elite-dispossession narrative overstates both the land and labor an expanding slave-estate economy would actually have required. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- The debate is explicitly unresolved even in Roselaar's own telling: she adopts the De Ligt "low count" revision as a working basis for her argument but does not claim it is proven, leaving open the possibility that a future "middle count" model could revise her population-pressure thesis's quantitative foundations.
- Whether Rosenstein's independent, non-demographic argument against mass dispossession should be read as corroborating Roselaar's population-growth thesis specifically, or as a parallel but methodologically distinct line of evidence against the same older consensus, is not something either source states directly — treated here as a parallel convergence, not a joint argument. ^[ambiguous]
