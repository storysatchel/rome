# The Execution of Julius Canus by Caligula

This is a historical-anecdotal episode as related in Seneca's *On Tranquillity of Mind* (ch. 14), presented as a real event from Seneca's own era rather than distant legend — Seneca stresses that "the circumstance that he was born in our own age cannot dim our admiration of him." It is a small, specific instance of arbitrary imperial violence against a private citizen, not part of any larger political crisis already covered in this wiki.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Julius Canus** ([[entities/julius-canus]]) — Roman Stoic philosopher; goal: die with dignity and intellectual curiosity intact; resources: none but his own composure
- **Caligula** ([[entities/caligula]]) — emperor; orders Canus's execution after a personal grievance, with apparently total discretionary power over his life

## What Happened

After "a long altercation" with Caligula — Seneca does not specify its subject — the emperor tells Canus as he is departing, "Don't flatter yourself with any silly hope; I have ordered your execution." Canus replies, "Thank you, most excellent prince," a line Seneca admits he cannot fully interpret (sarcasm about Caligula's cruelty, a taunt about his inconsistency, or genuine relief at being freed from his power) but calls "magnificent" regardless.

Canus then waits ten days for the sentence to be carried out, "without the slightest anxiety" by Seneca's account. When the centurion comes to summon him to join a column of condemned men being marched to execution, Canus is in the middle of a board game; he finishes counting the pieces, tells his opponent "don't cheat after I die and say you won," and remarks to the centurion that he is "one piece ahead" at the moment of his death. Told by his friends that they grieve to lose him, he answers, "Why are you sad? Your question is whether souls are immortal; I shall soon know." On his final walk, his philosophy teacher asks what he is thinking; Canus says he intends to use his last conscious instant to observe whether the soul perceives its own departure from the body, and promises to report the answer to his friends if he discovers anything.

## Outcome

Canus is executed, one of several deaths Seneca groups under the phrase "Caligula's carnage." Seneca offers no indication Canus's death achieved anything politically — its sole value in the essay is exemplary, illustrating Stoic *apatheia* (freedom from disturbance) in the face of arbitrary, unanswerable autocratic power.

## Open Questions

- Seneca does not specify the subject of Canus's "long altercation" with Caligula, nor give a precise date; the broader dating of the episode to Caligula's reign (37-41 CE) is background knowledge, not stated in this text. ^[inferred]
