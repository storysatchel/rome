# Gaius Gracchus Taunts "Honest" Piso Over the Grain Law

During his tribunate in 123 or 122 BC, Gaius Gracchus proposed his innovative *lex frumentaria*, providing monthly grain distributions to Roman citizens at a fixed, low price. The bill's most prominent senatorial opponent was L. Calpurnius Piso Frugi, consul of 133 BC and a man whose cognomen *Frugi* ("thrifty" or "honest") was central to his public reputation.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Gaius Gracchus** ([[entities/gaius-gracchus]]) — goal: pass and defend his grain law against senatorial opposition; resources: tribunician power, oratorical skill, popular support.
- **L. Calpurnius Piso Frugi** ([[entities/lucius-calpurnius-piso-frugi]]) — goal: discredit the grain law and its author without appearing hypocritical; resources: consular rank, reputation for personal integrity, wealth.

## What Happened

At a public meeting (contio) called to discuss the grain bill, Gracchus accused Piso of various improper and wicked actions. When Gracchus ordered Piso summoned to answer, his own lictor — unfamiliar with which "Piso" was meant, since it was a common cognomen — asked for clarification. Gracchus snapped back: "You are compelling me to call my enemy 'the Honest One.'" The line played on Piso's cognomen *Frugi*, turning a term of praise into a backhanded insult live in front of the crowd.

Once the law passed, Piso made a point of personally showing up at a grain distribution despite his substantial wealth and his public opposition to the law — a theatrical act clearly meant to provoke Gracchus and advertise his own frugal, principled persona. In front of the assembled Roman people, Gracchus challenged him publicly, asking how he could square collecting subsidized grain with having opposed the law that provided it. Piso's recorded answer was that he did not like seeing his property divided up among the citizens, but if it was going to happen regardless, he wanted his own share of it.

## Outcome

Neither side scored a clean rhetorical knockout: Gracchus's law passed and stood, but Piso's public stunt let him have the last visible word in the eyes of the crowd that day, undercutting the moral high ground Gracchus had tried to claim. The exchange became famous enough that Cicero, writing over half a century later, still quoted both the pun and the grain-queue confrontation as illustrations of Gracchus's quick tongue and Piso's needling persistence.

## Open Questions

- Whether the lictor's confusion over "which Piso" was staged or genuine is unclear even to ancient sources.
- Gracchus's response to Piso's grain-queue retort is not recorded.
