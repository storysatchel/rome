# Consul Metellus Celer's Arrest at the Hands of Tribune Flavius

In 60 BC the tribune of the plebs Flavius promoted an agrarian bill, almost certainly connected to Pompey's demand for land grants to his discharged veterans. The sitting consul Q. Caecilius Metellus Celer opposed the measure and clashed openly with Flavius, who responded with an extraordinary escalation: he threatened to prevent Metellus Celer from departing for his consular province and, for a time, actually had him imprisoned.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Q. Caecilius Metellus Celer, consul of 60 BC** ([[entities/metellus-celer]]) — goal: block Flavius's agrarian bill and otherwise oppose [[entities/pompey]]'s eastern settlement measures; resources: consular authority, personal animus (his wife Clodia's family connections notwithstanding), and public standing to resist tribunician pressure.
- **Flavius, tribune of the plebs** ([[entities/tribune-flavius-60bc]]) — goal: pass the agrarian bill providing land for Pompey's veterans; resources: tribunician power to propose legislation, to veto, and (as exercised here) to order a consul's arrest.
- **Pompey** ([[entities/pompey]]) — the measure's ultimate beneficiary, whose veterans needed land; goal: see his soldiers settled; resources: immense personal prestige and influence over allied tribunes, even while not holding office himself at the time.

## What Happened

Metellus Celer, as consul, opposed the publicans' claims in connection with their Asian contracts and separately clashed with Flavius over the tribune's agrarian bill for Pompey's veterans. ^[extracted] Flavius escalated the confrontation dramatically: he threatened to prohibit the consul from departing [[entities/rome|Rome]] for his assigned province and had him imprisoned, at least temporarily. ^[extracted] Despite this extraordinary pressure, Metellus Celer achieved his purpose — the tribunician proposal was not passed. ^[extracted]

## Outcome

The agrarian bill failed; Metellus Celer's resistance, even under arrest, held. The episode is a striking demonstration of a tribune's coercive power over even the Republic's highest annual magistrate, and of how far political struggles tied to Pompey's settlement could escalate short of outright violence.
