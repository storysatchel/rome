# *Praetorian*

## Coverage note

This staged reference consolidates title-page, contents, introduction, and representative sampled evidence only; it does not claim full-book coverage. ^[ambiguous]

## Sampled claims

- Praetorian bodyguards developed from ad hoc escorts of late-Republican generals (contents, ch. 1 “Evolution,” 44–31 BC; EPUB lines 720–744). | ^[ambiguous]
- The Guard’s privileged status and pay created dependence between emperors and a powerful elite military body (PDF pp. 32–40). ^[ambiguous]
- Praetorian bodyguards developed from ad hoc escorts of late-Republican generals (contents, ch. 1 “Evolution,” 44–31 BC; EPUB lines 720–744). ^[ambiguous]
- Augustus converted these forces into a permanent institution after Actium, integrating personal military power into the imperial army (introduction and ch. 1, EPUB lines 724–728). ^[ambiguous]
- The Guard’s permanent concentration in Rome from AD 23 made it a direct political force; the Castra Praetoria enabled rapid intervention in the capital (introduction, EPUB lines 678–686). ^[ambiguous]
- Equestrian prefects were preferred over senators to reduce the danger of an independent rival controlling the Guard (introduction, EPUB lines 662–666). ^[ambiguous]
- Praetorians were privileged soldiers but also performed policing, administrative, construction, taxation, mint, and grain-supervision work (introduction, EPUB lines 670–674). ^[ambiguous]
- The Guard’s political leverage culminated in events such as the auction of the empire after Pertinax’s murder (sampled introduction; PDF pp. 32–35). ^[ambiguous]
- The 193 auction of imperial power illustrates the Guard’s ability to make and unmake emperors (PDF pp. 32–35). ^[ambiguous]
- Its origins lie in personal escorts of generals including Octavian and Antony during the civil wars (PDF pp. 36–46). ^[ambiguous]
- Augustus’ settlement institutionalized the Guard while placing command in equestrian rather than senatorial hands (PDF pp. 37–39). ^[ambiguous]
- Sejanus’ concentration of cohorts at Castra Praetoria in AD 23 transformed the Guard into a force capable of immediate Roman political intervention (PDF pp. 39–41). ^[ambiguous]
- Constantine disbanded the Guard after Maxentius’ defeat at the Milvian Bridge (PDF pp. 29–31). ^[ambiguous]
