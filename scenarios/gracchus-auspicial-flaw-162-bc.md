# The Consul Who Remembered His Own Auspicial Error a Year Too Late (163/162 BC)

In one of the most striking episodes in Driediger-Murphy's case for Romans taking augury as sincerely binding (rather than as elite political theater), the consul Ti. Sempronius Gracchus — father of the future tribunes — voluntarily reported, a year after the fact and while serving far away in his province, a ritual flaw in his own conduct of the previous year's consular elections. The report forced the sitting consuls he had helped elect to abdicate mid-term.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Ti. Sempronius Gracchus** ([[entities/tiberius-sempronius-gracchus-cos-177-163]]), cos. 163 and an augur — presided over the disputed election, initially resisted the haruspices' adverse ruling, then voluntarily reported his own error a year later.
- **P. Cornelius Scipio Nasica Corculum** ([[entities/publius-cornelius-scipio-nasica-corculum]]) and **C. Marcius Figulus** ([[entities/gaius-marcius-figulus]]), cos. 162 — elected under the flawed auspices; forced to abdicate once the error was confirmed.
- The College of Haruspices — initially consulted after an *in-religionem* incident during the election, and ruled Gracchus "not a lawful presiding officer" (*non iustus rogator*).
- The Senate and the College of Augurs — ultimately accepted Gracchus's self-report and ordered the new consuls to step down.

## What Happened

While presiding over the elections for 162 BC, Gracchus experienced an unsettling incident: the *primus rogator* (the citizen whose century voted first, setting the tone for the rest) died during the proceedings, an event the Roman public immediately took as a source of religious anxiety (*religio*). Gracchus referred the matter to the senate, which sought a formal ruling from the haruspices (ritual experts, not yet organized as a formal priestly college but recognized as authoritative consultants). The haruspices concluded that Gracchus had not been a legitimate presiding officer for the election — effectively declaring the resulting vote tainted. Gracchus, by Cicero's account visibly angered at being told he was "not lawful," rejected the ruling, and the senate took no further action for months. Then, sometime well into 162 BC, while serving as governor in his province (Sardinia, according to Cicero's own later account), Gracchus happened to be reading his own augural notebooks and suddenly recalled — on his own, unprompted — that he had in fact made a genuine ritual error (*vitio tabernaculum captum*, an improperly sited augural tent) during the election he had presided over the previous year. He wrote to the College of Augurs reporting his own recollected mistake. The augurs referred the matter to the senate, which this time required the sitting consuls — Scipio Nasica Corculum and Marcius Figulus, both elected under Gracchus's original, flawed auspices — to abdicate.

## Outcome

Both consuls for 162 BC abdicated mid-term because of an error their predecessor had, by his own unprompted admission, both committed and forgotten for roughly a year. Cicero cites the episode explicitly as proof that Roman magistrates took their own ritual obligations with total sincerity, independent of any political advantage — Gracchus gained nothing politically from confessing an error that could easily have stayed buried, and in fact had initially fought off an identical accusation from the haruspices only months earlier.

## Open Questions

- What specifically triggered Gracchus's recollection — described vaguely as rereading "the books" — is unclear; whether this refers to his personal augural notes or to a more formal ritual text is debated among the modern scholars Driediger-Murphy cites. ^[ambiguous]
- Modern scholarship is divided (a division Driediger-Murphy explicitly engages) on whether Gracchus's self-report reflects genuine religious conviction or a politically calculated maneuver whose true motive is lost to us; Driediger-Murphy argues for sincerity as the more economical reading of the evidence. ^[ambiguous]
