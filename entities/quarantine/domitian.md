# Domitian

Domitian was a Roman emperor of the late first century AD whose assassination was followed by attacks on his public memory. His posthumous reputation was shaped by a durable tradition of hostile writing. The discussion in *God on Earth* traces how fourth- and fifth-century pagan and Christian authors repeated an image of a cruel, luxurious, and avaricious tyrant already established after his death.

## Different traditions, a shared condemnation

Christian historians placed Domitian among persecuting emperors and compared him with Nero. Lactantius interpreted his violent death and the Senate’s attack on his memory as divine vengeance, making senatorial action an instrument of God’s justice. Eusebius likewise cast him as an heir to Nero’s hostility toward Christians, although he also transmitted Tertullian’s claim that Domitian soon abandoned persecution and recalled exiles.

Pagan authors could reach a similar moral judgment without Christian explanation. Eutropius compared him unfavourably with his father and brother and emphasized his dishonourable death and burial. Zosimus, writing around 507, continued the familiar catalogue of cruelty, luxury, and greed.

## The afterlife of an image

The chapter interprets this continuity as evidence that Domitian’s condemnation was established early, rather than slowly accumulating in later centuries. This is an account of historical memory, not proof that every allegation against him was true.

Medieval chronicles inherited the persecutor image. The twelfth-century *Kaiserchronik* called him an enemy of God, while later traditions amplified his association with evil. The following chapter expressly cautions that the systematic persecution implied by many medieval texts is unlikely. The distinction between remembered tyrant and recoverable policy remains essential.
