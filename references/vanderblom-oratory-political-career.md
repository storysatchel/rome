# Oratory and Political Career in the Late Roman Republic

Henriette van der Blom (University of Glasgow; previously Birmingham, Oxford) published this monograph with Cambridge University Press in 2016, building on her role as editorial board member and Research Fellow of the *Fragments of the Roman Republican Orators* (FRRO) project. The book investigates how far Roman politicians actually relied on oratorical performance to build political careers, deliberately moving the analysis away from Cicero — whose surviving speeches dominate the field — toward six other politicians: Gaius Gracchus, Pompeius Magnus, Julius Caesar, Lucius Calpurnius Piso Caesoninus, Cato the Younger, and Mark Antony (the triumvir).

## Core Argument / Thesis

Tacitus's *Dialogus de oratoribus* presents eloquence as a near-absolute necessity for public success under the Republic, a view inherited largely from Cicero's own self-presentation as the orator-statesman *par excellence*. Van der Blom argues this is unrepresentative: successful Republican careers were built through combinations of ancestry, wealth, patronage/networks, military achievement, personal charisma, *and* oratory, with no fixed hierarchy among these factors. Oratory mattered less for its technical brilliance than as part of a politician's broader "public profile" — the credible, consistently communicated self-presentation across the *contio*, Senate, and law courts that voters and peers used to judge a candidate's worthiness (*dignitas*).

## Key Claims and Evidence

- Marius, who won an unprecedented seven consulships, relied on military exploits and politicking rather than oratory, has no entry in Malcovati's *Oratorum Romanorum Fragmenta* and is excluded from Cicero's own history of orators (the *Brutus*) — yet surviving testimony shows he could be an effective speaker "when put on the spot" (e.g. silencing a riotous crowd after the murder of Memmius in 100 BC).
- Pompey, Piso Caesoninus, and Mark Antony were all "reactive" rather than active seekers of oratorical occasions — they spoke when magistracy required it but used other channels (visual spectacle, military reputation, patronage) to build their profiles — and none was designated a "good orator" by contemporaries or posterity, in contrast to Gracchus, Caesar, and Cato, who actively sought out speaking opportunities.
- The plebeian tribunate, never part of the formal *cursus honorum* and barred to patricians, offered near-unlimited access to oratorical occasions (the right to call a *contio* and present bills); candidates who sought it despite limited formal advancement prospects (especially after Sulla's reforms made it a political dead end until 70 BC) were typically motivated either by a wish to build an oratorical/future-praetor profile or, as with the Gracchi, genuine ideological commitment to popular causes.
- Van der Blom situates her study amid the ongoing historiographical debate over the *contio*'s political significance: Millar's "democratic" model (contional communication shows genuine popular power) versus Mouritsen's argument that the contional crowd was unrepresentative, versus Hölkeskamp's reading of the *contio* as symbolic elite-mass consensus display, versus Morstein-Marx's "ideological monotony" thesis (later partially revised by Morstein-Marx himself, per van der Blom's citation of his 2013 work).
- Even speeches delivered in the Senate (closed to non-senators) could leak out and shape public perception of an orator indirectly, meaning no venue was fully insulated from a politician's broader public image.
- Aulus Gellius's *Attic Nights* is identified as a crucial, non-political-focus source preserving apparently verbatim fragments of republican oratory (e.g. Cato the Elder, Gaius Gracchus, Caesar) that would otherwise be lost, precisely because Gellius's primary interest was linguistic/literary rather than historical.
- **Conclusion ("Towards a New Brutus")**: across all six case studies, the single most important factor for political success was not any one career ingredient but "the willingness and ability to communicate the various elements in a politician's public profile... in a credible, consistent and appealing manner." This openness of criteria (versus a strict oratory-only or ancestry-only gate) helps explain why so many *novi homines* could climb the *cursus honorum* despite elite advantages in wealth and networks. ^[inferred] (van der Blom's own explicit synthesis, offered as a candidate answer to a question left open by Hopkins and Burton's 1983 prosopographical study of consular access 250–50 BC.)
- Gaius Gracchus's case illustrates the limits of a single public profile: his broad "champion of the people" image concealed real divergence in interest between urban plebs (favored the grain law), rural plebs (favored the agrarian law but opposed the Italian enfranchisement bill), equestrians (favored jury reform), and Latins/Italians (wanted citizenship rights) — his failure to differentiate messaging to these sub-groups contributed to his political collapse.
- The volume's closing claim: if Cicero's *Brutus* (his history of Roman orators) were rewritten, the inclusion criterion would shift from oratorical ability to "the ability to project a coherent and exciting narrative about one's person, claim to political power and influence" — which would admit Piso, Cato, Antony, Sulla, Catiline, and Clodius alongside the orators Cicero already could not ignore (Gracchus, Pompey, Caesar).

## Structure / Scope

Covers the late Roman Republic from Gaius Gracchus's tribunates (123–122 BC) through Mark Antony's triumviral period (post-43 BC). Part I ("The Role of Oratory in Roman Politics") covers oratorical settings (courts, *contio*, Senate) and "other routes to political success" (ancestry, wealth, patronage, military exploits, charisma). Part II presents six case-study chapters, each built around a distinct theme: tribunician oratory and family inheritance (Gaius Gracchus); politics conducted "behind the scenes" (Pompey); oratory as career "springboard" (Caesar); Piso Caesoninus's largely non-oratorical career; "powerful profiling" via self-presentation (Cato the Younger); and career-making amid crisis (Mark Antony). Extensive appendices (observed: Appendix 1 Gracchus, Appendix 2 Pompey, Appendix 3 Caesar, Appendix 5 Cato, with Appendices 4 and 6 presumably covering Piso and Antony) tabulate every attested public speech by each figure with date, venue, topic, and full source citation — a research resource in its own right, drawing on and correcting Pina Polo's (1989) and Bonnefond-Coudry's (1989) earlier catalogues and Malcovati's (1976) fragment collection.

## Significance

The book explicitly positions itself as a corrective to Cicero-centrism in Republican oratory scholarship, arguing Cicero's career — beginning in the courts at the comparatively late age of thirty-six and continuing forensic advocacy even as a senior consular — is itself exceptional rather than paradigmatic. It is conceived as a companion to the FRRO project's aim of making non-Ciceronian oratory as accessible to scholars as Cicero's (uniquely fully preserved) speeches. It engages directly with Morstein-Marx's *Mass Oratory and Political Power* ([[references/morsteinmarx-mass-oratory]]) on the *contio* debate and shares contributors and source material (the FRRO project) with *Institutions and Ideology in Republican Rome* ([[references/vanderblom-gray-steel-institutions-ideology]]), of which van der Blom is co-editor.

## Open Questions

- Van der Blom acknowledges the "bad orator" (*malus orator*) designation (following Wisse 2013) was subjective and worked on a sliding scale, making it genuinely difficult to determine how poor an orator's technical skill had to be before it measurably hurt his political prospects. ^[ambiguous]
- The book does not fully resolve whether increasing attention to rhetorical training in the 90s–80s BC (first Latin rhetoric handbooks, closure of the first Latin rhetoric school, rise of study trips to the Greek East) reflects a genuine rise in oratory's political importance or a parallel but independently caused trend (e.g. the consulship's shift from a military to a civil magistracy after Sulla, per Pina Polo).
