# Collegium Augurum

The *Collegium Augurum* (College of Augurs) was the Roman priestly body entrusted with interpreting the will of Jupiter regarding proposed public actions. Unlike the pontiffs, who managed sacred law and the mechanics of ritual, augurs were divine intermediaries who determined whether the gods approved or disapproved of specific civic, legislative, or military undertakings. Holding life-long tenure, the augurs possessed formidable constitutional veto power over popular assemblies, consular elections, and statutory legislation through the announcement of adverse omens (*obnuntiatio*).

```
[ Auguraculum (Arx / Capitoline) ]
               │
               ▼  (Delimits Templum in Caelo via Lituus)
[ Augural Observation ]
  ├── Auspicia Impetrativa (Solicited Signs: Feeding Chickens, Flight of Birds)
  └── Auspicia Oblativa (Unsolicited Omens: Lightning, Thunder, Prodigies)
               │
               ▼
   [ Constitutional Intervention ]
  ├── Obnuntiatio (Declaration of Unfavorable Sign ──► Disperses Assembly)
  ├── Vitium Discovery (Ritual Flaw ──► Voids Elections / Abrogates Laws)
  └── Inauguratio (Permanent Sacred Sanctification of Priests and Places)
```

## Structure, Membership, and the *Lituus*

According to Roman tradition, Romulus created the first three augurs, representing the original archaic tribes (*Ramnes*, *Tities*, *Luceres*). 
- **Expansion of the College:** In 300 BCE, the plebeian agitation culminated in the *Lex Ogulnia*, which expanded the college from four to nine members, mandating that five seats be reserved for plebeians. Sulla increased the college to fifteen in 81 BCE, and Julius Caesar raised the number to sixteen in 44 BCE. Under the empire, emperors co-opted supernumerary members at will.
- **Insignia and Vestments:** The augurs wore the *trabea*, an archaic ceremonial mantle bordered with purple stripes and scarlet bands. Their definitive badge of office was the *lituus*, a curved, knotless wooden staff used to demarcate quadrants in the sky during ritual observation.
- **Physical Observation Posts:** The premier official observation sanctuary was the *Auguraculum* on the northern peak of the Capitoline Hill (*Arx*). A secondary auguraculum stood on the Quirinal Hill, and temporary auguracula were established in the Campus Martius or military camps prior to battles or voting assemblies.

## Theology and Mechanics of Augury

Roman augury did not predict the future; rather, it ascertained whether Jupiter granted immediate divine favor (*fas*) or prohibition (*nefas*) to a designated undertaking. 

Augural signs were categorized across two fundamental axes:
1. **Type of Inquirer:**
   - *Auguria:* Permanent sanctifications of people (e.g., inauguration of the *Rex Sacrorum* or *Flamen Dialis*) or sacred ground (*loca inaugurata* such as the Curia Hostilia, the Rostra, or the Comitium). Once inaugurated by an augur, a space became a *templum*, permitting lawful senatorial or magisterial deliberations.
   - *Auspicia:* Ephemeral, action-specific divine signs solicited prior to convening an assembly, crossing the pomerium, departing for provincial commands, or initiating military battle.
2. **Method of Manifestation:**
   - *Auspicia Impetrativa:* Signs actively solicited through prescribed ritual inquiries. The primary form for assemblies and civil business was *ex avibus* (observing the flight paths of *alites* such as eagles and vultures, or the vocalizations of *oscines* such as ravens, crows, and owls). For military commanders in the field, *ex tripudiis* (observing the feeding behavior of sacred chickens, *pulli sacri*) was customary: if the chickens ate so ravenously that grain dropped from their beaks to the ground (*tripudium solistimum*), the omen was auspicious.
   - *Auspicia Oblativa:* Unsolicited, spontaneous divine manifestations, such as lightning, thunder peals, sudden swoops of birds, or epileptic seizures among the crowd (*morbus comitialis*), demanding immediate suspension of civic business.

To observe solicited signs, the augur sat within the auguraculum facing south or east. Delimiting the celestial vault with his *lituus*, he divided the visible sky into four quadrants (left/east representing favorable signs, right/west representing unfavorable signs).

## Constitutional Power: *Obnuntiatio* and Assembly Vetoes

The College of Augurs possessed unique institutional mechanisms capable of halting the machinery of the Roman state:
- **`Obnuntiatio` (The Announcement of Unfavorable Omens):** If an augur or magistrate with auspicial rights (*ius auspiciorum*) observed or declared an adverse sky-sign (*de caelo servare*), he announced it via *obnuntiatio*. The phrase *"alio die"* ("on another day") immediately dissolved the assembly, dispersing voters and preventing the passage of bills or election of magistrates.
- **`Vitium` (Ritual Flaw) and Invalidation:** The college held retrospective authority to investigate whether past civic acts had been tainted by ritual flaws (*vitia*). If an augural inquiry determined that a magistrate had crossed the pomerium without proper auspices or had misreported signs, the Senate issued a decree commanding the magistrate to abdicate.
- **Historical Clashes:**
  - In 162 BCE, the consul Tiberius Sempronius Gracchus realized months into his campaign that he had improperly pitched his auspicial tent when crossing the pomerium. Gracchus informed the College of Augurs from Sardinia; the college declared the election tainted by a *vitium*, compelling both sitting consuls to abdicate.
  - In 59 BCE, during Julius Caesar's consulship, his conservative colleague Marcus Calpurnius Bibulus retreated to his house and announced that he was "watching the skies" (*servare de caelo*) every voting day to block Caesar's agrarian and fiscal legislation. Caesar ignored the obnuntiatio and rammed the laws through the popular assembly. Following Caesar's consulship, conservative senators used Bibulus's continuous obnuntiatio to challenge the legal validity of all Julian legislation (*Leges Iuliae*).
  - In 44 BCE, Mark Antony, acting as both consul and augur, attempted to prevent the election of Publius Cornelius Dolabella as suffect consul by shouting *"alio die"* during the assembly, triggering months of bitter legal and theological debate with Cicero (*Philippic* 2.80–84).

## Augurs as Political Actors

Cicero, who was co-opted into the College of Augurs in 53 BCE, described the priesthood in *De Legibus* (2.31) as the highest and most authoritative office in the Republic, emphasizing that augurs could grant or deny permissions to hold assemblies, dismiss voting bodies, compel consuls to lay down their imperium, and revoke statutory laws passed in violation of ritual norms.

Because augurs were appointed for life and could not be stripped of their augural dignity even if convicted of high crimes or banished, patrician and plebeian nobiles competed fiercely for vacancies. Factions within the Senate routinely used friendly augurs to stonewall reformist tribunes, filibuster elections, or postpone military levies.

## Transition to the Principate

Under Augustus, the constitutional independence of the augural college was curtailed. Augustus himself became an augur in 41 BCE, using augural theology to legitimize his autocratic rule (notably through the ancient *Augurium Salutis*, a ritual inquiry into whether the gods permitted prayers for the safety of the Roman people, enacted in 29 BCE to celebrate the restoration of peace). While the college continued to meet throughout the imperial period, ratifying honors, performing inaugurations of temples, and participating in imperial banquets, its independent constitutional veto was neutralized, operating henceforth under the direct patronage and direction of the emperor.
