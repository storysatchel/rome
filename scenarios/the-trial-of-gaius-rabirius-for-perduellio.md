# The Trial of Gaius Rabirius for Perduellio (63 BC)

Some forty years after the violent suppression of Saturninus's revolt in 100 BC, Titus Labienus accused the now-elderly former senator Gaius Rabirius of personally killing Labienus's uncle, one of Saturninus's followers, during that episode. The charge revived the archaic procedure of *perduellio* ("treason" in a specifically military-religious sense), which had largely been superseded in practice by the newer charge of *maiestas*.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Titus Labienus** ([[entities/titus-labienus]]) — goal: obtain a conviction avenging his uncle's death decades earlier; resources: tribunician office, the revived archaic *perduellio* procedure.
- **Gaius Rabirius** ([[entities/gaius-rabirius]]) — goal: avoid conviction and execution by crucifixion; resources: elderly status, Cicero as his defense counsel.
- **Cicero** ([[entities/cicero]]) — goal: successfully defend Rabirius and, implicitly, defend the legitimacy of the Senate's emergency powers (*senatus consultum ultimum*) used against Saturninus; resources: oratorical skill.
- **Julius Caesar and Lucius Julius Caesar** ([[entities/julius-caesar]], [[entities/lucius-julius-caesar-duumvir]]) — appointed as special commissioners (*duumviri*) to try the case under the ancient procedure; possible motive: demonstrate the limits of the *senatus consultum ultimum* against sitting legislators, though that political purpose is conjectural.
- **Quintus Caecilius Metellus Celer** ([[entities/metellus-celer]]) — role: as presiding magistrate over the symbolic military flag on the [[entities/janiculum|Janiculum]], dissolved the assembly before the death sentence could be ratified by pulling the flag down.

## What Happened

Cicero's surviving defense speech argues that the chaos of 100 BC had left [[entities/rome|Rome]]'s safety in serious danger, that Saturninus's own treasonable activities were to blame, and that hiding or inaction at the time would have been tantamount to complicity. Despite this, Cicero's eloquence had little effect: Rabirius was condemned and sentenced to death by crucifixion. ^[extracted] However, just as the people were about to ratify the verdict, Metellus Celer pulled down the military flag flying over the [[entities/janiculum|Janiculum]] hill — a traditional signal whose removal automatically dissolved the assembly. ^[extracted] Julius Caesar, one of the two commissioners appointed to try the archaic charge, may have orchestrated the trial to demonstrate that the Senate's emergency decree (*senatus consultum ultimum*) could not simply be used to eliminate legislators who had pushed through measures the Senate opposed. This remains a conjectural political explanation. ^[inferred]

## Outcome

The trial never resumed after the flag-lowering dissolved the assembly, and Rabirius was never formally convicted. His ultimate fate is not recorded in the surviving sources.

## Open Questions

- Whether Metellus Celer's flag-lowering was a spontaneous procedural technicality or a pre-arranged maneuver to save Rabirius remains unresolved here. ^[ambiguous]
- Rabirius's fate after the trial's collapse is explicitly unknown.
- Whether Caesar orchestrated the trial as a political demonstration against the *senatus consultum ultimum*, or Labienus pursued a more straightforward prosecution, remains uncertain. ^[ambiguous]
