# The Republican Roman Army: A Sourcebook

Michael M. Sage (University of Cincinnati) published this sourcebook with Routledge in 2008 as part of the Routledge Sourcebooks for the Ancient World series. Rather than a monograph with a connected argument, it is a curated anthology of translated ancient testimony — many passages newly translated and not previously available in English — on the Roman army from the regal period to the end of the Republic, each excerpt framed with contextualizing commentary.

## Core Argument / Thesis

As a sourcebook, this work has no single thesis; instead its organizing principle is editorial selection and commentary. Sage's stated goal in the Introduction is "to bring together relevant ancient testimony on various aspects of the Roman army and its relation to Roman society to the end of the Republic," adding commentary "to place these passages in context and to provide some of the insights offered by recent scholarship on these problems." The selection favors passages that illuminate institutional mechanics (recruitment, organization, pay, camp life, discipline) and the "war and society" relationship between army and polity, following what Sage calls a post-1978 scholarly shift (triggered by John Keegan's *The Face of Battle*) away from pure tactics/strategy and toward recreating the lived experience of battle and the army's social embedding. ## Key Claims and Evidence

- The ancient source base requires caution: Livy and Dionysius of Halicarnassus (writing c. the turn of the millennium) anachronistically project the 3rd/2nd-century army back onto the Regal period and early Republic; Polybius alone, as both a contemporary witness and a former Achaean *hipparchus*, provides a technically-informed account (Histories Book 6) that is "fundamental to any description of the Roman army of this period."
- Polybius's own account has a documented blind spot: he never mentions the cohort as a tactical unit, likely because his personal exposure to it (accompanying Scipio to Spain in 151/0) came during a siege-dominated campaign that showcased the cohort poorly, and because Book 6 was probably drafted before that Spanish trip.
- The antiquarian tradition (chiefly M. Terentius Varro, 1st century BC, surviving only in fragments) provides a particularly valuable channel for understanding Republican military institutional development, since narrative historians avoided technical vocabulary that antiquarians embraced.
- Military pay is dated, following Livy and Diodorus Siculus, to the final war against Veii (Livy's date: 406 BC) — introduced not as pay in a modern sense but as compensation enabling the lowest-qualifying census classes to serve, functioning as a manpower-expansion mechanism under heavy military pressure rather than a straightforward plebeian concession.
- *Tributum* is characterized as a mandatory loan (not a formal tax) tied specifically to active campaigning — in peaceful years like 347 BC it was not levied, and it was occasionally repaid from war booty — ending altogether in 167 BC once conquest-derived revenue replaced it.
- The Polybian marching-camp description (Histories 6.27–32) is cross-checked against the Numantia-area Spanish camps (Renieblas, including Q. Fulvius Nobilior's 153 BC winter camp and Scipio Aemilianus's 133 BC siege camps), which broadly confirm but also complicate Polybius's model — e.g., one archaeological reconstruction suggests Polybius's "two-legion camp" description actually represents half of a four-legion camp.
- Three types of Roman marching formation are distinguished (standard column, parallel-column "danger" formation, and defensive square), with Sallust's Metellus-in-Numidia and Caesar's Belgic campaign cited as evidence that formations were adapted flexibly to the nature of the threatening force (e.g., light cavalry vs. infantry).
- Two concrete incidents drawn from the quoted ancient testimony: Plutarch's account of M. Claudius Marcellus's single-combat killing of the Insubrian king Viridomarus at Clastidium in 222 BC, earning Rome's third-ever *spolia opima* (see [[scenarios/duel-of-marcellus-and-viridomarus-at-clastidium]]); and the praetor Gaius Lucretius's 171 BC sack of Haliartus, a precursor to his later conviction for abuses against Rome's Greek allies (see [[scenarios/siege-and-sack-of-haliartus]]).

## Structure / Scope

Three chronological chapters move from "Rome's Earliest Armies: The Archaic and Servian Period" (political/social institutions, the archaic army's structure, the *gens*/clan as a fighting unit, the hoplite phalanx's introduction via Etruria) through "The Development of the Manipular Army" (the manipular reform, Roman allies, the army in battle, changes in census qualifications) to "The Army of the Late Republic" (the cohort's rise as the tactical unit, booty, military honors and punishments, conditions of service, battle, Roman imperialism, siege warfare, and naval warfare). Sources quoted include Varro, Plutarch, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Livy, Polybius, Aulus Gellius, Caesar, Sallust, and the Lapis Satricanus inscription, each given in translation with Sage's framing commentary.

## Significance

Sage positions the book within a specifically post-1978 scholarly turn toward "war and society" framing, crediting Keegan's *The Face of Battle* (1978) as the originating influence for treating battle narrative as a vehicle for recreating lived experience across centuries of evidence (he singles out the Second Punic War and Caesar's campaigns as uniquely rich for this kind of reconstruction). Armstrong and Fronda's *Romans at War* later cites Sage's commentary apparatus as substantial enough that the sourcebook "contains enough exegesis to warrant being included as a study in its own right" — i.e., contemporary scholarship treats this volume as functionally a secondary synthesis, not merely a raw-text anthology. ## Open Questions

- Because the book is an anthology rather than an argument, it does not resolve the debates it surfaces (e.g., the Servian army's size and composition, the authenticity of the early *tributum* date, the correct multiplication of Fabius Pictor's 225 BC manpower figures) — these are presented with competing scholarly positions rather than a settled conclusion. ^[ambiguous]
- The sample read for this page concentrated on the archaic/Servian chapter and the late-Republic march/camp/pay sections; the manipular-army-in-battle and Roman-imperialism/siege-warfare/naval-warfare sections (pp. 150–297 per the table of contents) were not examined in comparable depth, so claims specific to those sections are not represented here. ^[ambiguous]
