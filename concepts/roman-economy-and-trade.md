# Roman Economy and Trade

The Roman economic-history debate, long polarized between "primitivist" (exploitative, undeveloped) and "modernist" (integrated market, proto-capitalist) readings. Bang's comparative "bazaar" model offers a specific third framework; Scarre's atlas gives the trade geography that framework describes.

## Key Ideas

- **Neither primitivist nor modernist:** Bang argues both camps project anachronistic frameworks onto Rome — the primitivist view (Finley) sees only exploitation, the modernist view (Rostovtzeff) imagines something close to a laissez-faire market with a cosmopolitan bourgeoisie. Bang finds elements of both already present in Roman self-description (Aelius Aristeides' 2nd-century panegyric to Rome depicts the city simultaneously as a liberal magnet for the world's goods and an all-commanding tributary power) and argues for a framework that holds both in tension rather than resolving them.
- **The comparative method: Mughal India, not early modern Europe.** Bang's central methodological move is replacing the implicit early-modern-European benchmark (behind most "modernization narrative" readings of Rome) with 17th-century Mughal India — a large pre-industrial agrarian empire that, like Rome, extracted surplus from peasant populations via tribute rather than developing a competitive inter-state market system. The comparison is licensed by shared political-economic structure, not cultural resemblance.
- **The "bazaar" model of exchange:** using Clifford Geertz's anthropology of bazaar economies, Bang characterizes Roman markets as opaque, volatile, and only discontinuously connected — not an integrated price system. Merchants managed this endemic uncertainty through *clientelization*: cultivating personal, durable trust relationships with specific trading partners, because an impersonal, generalized "market" was largely unavailable to them.
- **Elite wealth stayed agrarian:** Roman landowners kept the economic upper hand over commercial middlemen (shifting market risk onto merchants via contract convention), and elite social mobility tracked access to agricultural land, not commercial fortune — unlike the Dutch Republic, where merchant capital could rival and fund the landed/political elite.
- **The physical geography of this economy:** Scarre's 2nd-century trade map shows the concrete infrastructure Bang's abstract model describes — named sea routes with sailing times (Alexandria–Puteoli, 15–20 days), commodity-production zones (wine, oil, grain, metals) distributed across the provinces, and long-distance connections east via the Silk Route (to Serica/China) and Indian Ocean sea routes (to Muziris and Taprobane/Sri Lanka, trading for incense and spices). Diocletian's Edict of Prices (301 AD) — cheaper to ship grain from Spain to Syria by sea than move it 75 miles overland — is concrete evidence for exactly the kind of cost-driven, geographically discontinuous trade pattern Bang's "bazaar" model predicts rather than a single uniform price system. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- Bang's book was sampled only in its framing chapters (Prolegomena, early Ch. 1, Epilegomena); the detailed Part II evidence on customs administration (*portorium*) and the social/legal infrastructure of exchange (cult, courts, credit — Ch. 5) was not sampled, so this page cannot yet detail the *mechanisms* of clientelization beyond the concept itself.
- Neither source directly states whether Scarre's concrete trade-route data would support or complicate Bang's "discontinuous, opaque markets" characterization — this page treats them as complementary (abstract model + physical map) rather than testing one against the other, which is itself an inference this wiki has not verified against primary evidence. ^[ambiguous]
