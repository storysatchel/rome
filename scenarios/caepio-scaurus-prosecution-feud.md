# The Caepio–Scaurus Mutual Prosecution Feud (92/91 BC)

Two of the Late Republic's most senior and bitterest rivals, Q. Servilius Caepio and M. Aemilius Scaurus, each accused the other of extortion (*repetundae*) in the same short window of late 92 to early 91 BC — and each tried to manipulate the courts' scheduling rules to see his own rival tried first.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Q. Servilius Caepio** ([[entities/quintus-servilius-caepio-pr-91]]), pr. 91? — prosecuted Scaurus for extortion; was in turn prosecuted by Scaurus.
- **M. Aemilius Scaurus** ([[entities/marcus-aemilius-scaurus-cos-115]]), cos. 115, cens. 109 — one of the Republic's most powerful senior statesmen (princeps senatus); prosecuted by Caepio, and counter-prosecuted him in return.
- **Cn. Cornelius Dolabella** (pr. 81) and **M. Iunius Brutus** — joined Caepio as co-prosecutors of Scaurus.

## What Happened

The personal and political animosity between Caepio and Scaurus — rooted partly in the aftermath of the disastrous 105 BC defeat at [[entities/arausio|Arausio]], for which Caepio's father had been condemned in absentia years earlier — boiled over into dueling extortion prosecutions. Caepio, joined by Dolabella and possibly Brutus, charged Scaurus with extortion. Scaurus retaliated by charging Caepio with the same offense. Because Roman procedure allowed each side to request a shorter or longer period for *inquisitio* (evidence-gathering) before trial, Scaurus — despite being sued first — maneuvered to have Caepio's trial (in which Scaurus was prosecutor) scheduled and decided before his own trial (in which he was the defendant) came up. Both trials appear to have reached a formal second hearing (*actio*), implying verdicts were actually rendered rather than one side simply abandoning the case.

## Outcome

Both men were probably acquitted, or at least neither case is recorded as resulting in exile or conviction — Scaurus went on to remain politically active, and Caepio saw further military service in the subsequent Social War, which would have been impossible after a condemnation. The episode is remembered less for its legal outcome than as a vivid illustration of how Rome's extortion courts could be weaponized as instruments of purely personal vendetta between senior statesmen, with procedural gamesmanship over trial scheduling treated as a legitimate tactical weapon.

## Open Questions

- Whether either trial in fact reached a verdict, or whether one or both were simply dropped once the underlying political feud lost urgency, is not resolved by the surviving citations (a fragment of Charisius could belong to either case). ^[ambiguous]
