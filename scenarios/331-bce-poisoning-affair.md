# The 331 BCE Poisoning Affair — Mass Trial of Roman Matrons

In 331 BCE, during a period in which many Romans were dying of an unexplained illness, a slave informed authorities that a group of twenty Roman matrons — both patrician and plebeian — were preparing and distributing substances (*venena*) they called remedies (*medicamenta salubria*) but which the slave alleged were in fact poisons. Further investigation, prompted by the attendants of other households coming forward, implicated the mistresses of another 170 women, all of whom were ultimately found guilty of the same crime. Livy, recording the episode in his *History of Rome*, explicitly questions the veracity of the official account and notes that other ancient authorities doubted it too — leaving open whether the matrons were guilty of deliberate murder, had produced genuinely medicinal preparations that killed inadvertently, or were innocent women whose remedies were simply misidentified as poisons once the Latin term *venenum* (which covered both "poison" and "remedy") was read in the worst light.

## Parties & Stakes

- **The informant slave** — testified against an initial group of twenty matrons, triggering the wider investigation; identity otherwise unrecorded.
- **The ~190 accused Roman matrons** (unnamed as individuals in the surviving account) — accused of mixing and administering *venena*, blurring the line this wiki's [[concepts/roman-domestic-life|domestic-life]] material draws between medicine and poison; ultimately convicted collectively.
- **Roman authorities conducting the investigation** — part of the Republic's early [[concepts/roman-law-and-trials|trial and investigation apparatus]]; expanded the inquiry from the original twenty to encompass the attendants' further accusations against 170 more households.

## What Happened

The affair unfolded against a backdrop of an unexplained spike in deaths at Rome. A household slave's testimony that her mistress's circle of matrons were preparing poisonous concoctions under the guise of medicine set off a cascading investigation: once the first twenty were implicated, attendants (likely enslaved household staff) belonging to 170 further matrons came forward with similar accusations against their own mistresses, expanding the affair to roughly 190 accused women across the patrician and plebeian elite. One possible reading of the episode emphasizes the structural ambiguity of Roman domestic medicine: the same Latin vocabulary (*venenum*, qualified only by *bonum* or *malum*, "good" or "bad") covered medicinal remedies, poisons, and magical substances alike, so that an accusation of poisoning and a claim of healing could rest on the identical physical evidence.

## Outcome

All of the accused matrons were found guilty, per the surviving tradition, though Livy himself flags doubt about whether this was a genuine mass poisoning conspiracy or a panic-driven overreach amid an epidemic later rationalized as a crime. ^[ambiguous] No individual sentences or subsequent fates are recorded in the summarized account.

## Open Questions

- Whether the underlying deaths were caused by poisoning, disease, or a combination, and whether the "confession"/conviction reflects genuine guilt or a coerced/constructed narrative, is explicitly disputed by Livy and remains unresolved. ^[ambiguous]
- The fate of the convicted matrons after judgment is not specified in the source material consulted.
