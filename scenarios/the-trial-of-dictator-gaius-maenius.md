# The Trial of the Dictator Gaius Maenius for Abuse of Office

In 314 BC C. Maenius was appointed dictator with the specific mandate of investigating conspiracies among the Campanians. Instead of resigning once that task was complete, he turned his dictatorial powers inward, using the opportunity to investigate alleged plots by prominent Romans to obtain magistracies by fraudulent means — an overreach that triggered a political storm among the very aristocrats he targeted.

## Parties & Stakes

- **C. Maenius** ([[entities/gaius-maenius-dictator]]) — dictator of 314 BC; goal: (per Livy) root out corrupt scheming for office among the nobility; resources: dictatorial *imperium*, freedom from ordinary magisterial checks during his term.
- **M. Folius** ([[entities/marcus-folius-magister-equitum]]) — Maenius's *magister equitum* (master of the horse); goal: support the dictator's investigation; resources: his office as the dictator's deputy.
- **Complaining Roman aristocrats** — goal: stop the dictator's investigation into their own conduct and remove him from office; resources: collective political pressure and complaints lodged with the senate/consuls.

## What Happened

After concluding his original commission regarding [[entities/campania|Campania]], Maenius did not lay down his dictatorship as expected; instead he investigated supposed conspiracies among prominent Romans to win magistracies through illicit means. This provoked numerous complaints from the aristocrats under scrutiny, who pressed for his removal. Under mounting pressure, Maenius — before resigning — delivered a vehement speech in his own defense, addressing the consuls directly and requesting that if the senate wished to pursue a further investigation into the matter, it do so properly rather than through a dictator who had exceeded his mandate. ^[extracted] Both Maenius and his magister equitum Folius were ultimately forced to resign. ^[extracted] Livy himself notes the episode's historicity was already contested in antiquity. ^[ambiguous]

## Outcome

Maenius and Folius resigned the dictatorship and mastership of the horse; no further formal trial of Maenius is attested in the surviving tradition, suggesting that political pressure rather than a judicial verdict ended the affair.

## Open Questions

- The episode's historicity is strongly contested: it may be an annalistic invention projecting later political anxieties about dictatorial overreach onto the early Republic. ^[ambiguous]
