# Roman Religion and Augury

Roman public religion centered on divination through augury — reading bird-signs, sky-signs, and other prodigies to validate or block proposed public acts — and operated through both formal priestly colleges and dedicated religious tribunals.

## Key Ideas

- Public augury included signs with lasting effects that "inaugurated" people or places (*auguria*) and action-specific signs for a proposed act (*auspicia*). The latter could arrive spontaneously (*auspicia oblativa*) or through ritual solicitation (*auspicia impetrativa*).
- Jupiter was consulted via augurs or magistrates before nearly every major act of Roman public life — creating priests, electing magistrates, holding assemblies, passing legislation, crossing the *pomerium*, crossing rivers with an army, or joining battle — making augury load-bearing infrastructure for the entire Republican political system, not a peripheral ritual.
- Cicero's *De Legibus* 2.31 describes the augurs' formal powers to adjourn or nullify assemblies, invalidate laws, and compel a consul's abdication. Cicero, himself an augur from 53/52 BC, celebrates these powers; his testimony combines institutional description with advocacy.
- The interpretation of divine signs is contested. One reading treats magistrates and augurs as instrumentally "creating" signs to manage political outcomes; an alternative emphasizes belief that augury genuinely expressed and responded to Jupiter's will. Religious conviction and political advantage need not have been mutually exclusive.
- Religious authority also extended into adjudication: the *quaestio apud pontifices*, distinct from standing criminal courts, tried Vestal Virgins for *incestum* in 114 BC. See [[concepts/roman-law-and-trials]] for this sacral jurisdiction within the broader court system.
- A case from 215 BC links augury and elite rivalry. A thunderclap during consul-elect Marcellus's inauguration led to a ruling that his election was religiously flawed and his resignation in favour of [[entities/clan-fabius|Fabius Maximus]] (Cunctator), a fellow augur and newly made pontiff. Their rivalry involved the Fabii's association with the cavalry-patron god Honos, asserted since 304 BC and renewed by Fabius's dedication in 233 BC of the Temple of [[entities/temple-of-honos|Honos]]. Marcellus's vow at Clastidium in 222 BC of a joint Honos-and-[[entities/temple-of-virtus|Virtus]] temple could appear an encroachment. Whether the election episode reflects religious belief, political manipulation, or both remains unresolved; these motives were "not mutually exclusive." ^[inferred]
- Cicero's elite, philosophically self-conscious account may not represent the beliefs or daily practice of all magistrates. ^[ambiguous]

## Open Questions

- The degree to which augury functioned as religious conviction, political instrument, or both remains contested; the disagreement is not adjudicated here. ^[ambiguous]
- Detailed procedures such as *tripudium* and *obnuntiatio*, and the Bibulus affair of 59 BC, require separate treatment beyond this synthesis.
