# The Mob Killing of the Tribune Publius Furius Mid-Trial (99/98 BC)

Around 99 or 98 BC, the former tribune of the plebs P. Furius was put on trial before the popular assembly for his conduct in office the previous year — and never received a verdict, because a crowd killed him before the trial could conclude (Alexander, case #79).

## Parties & Stakes

- **P. Furius** ([[entities/publius-furius-tribune]]), tr. pl. 100 or 99 — defendant, accused of misconduct as tribune; killed by a mob before judgment.
- **C. Appuleius Decianus** ([[entities/gaius-appuleius-decianus]]), tr. pl. 99 or 98 — one of (possibly two) prosecutors.
- **C. Canuleius** ([[entities/gaius-canuleius]]), tr. pl. 99? — possibly a second, separate prosecutor (the sources disagree on whether there was one prosecution by two men or two separate prosecutions).

## What Happened

Furius, prosecuted for his actions during his own tribunate (likely connected to the violent politics surrounding the tribune Saturninus and his allies in the previous years), was brought before the people on a charge serious enough that his life was at stake. Before the *iudicium populi* reached its formal conclusion, a crowd attacked and killed him. Ancient accounts differ on the precise mechanics: Valerius Maximus states he was never formally condemned; Appian says he was killed by a mob while still under indictment; Cassius Dio places the killing in the assembly itself. The most economical reconstruction, per Alexander, is that the mob acted during the trial itself, before any verdict was reached.

## Outcome

Furius died without ever being formally convicted or acquitted — popular violence substituted for, and preempted, the judicial process. The episode is one of several in this decade (alongside the deaths of Saturninus and his allies a few years earlier) illustrating how thin the line had become between formal *iudicia populi* and mob action in the turbulent aftermath of the Saturninus affair.

## Open Questions

- Whether there was one joint prosecution by Decianus and Canuleius together, or two separate prosecutions (the first perhaps ending in an acquittal before a second, fatal one began), is disputed among the ancient sources Alexander cites. ^[ambiguous]
