# Rome and the Mediterranean 290 to 146 BC: The Imperial Republic

Nathan Rosenstein's volume in the Edinburgh History of Ancient Rome series (Edinburgh University Press, 2012) covers Rome's transformation from a regional Italian hegemon in 290 BC to Mediterranean dominance by 146 BC, the year of Carthage's and Corinth's destruction. The organizing question is how that conquest became possible; Polybius’s question — "by what means...was almost the whole of the inhabited world conquered" — provides an ancient point of departure, not an uncontested explanation of Roman government.

## Core Argument / Thesis

Rome’s conquest admits no single-cause explanation. An aristocratic reading of Republican government challenges Polybius’s "mixed constitution" model, while competing explanations of expansion emphasize either an exceptionally war-driven, profit-seeking elite or the competitive pressures of "interstate anarchy" among comparably bellicose states. A further reconstruction centers on allied manpower (*socii* bound by the *formula togatorum* and *foedera*) and resilient agrarian households: together these could sustain simultaneous wars without the systemic dispossession of small farmers or dependence on plunder as the primary financing mechanism. This is a demographic and institutional interpretation, not an uncontested replacement for all competing models. [[concepts/roman-provincial-administration]]

## Key Claims and Evidence

- Estimated growth of directly controlled territory (*ager Romanus*) from roughly 38,600 km² after Sentinum (294 BC) to nearly 100,000 km² by 225 BC, together with an allied (*socii*) population estimated at some 725,000 by 290 BC, suggests a demographic base capable of supporting sustained large-scale warfare.
- *Imperium* in the 3rd–2nd centuries meant "hegemony" or "dominion," not a territorial empire in the modern sense; the degree of subordination it implied varied hugely, from the nearly untouched Illyrian tribes to the formal treaty-bound Italian *socii*, whom Rome demanded military manpower from (via the *formula togatorum*) but did not tax in money or interfere with internally.
- Polybius’s Fabius Pictor-derived 225 BC manpower table gives Rome and its allies roughly 580,000 infantry and 54,000 cavalry. Compared with Alexander the Great’s invasion army of fewer than 60,000, these reported totals illustrate the alliance system’s potential scale advantage; they are not a direct count of forces deployed together.
- Small farms may have survived sustained military service through flexible marriage timing, widespread small-scale slaveholding and women’s agricultural labor. This reconstruction challenges the older model in which conscription systematically dispossessed Roman and Italian farmers, enriching senatorial owners of slave-staffed *latifundia*.
- Model-based estimates for 200–168 BC exceed 100,000 combat deaths plus 75,000–100,000 disease deaths among legionaries and allied *socii*, implying annual mortality of 4.75–5.45% and a non-return rate of 34–40% among those serving. A disputed inference is that earlier inheritance, improved land access and favorable marriage markets could have materially benefited surviving rural families despite these losses. ^[inferred]
- Urban growth at Rome (roughly 200,000 c. 200 BC to 375,000–500,000 by mid-century) may have owed more to construction employment and festival or spectacle life than to dispossessed farmers. One supporting calculation assigns wine and oil supply only about 0.5% of Italy’s arable land and 35,000–70,000 slave workers, too small a footprint within this model to explain mass displacement.
- The 186 BC Bacchanalian Conspiracy suppression is read not primarily as religious persecution but as senatorial anxiety about any organization operating outside aristocratic oversight, compounded by genuine uncertainty in the evidence about the cult's actual scale and intentions. ^[ambiguous]
- Two tribunes of the plebs successfully prosecuted the praetor who had commanded the fleet during the abuses against Greek allies in 171 BC, securing conviction, exile and a fine. This episode supplies a specific case behind the label "new brutality"; compare the Sage sourcebook treatment of Haliartus in [[scenarios/siege-and-sack-of-haliartus]] and the trial-procedure page [[scenarios/the-trial-of-gaius-lucretius]].

## Structure / Scope

Seven chapters move chronologically and thematically: an introductory survey of the mid-Republican aristocracy and Roman constitution (magistrates, priesthoods, popular assemblies including the wealth-weighted *comitia centuriata*); Rome's wars with Pyrrhus and Carthage; the mechanics of *imperium* and the allied army system; the Hannibalic War; the conquest of Gaul, Greece, and Spain; a chapter on "the new brutality" of later 2nd-century warfare; and a concluding chapter on the social and economic impact of empire. Primary source reliance centers on Polybius (for the constitution and the Hannibalic War) and Livy (for narrative detail, especially via Table 1's reconstruction of the *comitia centuriata*'s wealth-weighted voting structure), supplemented by numismatic evidence (coin reverses illustrating magistrates, deities, the *corvus*) and archaeological material (the Numantia camps, battle-site plans for Trebia, Trasimene, Cannae, Ilipa, New Carthage).

## Significance

Italian alliance mechanics and agrarian demography offer a bridge between explanations centered on Roman exceptionalism and those centered on interstate competition, without fully endorsing either. The demographic and economic reconstruction continues an approach developed in *Rome at War* (2004). Its relation to the broader "war and society" literature is illustrated by [[references/armstrong-fronda-romans-at-war|*Romans at War: Soldiers, Citizens, and Society in the Roman Republic*]], a volume by students and collaborators dedicated to Rosenstein and citing this book; treating that connection as evidence of a field-wide consensus would go beyond the observed relationship. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- The revisionist demographic argument depends on model-based estimates of land availability, marriage markets and migration rather than direct ancient testimony. Roman census figures are themselves contested: Table 4’s "raw" and Brunt’s "corrected" counts differ by 10–20% in several years. ^[ambiguous]
- The Bacchanalian suppression "defies a convincing solution": the surviving evidence is too overlaid with melodrama to establish the cult’s scale, intentions or the authorities’ motives securely.
