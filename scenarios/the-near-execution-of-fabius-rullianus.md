# The Near-Execution of Fabius Rullianus by Dictator Papirius Cursor (325 BC)

In 325 BC the dictator Lucius Papirius Cursor returned to [[entities/rome|Rome]] to retake unfavorable auspices, leaving his master of the horse Quintus Fabius Rullianus in command of the army with strict orders not to engage the Samnites. While Papirius was away, Roman scouts reported the enemy unprepared; Rullianus attacked and won, then — allegedly to deny Papirius any share of the glory — burned the captured weapons and reported his victory directly to the Senate rather than to the absent dictator.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Quintus Fabius Rullianus** ([[entities/quintus-fabius-rullianus]]) — goal: win a decisive battle and claim credit for the victory; resources: command of the field army, support of his troops, his father Marcus Fabius's political backing, right of appeal (*provocatio*) to the people.
- **Lucius Papirius Cursor** ([[entities/lucius-papirius-cursor]]) — goal: enforce dictatorial authority and military discipline, deny a subordinate unearned glory; resources: supreme dictatorial *imperium*, lictors, army command upon return.
- **Marcus Fabius Ambustus** ([[entities/clan-fabius]]) — goal: save his son's life; resources: senatorial prestige, oratory before the assembly.

## What Happened

When Papirius learned of the unauthorized victory, he hurried back to [[entities/samnium|Samnium]] "in a vengeful mood." He assembled the troops and publicly interrogated Rullianus about his disobedience; his lictors prepared their axes for an execution and seized Rullianus. Rullianus's father, Marcus Fabius, urged his son to make a final appeal to the Roman people (*provocatio ad populum*). An assembly gathered near the camp; Marcus Fabius spoke on Rullianus's behalf, invoking the memory of past dictators who had been less harsh, while Papirius's side invoked military discipline and the precedent of the "Manlian orders" — the earlier dictator Titus Manlius Torquatus's execution of his own son for disobeying a no-engagement order during the Latin War. ^[extracted]

## Outcome

Papirius, while furious, recognized the people's authority to pardon Rullianus, and Rullianus was not executed. Papirius nonetheless stripped Rullianus of any further command authority for the remainder of the campaign and returned to the field; his troops, angry that their commander had not honored their earlier pleas to spare Rullianus, reportedly fought poorly in the next battle. Rullianus went on to be elected consul for 322 BC, his reputation apparently undamaged by the episode.

## Open Questions

- Whether Rullianus's victory and subsequent report directly to the Senate were a calculated plan to steal the dictator's glory, as some ancient sources imply, or simply an exploitation of a battlefield opportunity remains ambiguous. ^[ambiguous]
- The historicity of the extended speeches Livy attributes to both sides is doubtful.
