# Turnus Kills Pallas in Single Combat

**This is a legendary/literary episode** from Book X of the *Aeneid* ("The Death of Princes"), set during the pitched battle between the Trojan-Arcadian alliance and the Rutulians.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Pallas** ([[entities/pallas]]) — young Arcadian prince, son of King Evander, fighting as Aeneas's ally; goal: win glory or die nobly, having been sent to war by his father
- **Turnus** ([[entities/turnus]]) — Rutulian king and leader; deliberately isolates Pallas for personal combat
- **Hercules/Jupiter** (divine witnesses, not independent parties) — Hercules grieves for his former host's son but is told by Jupiter that Pallas's fate is fixed and cannot be altered

## What Happened

At his sister Juturna's urging, Turnus abandons his chariot command post to confront Pallas directly, ordering his own troops to clear the ground so the two can fight alone: "I take Pallas, Pallas falls to me. I wish his father stood here to watch." Pallas, though visibly outmatched, refuses to yield — "either I win the honor of taking spoils from the enemy commander, or I die a noble death" — and prays to Hercules (his father Evander's former divine guest) for aid. Hercules hears the prayer and grieves, but Jupiter tells him every man's life has a fixed limit and Turnus too "is called by fate"; the gods do not intervene.

Pallas throws first, his spear grazing Turnus's shoulder through the shield rim. Turnus's return throw punches clean through Pallas's shield, cuirass, and chest, killing him instantly. Standing over the body, Turnus taunts the dead prince, sends word to Evander that his son is returned "in that state which his father merited," and strips Pallas's elaborately engraved swordbelt (depicting the mythical slaughter of the sons of Aegyptus by the Danaids) as a battlefield trophy, which he then wears on his own shoulder.

## Outcome

Pallas is dead; Turnus keeps the swordbelt as a prized spoil of war. The trophy proves fatal in the long run: in the poem's final duel ([[scenarios/aeneid-aeneas-turnus-final-duel]]), Aeneas recognizes the belt on Turnus's shoulder and kills him in a fury over it rather than granting the mercy he had been about to extend.

## Open Questions

- None significant within this episode; its consequences are resolved explicitly in the poem's final duel.
