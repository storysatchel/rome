# Mithras

Mithras was the god of mystery associations whose cult developed in the Roman empire. His name evokes the Iranian deity Mitra, but the Roman cult should not simply be treated as an unchanged Persian import. It was especially associated with soldiers, although its communities were not confined to them. No systematic ancient account of its mythology or theology survives; inscriptions, images, buildings and scattered literary references therefore carry much of the evidence.

## Shrines and images

Mithraea in widely separated provinces shared a recognisable arrangement: a long, cave-like room, benches along its sides and an image of Mithras killing a bull at the far end. Other images show his birth from rock and his association with the sun god Helios-Sol. Their interpretation remains difficult. Even where the bull-slaying scene recurs, subsidiary scenes and sequences of planetary gods vary, and a single uniform doctrine cannot safely be reconstructed from them.

## Association and boundaries

Worshippers entered ranks of initiation; evidence from Mainz includes figures identified with the grades of Father, Heliodromus and Miles. The handshake of Mithras and Helios-Sol after the bull's death supplied one image through which members could understand their bond. How far a particular symbolic interpretation was shared across the empire is uncertain.

The associations are generally understood as excluding women from initiation, but this does not mean that women had no relation to Mithras. A woman's prayer and dedications to him complicate any simple equation between a male initiatory group and the entire circle of people who might address the god. The Mithraic evidence also intersects with other cults: the Aventine sanctuary of Jupiter Dolichenus contained Mithraic reliefs alongside representations of several other deities. Such proximity does not establish that their worship or teachings were identical.
