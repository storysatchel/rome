# Caesar

Julius Caesar was the late Republican dictator whose building projects recast Rome’s civic and political spaces in the 40s BCE. His relocation of the Rostra and restoration of statues of Sulla and Pompey joined public benefaction with visible claims to authority.

## Restoring rivals in public space

Caesar ordered the statues re-erected on the new platform. The gesture offered a pointed contrast with Sulla’s use of the Rostra to display the severed heads of political opponents. Instead of exhibiting the destruction of his adversaries, Caesar could appear to restore their public standing.

Dio reports that Caesar was praised both for the restoration and for allowing Antony the glory and inscription associated with the work. The interpretation in *Architecture and Politics in Republican Rome* stresses that this apparent generosity also demonstrated supremacy: Caesar possessed the power to grant symbolic clemency and even to decide who received credit for it.

## Reordering the Forum

The restored statues belonged to a larger transformation of Rome’s political centre. In 44 BCE, Caesar relocated the Rostra to the Forum’s western end. Remains of a curved cement platform approached by six travertine steps preserve part of the project; its façade used coloured stone to make the speaker’s position conspicuous.

The new alignment separated the platform from the senate house and emphasized the speaker’s relationship with the assembled people. Davies reads the arrangement as an architectural expression of Caesar’s unmediated authority. His dictatorship also removed the usual limits of annual magistracies, allowing urban schemes on a scale rivals could not match.

Projects beyond the Forum included theatres and planned public collections of Greek and Latin books, with Varro assigned to acquiring and classifying them. The plan for libraries extended cultural access beyond elite private collections, though a plan should not be confused with a completed Caesarian institution.
