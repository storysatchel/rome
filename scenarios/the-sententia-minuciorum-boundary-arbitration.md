# The Sententia Minuciorum — Rome Arbitrates a Boundary Dispute Between Genua and the Langenses Viturii

In 117 BC a territorial quarrel broke out in Liguria between the allied coastal city of Genua (modern Genoa) and the Langenses Viturii, a local community occupying the inland hinterland. Rather than let the parties fight it out, the senate dispatched two legates — both apparently named Minucius, hence the surviving inscription's name *sententia Minuciorum* — to investigate and hand down a binding verdict. The decision was engraved on a bronze or stone tablet set up on the disputed ground itself, one of the best-preserved Roman arbitration documents to survive from the Republic.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Genua** ([[entities/genua]]) — an allied coastal polity; goal: secure control of fields and access up the valley; resources: status as a Roman ally, local standing.
- **Langenses Viturii** ([[entities/langenses-viturii]]) — a Ligurian hill community; goal: retain their traditional fields against the coastal city's encroachment; resources: occupation and customary use of the contested land.
- **Senatorial legates (the Minucii)** — arbiters acting with the senate's delegated authority; goal: produce an enforceable, inscribed settlement; resources: imperium-backed authority to survey and fix boundaries.

## What Happened

The two legates defined an inner core of land belonging to the Langenses by tracing its outer limits along rivers and streams, switching from one watercourse to another at four marked points. Around this inner zone they then established a second, largely concentric zone of public land (*ager publicus*), whose outer limits they fixed by setting the locations of fifteen boundary markers. The entire verdict was then inscribed and erected on the site as a permanent, visible record — a pattern the Romans repeated elsewhere in Italy for similar disputes, including [[entities/pisae|Pisae]] versus the colony of [[entities/luna|Luna]] in 168 BC and [[entities/ateste|Ateste]] versus [[entities/vicetia|Vicetia]] in 134 BC. ^[inferred]

## Outcome

The Langenses retained a defined core of fields; a buffer zone of public land separated the two communities' holdings, reducing future friction. The inscribed tablet itself functioned as permanent physical evidence of Roman arbitral authority extending into the Italian countryside, reinforcing Rome's role as the ultimate guarantor of local land claims among its allies.

## Open Questions

- The exact family relationship (if any) between the two legates named Minucius remains unestablished here. ^[ambiguous]
- Whether the dispute involved prior violence between the two communities, or was purely a civil petition, is not stated. ^[ambiguous]
