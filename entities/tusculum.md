# Tusculum

Tusculum was a Latin town near Rome, cited by Astin as the birthplace of Marcus Porcius Cato the Elder in 234 BC. Astin's biography uses Cato's Tusculan origin as a point of source-critical correction: ancient sources (Velleius, Valerius Maximus) frame Cato's background as one of humble poverty for rhetorical contrast with his later eminence, but Astin argues Cato was in fact a Roman citizen by birth from a Tusculan family of at least moderate wealth, not a self-made man from nothing.

## Key Ideas

- Cato's birth at Tusculum in 234 BC opens Astin's chronological reconstruction of his life, which runs across the biography's first six chapters through the censorship of 184 BC and Cato's later years.
- Astin treats the "humble origins" narrative attached to Cato in some ancient sources as rhetorical exaggeration rather than literal fact, arguing instead that Cato's Tusculan family held at least moderate wealth — a correction that depends on Tusculum's status as a town producing Roman citizens of real, if not elite Roman senatorial, standing. ^[inferred: the citing page states Cato's family status directly but frames Tusculum's civic status only implicitly]
- Tusculum's role in this account is specifically as Cato's origin point and as the backdrop against which later ancient authors contrasted his "new man" (*novus homo*) political rise at Rome — the town itself is not otherwise described in the material available from this source. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions
