# cities

Roman cities were centres of civic administration, elite residence, and concentrated population. In the first and second centuries AD their network combined an exceptionally large capital with other major cities and numerous small communities, producing a level of urbanization striking by pre-industrial European comparisons.

## Large centres and small cities

The source estimates that Carthage, Alexandria, and Antioch together probably held at least a million people in addition to Rome’s population. Perhaps 5 percent of the empire’s inhabitants lived in cities of more than 100,000 people, compared with about 3.3 percent of Europe’s population around 1800. These are comparative estimates, not precise ancient census totals.

Roman Italy alone had, at one point, 431 administrative cities. Excluding Rome, an estimated 20 percent of Italy’s population lived in cities, though many were small: the author’s calculation implies an average of approximately 2,000 inhabitants for those outside the capital. A high level of urbanization thus coexisted with substantial variation in urban scale.

## Rome within an imperial population

Jongman places this urban network within an empire estimated at sixty million or more people in the first and second centuries AD. Rome itself is estimated at approximately one million inhabitants. The comparison with early modern Europe emphasizes the scale: in 1500, only four European cities exceeded 100,000, with a combined population of about 450,000.

These comparisons are arguments about relative historical scale, built from demographic estimates rather than equally complete censuses. They support the importance of urban demand and concentrated population without requiring all Roman cities to resemble the capital or implying uniformly prosperous urban lives.
