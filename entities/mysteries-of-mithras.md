# Mysteries of Mithras

The *Mysteries of Mithras* (commonly referred to as Mithraism) constituted one of the most widespread and influential esoteric mystery cults of the Roman Empire between the late first century and fourth century CE. Claiming Persian (*Persicum*) antiquity but formulated within the urban Roman and provincial environments, the cult venerated Mithras (*Sol Invictus Mithras*), an unconquered cosmic savior god associated with light, truth, contracts, and the seasonal victory over cosmic chaos. Organized into autonomous, secretive, all-male fraternal cells meeting in windowless subterranean sanctuaries (*mithraea*), Mithraic communities drew heavily from legionary soldiers, imperial freedmen, customs officials (*publicani*), and municipal bureaucrats.

## Architectural Setting: The *Mithraeum*

Mithraic communities met not in open public temples, but in intentionally concealed, private, subterranean or semi-subterranean chambers known as *mithraea* (or *spelaea*, "caves"), mirroring the mythological cave where Mithras captured and slew the divine bull.

Over forty distinct *mithraea* have been identified archaeologically in Rome and its port city of Ostia Antica. Notable surviving sanctuaries in Rome include:
- **Mithraeum of San Clemente:** Located beneath the fourth-century Christian basilica and a first-century Roman insula.
- **Mithraeum of the Circus Maximus:** Constructed within the substructures of the imperial seating tiers.
- **Mithraeum of the Baths of Caracalla:** The largest known mithraeum in Rome, featuring elaborate underground corridors and a sunken baptismal/cleansing pit.
- **Mithraeum of Santa Prisca:** On the Aventine Hill, featuring wall frescoes depicting the seven initiation grades and procession of lions.

### Interior Layout
A typical mithraeum held between twenty and forty initiates at a time. The layout was standardized throughout the empire:
- **Central Nave:** A narrow sunken central aisle running from the entrance to the cult niche.
- **Podia (Benches):** High, inclined masonry or stone platforms running along both side walls, padded with carpets or straw, where initiates reclined during communal meals.
- **The Apse:** At the far end of the sanctuary stood the focal cult image—either a painted relief, free-standing marble sculpture, or fresco depicting the **Tauroctony**.

## The Tauroctony and Mithraic Iconography

The central icon of every mithraeum was the *Tauroctony* (the bull-slaying scene), a complex astronomical and mythological allegory:
- **The Central Act:** Mithras, depicted as a vigorous young man wearing a tunic, boots, and a billowing Phrygian cap, kneels on the back of a recumbent white bull. Forcing the beast down with his knee, he pulls back the bull’s snout with his left hand while plunging a short dagger into its neck with his right, averting his gaze toward the radiant sun.
- **Cosmic Regeneration:** From the dying bull’s wound flows sacred life: the tip of the bull's tail frequently morphs into ears of ripe wheat (*spicae*), symbolizing agricultural fertility born from death.
- **The Animal Cohorts:**
  - A **dog** (sacred companion) and a **snake** (terrestrial life) leap up to lap the spurting blood.
  - A **scorpion** (creature of the dark) attacks the bull's genitals, attempting to corrupt the generative seed of creation.
  - A **raven** (*corax*) perches on Mithras’s cloak, acting as the divine messenger between the Sun (*Sol*) and Mithras.
- **Astronomical Markers:** The two torch-bearing attendants flank the scene: **Cautes**, holding an upturned burning torch (symbolizing sunrise, spring, and ascending life), and **Cautopates**, holding a downturned extinguished torch (symbolizing sunset, autumn, and death). In the upper corners appear the busts of Sol (the Sun god) and Luna (the Moon goddess).

Modern astronomical interpretations (notably David Ulansey) demonstrate that the figures correspond directly to constellations along the celestial equator during the precession of the equinoxes (Taurus, Canis Minor, Hydra, Scorpio, Corvus), representing Mithras as a cosmic sovereign capable of shifting the celestial sphere.

## The Seven Initiation Grades

Unlike open polytheistic civic cults, Mithraism was strictly hierarchical, admitting candidates only through a progressive sequence of seven distinct initiation grades, each governed by a specific celestial body:

| Grade | Latin Title | Translation | Governing Planet | Insignia / Ritual Symbols |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | *Corax* | Raven | Mercury | Caduceus, beaker, raven mask |
| 2 | *Nymphus* | Bridegroom | Venus | Diadem, lamp, veil |
| 3 | *Miles* | Soldier | Mars | Lance, helmet, military pack |
| 4 | *Leo* | Lion | Jupiter | Fire shovel (*batillum*), sistrum, honey |
| 5 | *Perses* | Persian | Moon | Scythe, Phrygian cap, sickle |
| 6 | *Heliodromus* | Sun-Runner | Sun | Radiant crown, torch, whip |
| 7 | *Pater* | Father | Saturn | Phrygian cap, patera, staff (*virga*) |

Initiation into each rank required rigorous tests of physical endurance, fasting, and oaths of secrecy (*sacramentum*). Tertullian (*De Corona Militis* 15) records that during the initiation of the *Miles* (Soldier), a crown was offered to the candidate on the point of a sword; the initiate was instructed to brush it aside with his hand onto his shoulder, declaring: *"Mithras is my only crown!"* From that day on, a Mithraic soldier never wore a victory garland on his head.

The community was presided over by the **Pater**, who sat in an elevated stone throne at the head of the mithraeum, assisted by the *Heliodromus*. The lower three grades (*Corax*, *Nymphus*, *Miles*) functioned as acolytes and servants, serving the sacred food and wine to the higher grades (*Leo*, *Perses*, *Heliodromus*, *Pater*) reclining on the podium benches.

## Social Composition and Cross-Class Dynamics

Mithraism was an exclusively male community. Women were barred from membership and are entirely absent from the epigraphic record of the cult.

The social profile of Mithraic brotherhoods reveals an extraordinary cross-class cohesion:
- **Imperial Bureaucracy and Customs:** Customs collectors (*portoria*), public contractors, imperial slaves, and wealthy freedmen (*liberti*) in the service of the emperor were heavily represented. In Ostia, where maritime commerce and grain importations dominated civic life, the majority of mithraea were funded by merchant syndicates and freedmen administrators.
- **The Military:** Along the Danubian and Rhine frontiers (Pannonia, Moesia, Germania), as well as in Hadrian’s Wall in Britain, Mithraism became the preferred religion of legionary centurions, cavalry officers, and garrison commanders. The cult’s emphasis on discipline, brotherhood, courage, loyalty to oaths (*fides*), and triumph over death mirrored military virtues.
- **Social Mobility:** Within the walls of the mithraeum, ordinary civic distinctions could be inverted: a wealthy imperial slave or freedman holding the high rank of *Pater* could preside over and ritually command a freeborn civic official or army veteran holding the lower rank of *Miles*.

## Relationship to the Imperial Cult and Christianity

Mithras was perceived by Roman authorities as completely compatible with civic loyalty. Because Mithras was identified as *Sol Invictus* ("The Unconquered Sun"), the cult naturally dovetailed with third-century imperial ideology. Emperors such as Commodus (who was initiated into the mysteries), Septimius Severus, and Aurelian patronized Sol Invictus. In 308 CE, at the Conference of Carnuntum, Diocletian, Galerius, and Licinius jointly dedicated a sanctuary to Mithras, hailing him on the inscription as *fautori imperii sui* ("The patron protector of their Empire").

Because Mithraism practiced a sacramental communal meal of consecrated bread and wine, used holy water for lustrations, venerated December 25 (the winter solstice) as the birthday of the Unconquered Sun, and promised a final resurrection of the dead, early Christian apologists (such as Justin Martyr, *1 Apol.* 66, and Tertullian, *De Praescr. Haeret.* 40) denounced Mithraism as a demonic counterfeit of the Christian Eucharist. 

Following the triumph of Christianity under Constantine and the anti-polytheistic decrees of Theodosius I in the late fourth century, mithraea across Rome were systematically desecrated, blocked off, or converted into Christian church foundations (as observed directly beneath the Basilica of San Clemente).
