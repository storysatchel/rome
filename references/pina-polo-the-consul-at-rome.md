# The Consul at Rome

Francisco Pina Polo, Professor of Ancient History at the Universidad de Zaragoza, published *The Consul at Rome: The Civil Functions of the Consuls in the Roman Republic* with Cambridge University Press in 2011 (translated from the original Spanish research). The book's purpose is to analyze, on a strictly empirical rather than theoretical basis, the tasks performed by [[concepts/consulship|consuls]] in the civil sphere of their office between 367 and 50 BC — religion, diplomacy, legislation, jurisdiction, colonization, elections, and day-to-day politics — deliberately setting aside their much better-documented military role as army commanders.

## Core Argument / Thesis

Pina Polo observes that, despite extensive modern scholarship on the Roman senate, the popular assemblies, the tribunate of the plebs, the aedileship, the praetorship, and the censorship, no monograph before his had been devoted specifically to the functions and activities of the consulship — the Republic's supreme magistracy — since Heinrich Gabriel Römer's forgotten 1841 Utrecht dissertation. Earlier treatments (Mommsen's *Römisches Staatsrecht*, Giovannini's *Consulare imperium*, Lintott's *The Constitution of the Roman Republic*) approached the office primarily through the legal nature of *imperium*, inferring functions from doctrine rather than documenting them from narrative sources. Pina Polo instead reconstructs what consuls actually did "at work," using Livy and other historians as his primary evidence base, organizing the material chronologically into a pre-Sullan period (367–81 BC) and a post-Sullan period (80–50 BC).

## Key Claims and Evidence

- Polybius's account of the constitution frames consuls as holding the "monarchical" element of a mixed constitution: absolute military command abroad (recruiting, appointing military tribunes, punishing subordinates, controlling funds) and headship of the civil administration at Rome (presenting embassies to the senate, deliberating on policy, executing senatorial decrees, summoning and presiding over [[concepts/popular-assemblies-rome|popular assemblies]], and applying laws passed by the people).
- The consular year's start date varied considerably before stabilizing: irregular dates in the 5th–4th centuries BC, likely the Calends of May through much of the 3rd century, the Ides of March from (at latest) 217 BC, and finally 1 January from 153 BC onward — a shift Hans Beck and others connect to military exigencies (the Second Punic War, the Celtiberian rebellion in Hispania) rather than calendrical reform. ^[inferred]
- The author distinguishes the variable "consular year" from the fixed "official year" (which began in March and later January); only from 153 BC did the two coincide completely, which also fixed consular eponymous dating against a stable civil calendar.
- The inauguration of new consuls followed an invariant ritual sequence: pre-dawn auspices, donning the toga praetexta before the household Penates, a *salutatio*, a procession to the Capitol with senators and friends, sacrifice of a white ox to Jupiter by each consul, vows for the republic's welfare (*vota pro salute rei publicae*), and a first senate meeting on the Capitol itself, at which both consuls were expected to speak and a debate on the state of the republic was held.
- The first senatorial session of the year typically — though not invariably — addressed the *sortitio* or *comparatio* (allotment or negotiated division) of provinces between the two consuls and the size of the levy each would command, though Livy shows other urgent matters (tax doubling in 215, scheduling the censorial election in 214) could take precedence depending on the year's circumstances.
- A long scholarly debate, running from Mommsen through Giovannini to more recent authors like Drogula, concerns whether *imperium domi* (civil power within the *pomerium*) and *imperium militiae* (military power outside it) represented a single territorially-bounded power, or whether *imperium* was strictly a military concept confined outside the *pomerium*, with civil authority inside the city instead resting on a separate *potestas*. ^[ambiguous]
- Prosopographical scholarship — above all Broughton's *Magistrates of the Roman Republic* — underpins the book's chronological framework, alongside narrower studies (Neuendorff on consuls 78–49 BC, Lippold on 264–201 BC) and the *fasti consulares*.

## Structure / Scope

The study covers 367–50 BC, split into Part I ("The consular functions in the pre-Sullan age," chapters 1–11: taking office, civic religion, diplomacy, communication via edicts and *contiones*, legislation, jurisdiction, public works, colonization and land distribution, dictator appointment, presiding over elections, and the consular year) and Part II ("The consular functions in the post-Sullan age," chapters 12–14, addressing the disputed *lex Cornelia de provinciis ordinandis* and consular presence in Rome after Sulla). Livy is the dominant narrative source, supplemented by Cicero, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, and epigraphic/numismatic evidence (e.g., coin types depicting the Villa Publica and the Basilica Aemilia).

## Significance

Pina Polo frames his contribution explicitly against the "highly theoretical" legalistic tradition descending from Mommsen (quoting Lintott's own critique of Mommsen on this point), arguing that prior scholarship on consular power was directed at determining the abstract nature of *imperium* and then deducing functions from it, with many specific activities "simply presumed despite the fact that ancient sources do not mention them." His empirical, narrative-source-first method is presented as a corrective, paralleling in spirit (though independent in focus) Kaj Sandberg's institutional analysis of [[concepts/popular-assemblies-rome|assembly]] legislative practice and the broader modern reaction against Mommsenian constitutional formalism. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- The precise legal relationship between *imperium* and *potestas*, and whether the *pomerium* marked a strictly territorial or a qualitative (civil/military) boundary, remains unresolved in the scholarship the book surveys. ^[ambiguous]
- The book's own synthesis of the pre-153 BC consular year's starting dates concedes that evidence is sparse and sometimes contradictory for the 5th and 4th centuries, leaving the earliest chronology uncertain. ^[ambiguous]
