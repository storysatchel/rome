# La Vie Privée des Romains (Manuel des Antiquités Romaines, vol. XIV)

*La Vie Privée des Romains* ("The Private Life of the Romans") is Volume XIV of the *Manuel des Antiquités Romaines* ("Handbook of Roman Antiquities"), a major 19th-century German antiquarian series originally founded by Theodor Mommsen and Joachim Marquardt. This specific volume was authored by Joachim Marquardt, first published in German in 1864 and substantially revised for a second German edition; the text read here is the 1892 French translation by Victor Henry (doctor of law, lecturer at the Sorbonne), published in Paris by Ernest Thorin. The volume's purpose is encyclopedic and systematic: to synthesize scattered ancient documentary evidence into a coherent account of the private, domestic sphere of Roman life — the family, slaves, guests/clients/freedmen, the house, daily routine, and funerary customs — building on earlier antiquarian scholarship (Rossbach, Bernhardy, Wallon, Friedlaender) cited in Marquardt's own preface.

## Core Argument / Thesis

Marquardt's preface to the first edition states his aim as the synthesis (*synthèse*) of isolated surviving documents into general facts permitting a decisive assessment of Roman national character and its historical evolution, built "on the base of raw materials bequeathed by antiquity" (Avant-propos/Préface, lines ~115–150). Rather than a single argumentative thesis, the book is organized as a systematic descriptive survey moving outward from the core of the Roman family (the *pater familias*, the household's legal structure) through its dependents (slaves, clients, freedmen) to the physical setting (the house) and the rhythms of daily and ritual life.

## Key Claims and Evidence

- Roman private law vested the *pater familias* with near-absolute authority (*patria potestas*) over children and dependents, including, in the earliest periods, powers of exposure, sale (limited by the Twelve Tables to three successive sales of a son), and life-and-death authority (*vitae necisque potestas*) — a power that Marquardt notes was gradually curtailed, with Christian-era emperors eventually treating the killing of a son as *parricidium* (Chapter: Introduction, citing Gaius, Ulpian, the *Digest*, and the Twelve Tables via Gellius).
- The wife's position (*in manu*) was juridically assimilated to that of a daughter (*filiae familias loco*): she could not independently own property, though in return she gained inheritance rights within her husband's family; she could not be sold, nor killed without legitimate cause, distinguishing her position somewhat from that of a slave despite structural similarities. ^[ambiguous: the OCR of the surrounding legal citations (Festus, Boeckingius, Rossbach) is degraded enough that some nuances of the argument are uncertain]
- Marquardt's introduction cites specific named incidents illustrating the limits and enforcement of marital discipline: [[scenarios/egnatius-mecennius-wife-killing]] (a husband's unpunished killing of his wife for drinking wine) and [[scenarios/lucius-annius-senate-expulsion-307bce]] (a senator struck from the rolls by the censors of 307 BCE for divorcing without consulting a council of friends).
- The marriage chapter identifies [[scenarios/carvilius-ruga-first-divorce]] — Spurius Carvilius Ruga's divorce of his wife for infertility, traditionally dated to the 230s–220s BCE — as the jurists' textbook case establishing dowry-restitution law for faultless divorces.
- Chapter IV ("Les Esclaves" / "The Slaves") frames the growth of Roman slavery as driven by four main causes: the abandonment of direct cultivation by free peasants in favor of large estates worked by slave labor (since slaves were exempt from military service); the rise of luxury demanding specialized servile labor; administrative needs created by the sheer growing number of slaves requiring supervision; and the later adoption (following Greek precedent) of using slaves in industrial/craft occupations — linking directly to the household-staffing material also found in [[references/cato-varro-roman-farm-management]].
- Marquardt notes that in earlier, simpler times a single household slave sufficed even for notable figures — he cites Manius Curius taking two slaves on the Samnite campaign, Cato the Elder taking three to Spain, Scipio Africanus five, and Caesar three to Britain — illustrating the historically contingent and originally modest scale of Roman domestic slaveholding before its later massive expansion.
- Marquardt frames the overall historical arc of Roman slavery ambivalently: it was simultaneously one of the most violent ferments of imperial decline and a transformative force that imported foreign cultural influences, fueled commerce and industry via freedmen, and gradually eroded the final rigid social boundary (between slave and free) in a Roman world that had already dissolved distinctions of nationality and caste. ^[inferred: Marquardt's synthesis rather than a single cited ancient source]
- Chapter VI ("La Maison" / "The House") grounds its reconstruction of Roman domestic architecture heavily in the Pompeii and Herculaneum excavations (beginning 1748), arguing — against contemporary skeptics — that despite their provincial/municipal (non-senatorial) character, Pompeian houses are fundamentally representative of Italic (not purely Hellenic) domestic architecture, relevant to [[concepts/roman-house]] and [[concepts/roman-domestic-life]].
- Chapter VII ("La Vie de Tous les Jours" / "Daily Life") reconstructs the Roman day as originally governed by agricultural rhythms — rising with the sun, a morning sacrifice, breakfast, work until midday, a meal, a short siesta, further work until the evening meal — a pattern Marquardt presents as the ancestral template later complicated by urban life in Rome itself, and marked by recurring domestic festivals such as birthdays (*dies natalis*) and the New Year *strenae* gift-exchange.

## Structure / Scope

The volume covers Rome from the earliest periods through the Empire, organized in eight chapters: I. Names (Roman naming conventions for men, women, slaves); II. Marriage; III. Children and their education; IV. Slaves; V. Guests, clients, and freedmen; VI. The house; VII. Daily life; VIII. Funerals. Marquardt draws primarily on Roman jurists (Gaius, Ulpian, the *Digest*, the *Institutes*), literary sources (Cicero, Livy, Seneca, Plutarch, Suetonius, the satirists), inscriptions (*CIL*), and — notably for the house chapter — the archaeological record of Pompeii and Herculaneum, citing a substantial contemporary (1820s–1880s) French, German, and Italian antiquarian bibliography (Mazois, Overbeck, Nissen, Becker/Rein's *Gallus*, Wallon's history of ancient slavery).

## Significance

As a volume in the *Manuel des Antiquités Romaines* series co-founded with Mommsen, the book represents the high tide of German positivist antiquarianism applied to Roman social history — explicitly aiming at systematic completeness rather than narrow argument, and situating itself as building on and citing a dense web of prior 19th-century scholarship (noted throughout in extensive footnotes). The translator Victor Henry's preface (1891) frames his own contribution as drawing connections to Sanskrit/Indo-European comparative linguistics where relevant, distinguishing his additions from Marquardt's second-edition text via bracketed notation in the original. ^[inferred: synthesis of the prefatory material's framing]

## Open Questions

- Several legal-historical passages in the slavery and marriage chapters rely on citation chains (Rossbach, Walter, Böcking) whose full argument is not fully recoverable from the available OCR; nuances of Marquardt's position versus views he is summarizing or rebutting are not always separable in this scan. ^[ambiguous]
- The volume's heavy reliance on comparative-institutional reasoning (e.g., deriving Roman custom from supposed Indo-European parallels per the translator's preface) reflects period scholarly methods now largely superseded, and is not something the book itself flags as contestable.
