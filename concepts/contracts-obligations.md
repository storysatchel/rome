# contracts/obligations

Roman contract law did not treat every agreement as enforceable simply because the parties consented. In the classical scheme described by Gaius, obligations arose through recognized contractual types, distinguished by delivery of a thing, formal words, writing, or consent.

## Obligation and legal form

The source cautions against projecting this developed classification into early Roman law. Systematic classification began to emerge with jurists such as Quintus Mucius Scaevola in the early first century BCE. Earlier legal protection centred on obligations associated with transactions and the procedural formulas through which a remedy could be sought.

The older *legis actiones* demanded precisely spoken formulas. A claim had to fit the prescribed form, and an error in the ritual words could invalidate the case. Thus the history of enforceability involves both substantive duties and the procedures available to enforce them.

## Consent in lease and hire

*Locatio conductio*, lease and hire, illustrates the more specific Roman use of consent. It could concern a thing, labour, or a service in exchange for a price. The parties adapted a general legal scheme to their transaction; agreement on the object, price, and terms was relevant to a judge assessing a later dispute.

Writing was not essential to a consensual contract, but recording clauses could be prudent in complex commerce. Shipping arrangements might identify the owner, carrier, customer, vessel, capacity, and agreed price. These details show how practical contracting worked within a recognized legal category rather than through an unlimited principle that consent alone created enforceable obligations.
