# The Prosecution of Fulvius Nobilior Over the Plundered Art of Ambracia (187 BC)

After completing the Aetolian War by capturing [[entities/ambracia|Ambracia]] — once the seat of King Pyrrhus — in 187 BC, consul Marcus Fulvius Nobilior spared the city from being sacked but carried off a large quantity of dedicatory sculptures and paintings, which he displayed in striking numbers (785 bronze statues and 230 marble statues) in his subsequent triumph. Like other successful generals of the era, Fulvius faced prosecution from political rivals (*inimici*) hoping to block his triumph and damage his reputation; in his case, the seizure of art treasures became the actual substance of the charge.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Marcus Fulvius Nobilior** ([[entities/marcus-fulvius-nobilior]]) — goal: secure his triumph and defend his reputation against charges of sacrilege; resources: consular achievement in the Aetolian War, a large haul of captured art, personal reputation for religious learning.
- **Unnamed political rivals (*inimici*)** — goal: block Fulvius's triumph and tarnish his career; resources: ability to summon hostile Ambraciote witnesses to testify in Rome.
- **Ambraciote witnesses** — goal: seek redress (or at least publicize) the stripping of their city's temples; testimony: that Fulvius had despoiled every temple in the city, snatching all images of the gods, leaving "bare walls and doorposts" for the Ambraciotes to pray to.
- **The Senate / college of pontiffs** — role: arbiter, decreeing that the disputed art objects be submitted to the pontifices to determine which were sacred and which profane — the same procedure used for the spoils of [[entities/capua|Capua]] in 210 BC.

## What Happened

Summoned Ambraciote witnesses delivered damaging testimony in [[entities/rome|Rome]], claiming Fulvius had stripped every temple of its images, committing what amounted to sacrilege, and had spared only terracotta objects while taking everything else of value. ^[extracted] Gruen argues this accusation, rather than reflecting genuine impiety on Fulvius's part, actually demonstrates that official Roman policy disapproved of sacrilegious plunder — which is precisely why his rivals thought the charge would stick, and why the Senate responded by referring the disputed items to the pontifical college for a formal sacred/profane determination, mirroring the precedent set with the Capuan spoils in 210 BC. ^[inferred] Gruen notes Fulvius was, by all other indications, a man of considerable religious learning (he later composed a treatise on Rome's religious calendar), undermining the caricature of him as an indiscriminate, irreverent looter. ^[inferred]

## Outcome

The Senate's referral of the Ambracia art objects to the pontiffs for categorization effectively defused the sacrilege charge as a legal weapon, following established precedent rather than treating Fulvius as a unique offender. Fulvius's triumph proceeded, displaying the disputed statues in full.

## Open Questions

- The specific identities of the political rivals (*inimici*) who orchestrated the prosecution are not named in Gruen's account.
- Whether the Ambraciote testimony was essentially accurate or deliberately exaggerated by Fulvius's rivals for political effect is explicitly debated by Gruen, who leans toward viewing it as rhetorically inflated. ^[ambiguous]
