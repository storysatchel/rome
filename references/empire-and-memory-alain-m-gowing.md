# Empire and Memory

> [!tldr] Imperial authors and monuments selected, represented, and politically reused the memory of the Roman Republic.

## Scope and thesis

Memory of the Republic remained influential under the Principate. The study follows *memoria* and *historia* in imperial culture, with literary and urban case studies from Tiberius through Trajan (AD 14–117).

Republican memory was part of imperial Rome’s political culture rather than a completed past. Its recurring subjects included Cicero, Cato, Caesar, Pompey, *res publica restituta*, and the uses of *damnatio memoriae*.

## Literary and urban memory

- Imperial authors negotiated continuity and selective recall of Republican institutions and figures.
- The Forums of Augustus and Trajan materialized changing relationships to Rome's Republican past.
- Tiberian, Neronian, Flavian, and Trajanic contexts show that the political uses of Republican memory changed across the Principate.
- Literary representations interacted with the urban landscape of [[entities/rome]] and to the Augustan transformation of political memory.

These themes complement [[entities/augustus]] and the vault's account of the Republic's political transformation in [[concepts/fall-of-the-roman-republic]].

## Source criticism

Imperial texts and monuments are selective acts of remembrance. Their presentation of the Republic should be read as political construction, not as neutral historical preservation. ^[inferred]

## Open questions

- How did audiences distinguish genuine Republican precedent from imperial invention? ^[ambiguous]
- Did later imperial uses of Republican memory preserve alternative political possibilities or close them down? ^[ambiguous]
