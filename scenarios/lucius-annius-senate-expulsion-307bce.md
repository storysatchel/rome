# The Expulsion of L. Annius from the Senate for an Improper Divorce (307 BCE)

In 307 BCE (447 AUC), the Roman censors expelled the senator L. Annius from the [[entities/curia-hostilia|Senate]] specifically because he had repudiated his wife *nullo amicorum consilio adhibito* — "without calling in any council of friends" — a procedural norm that, while not formally codified in law, was evidently expected practice for a man of senatorial rank contemplating divorce. Marquardt cites the episode (via Valerius Maximus) as evidence that Roman divorces, even at this relatively early date and even though not requiring a specific fault-based grounds, were customarily expected to be witnessed or counseled by a circle of family friends (*amici*) acting in a quasi-judicial advisory capacity.

## Parties & Stakes

- **[[entities/lucius-annius-senator|L. Annius]]** — a sitting Roman senator; divorced his wife unilaterally, without consulting the customary council of friends, and lost his [[entities/curia-hostilia|Senate]] seat as a result.
- **The censors of 307 BCE** — enforced the customary expectation of counseled divorce as a matter of senatorial fitness, using their power of the *lectio senatus* to remove Annius from the rolls.

## What Happened

Procedural custom in the early-to-mid Republic held that even a "no-fault" divorce ought to be conducted with the involvement of friends or relatives (*amici*, *cognati*, *propinqui*) serving as informal witnesses or advisors — a practice attested elsewhere for adultery hearings conducted by family tribunals. Annius divorced his wife unilaterally and without this customary consultation. The censors of that year treated the omission as a serious enough breach of expected senatorial conduct (*mores*) to warrant striking him from the Senate during their review of its membership.

## Outcome

Annius lost his senatorial status. The case became a cited exemplum (via Valerius Maximus) of the censors' power to police even procedurally "legal" private conduct — divorce itself being lawful — when it violated unwritten expectations of propriety for a man of his rank.

## Open Questions

- Whether Annius's wife had committed any fault that might have justified the divorce on its merits is not stated. ^[ambiguous]
- The full text of the censorial justification beyond the "no council of friends" detail is not preserved in the source material consulted.
