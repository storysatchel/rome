# Divination, Prediction and the End of the Roman Republic

> [!tldr] Federico Santangelo shows how Roman divination was embedded in government, elite competition, and the political transformation from Republic to Principate.

## Scope and thesis

Santangelo studies the intersection of Roman politics, culture, and divination during the last two centuries of the Republic and the early Principate. The book treats divination as part of governmental decision-making and political communication, not as a separate religious sphere.

## Political cases

- The Senate's consultation of Etruscan haruspices during the 88 BC prodigy connected interpretation of signs to the Marius–Sulla crisis.
- The Sibylline Books and the 56 BC debate over Ptolemy XII show prophetic authority shaping foreign-policy decisions.
- Antony's use of augural prerogatives to obstruct Dolabella's election in 44 BC demonstrates how religious office could be mobilized in political conflict.
- Augustus restricted and controlled prophetic materials, including the Sibylline Books, as part of the new regime's management of religious authority.

The cases complement [[concepts/roman-religion-and-augury]] and illuminate the institutional breakdown discussed in [[concepts/fall-of-the-roman-republic]].

## Source criticism

The book compares literary, institutional, and religious evidence. Interpretations of prodigies and predictions are mediated by authors and later political contexts; their reported meaning cannot be assumed to equal the participants' private beliefs. ^[inferred]

## Open questions

- How did ordinary Romans participate in or contest elite control of divinatory interpretation? ^[ambiguous]
- Did Augustan control of prophetic materials reduce competing authority or redirect it into a new imperial form? ^[ambiguous]
