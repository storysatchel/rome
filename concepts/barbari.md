# Barbari

The category of *barbari* (singular *barbarus*) encompassed peoples living outside the political, legal, and cultural boundaries of the Roman world (*orbis Romanus*). While adapted from the Greek linguistic concept of the foreign "other," the Roman construction of the barbarian was fundamentally political, legal, and ethical rather than purely ethnic or linguistic. To the Roman elite, a *barbarus* was someone unformed by civic law (*ius civile*), urban institutions (*civitas*), and rational self-governance, yet possessing an untamed, irrational physical energy (*ferocitas*) that could be subdued, disciplined, and exploited through Roman arms and governance.

## Legal and political status: the spectrum of alterity

In Roman law and international diplomacy, foreigners fell along a structured legal spectrum defined by their relationship to the Roman state:

1. **Hostes:** Declared public enemies of the Roman people (*populus Romanus*) against whom a formal state of war existed. Outside formal treaties (*foedera*), non-treaty foreigners had no inherent rights under Roman law and could be captured, enslaved, or killed as an exercise of *ius gentium*.
2. **Barbari in *Barbaricum*:** Independent tribes and confederations residing beyond the imperial frontiers (*limes*). Rome maintained relations with these groups through client treaties, diplomatic gift-giving, dynamic trade checkpoints, and annual subsidies (as under Domitian with the Dacians).
3. **Dediticii:** Peoples who underwent unconditional surrender (*deditio in fidem*). Having surrendered their lands, weapons, and ancestral laws to the discretion of a Roman magistrate, they were completely destitute of civil rights (*nullius certae civitatis*). Their physical liberty was conceded *precario* (revocable at imperial pleasure), and they were prohibited from residing within Rome or acquiring Roman citizenship.[^mommsen-dediticii]
4. **Peregrini:** Free provincial non-citizen subjects organized into recognized municipal communities (*civitates*). Conquered tribal populations that were pacified and incorporated into newly created provinces (such as the Gauls, Spaniards, or integrated Dacians) transitioned from external *barbari* into domestic *peregrini*, subject to Roman provincial taxation and magistrates but governed locally by native customs.
5. **Cives Romani:** Roman citizens. The transition from *barbarus* to citizen was accomplished individually via military service in the auxiliary forces (*auxilia*) rewarded with citizenship diplomas upon honorable discharge (*honesta missio*), through individual imperial grants (*beneficia*), or collectively through Caracalla’s *Constitutio Antoniniana* of 212 CE, which extended citizenship to almost all free inhabitants of the provincial empire.[^constitutio-context]

## The visual and ideological construction of the barbarian

In Roman metropolitan monumentality and public art, the depiction of the barbarian functioned as a visual ratification of imperial authority and victorious order (*disciplina*):[^coulston-art]

- **Iconographic Stereotypes:** Barbarians were uniformly contrasted with the calm, draped toga-clad citizen (*gens togata*) or the armored emperor. Northern barbarians (Gauls, Germans, Dacians, Sarmatians) were depicted wearing trousers (*braccae*), belted tunics, fringed cloaks (*sagum*), wild disheveled hair, and full beards. Eastern barbarians (Parthians) were distinguished by soft Phrygian caps, tunics with long sleeves, and flowing trousers.
- **Postural Rhetoric:** On monuments such as [[entities/trajans-column|Trajan's Column]], the *Tropaeum Traiani* at Adamclisi, and the Arch of Septimius Severus, barbarians appear in scenes of frantic, disordered combat, begging on their knees for imperial mercy (*clementia*), fleeing across rugged terrain, or bound in chains as captive trophies beneath piles of captured weaponry.
- **The Ideology of Subjugation:** Imperial literature—crystallized in Virgil's *Aeneid* (6.851–853: *"regere imperio populos... parcere subiectis et debellare superbos"*)—cast the subjection of barbarians as a moral obligation. Rome did not merely conquer; it established peace (*pax*) and lawful custom over irrational savagery.

## State-directed settlement and military integration

Far from remaining permanently segregated behind frontier fortifications, hundreds of thousands of *barbari* were settled inside provincial territory throughout imperial history:[^ando-settlement]

- **Forced Resettlement (*Translatio Populorum*):** Augustus transferred 50,000 Germanic Sugambri into northeastern Gaul; Nero's governor of Moesia, Ti. Plautius Silvanus Aelianus, moved over 100,000 trans-Danubian people into Moesia to pay tribute; and Trajan settled broad populations *ex toto orbe Romano* into newly conquered [[entities/dacia|Dacia]] to replace the devastated native population.[^woolf-migration]
- **Labor and Agricultural Revitalization:** In the second and third centuries, emperors increasingly used barbarian settlement to repopulate devastated border zones, cultivate abandoned agrarian land (*agri deserti*), and secure predictable tax yields. Marcus Aurelius settled Marcomannic and Quadian captives across the Danube provinces and northern Italy.
- **Military Manpower:** The Roman army was a primary engine for barbarian assimilation. Foreign warriors enlisted in ethnic auxiliary units, learned military Latin, acquired Roman tactical discipline, and were rewarded upon discharge with Roman citizenship and legal marriage rights (*conubium*). By the fourth century, the distinction between Roman and barbarian blurred as the frontier defense rested heavily on allied barbarian federates (*foederati*), settled military colonists (*laeti* and *gentiles*), and Germanic military commanders (*magistri militum*).
