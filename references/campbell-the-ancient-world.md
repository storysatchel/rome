# The Ancient World (The Story of Civilization, Volume I)

Phillip Campbell's *The Story of Civilization: Volume I, The Ancient World* was published by TAN Books (Charlotte, NC) in 2016 as part of a homeschool curriculum including a workbook, test book, teacher's manual, and audio/video companions. A foreword by Brendan J. McGuire (Associate Professor of History, Christendom College) frames the book as a corrective to what it calls Reformation-era and Enlightenment "anti-Catholic" historiography (naming John Foxe and Edward Gibbon explicitly), positioning the volume as explicitly confessional history written for Catholic homeschooling families rather than a secular academic survey.

## Core Argument / Thesis

The book's organizing thesis, stated in its introduction and reiterated at its close, is providential: history culminates in salvation history, and the political rise of Rome — its wars, conquests, and civil strife — is narrated as groundwork for "the appearance of the real Son of God on the earth" under Augustus. Rome's own internal narrative arc treats the Republic's late-period civil wars (Gracchi through Augustus) as a century-long process of self-inflicted "demise" driven by war's corrosive effect on the citizen-farmer class, concluding that Augustus's peace was both a political resolution and providentially timed for Christ's incarnation.^[inferred]

## Key Claims and Evidence

- Rome's republican government is introduced through a simplified civics model: two annually elected consuls barred from immediate re-election, a Senate of old aristocratic families controlling finance and war declarations, and multiple popular assemblies (Tribal, Plebeian, Centurial) — explicitly contrasted with monarchy as "officials elected by the people govern[ing] by the rule of laws, not the decrees of kings."
- The patrician-plebeian conflict is resolved, in the book's telling, through the plebeian secession (a work stoppage and withdrawal to a mountain outside Rome) that forced patricians to create the tribunate; the book dates rough legal parity to 287 BC.
- The 118 years of near-continuous warfare between the start of the Punic Wars and 146 BC are identified as the direct cause of the *latifundia* system: farmer-soldiers' prolonged absences let wealthy senators buy up distressed farms and work them with war-captive slaves, displacing small farmers into urban Rome and creating the landless poor dependent on bread and games.
- Tiberius Gracchus's Land Bill (133 BC) and the Populares/Optimates divide are presented as the proximate trigger of a century of civil strife: Tiberius Gracchus is killed by a senatorial mob, Gaius Gracchus dies by suicide after defeat in 121 BC, and the Gracchan land question is explicitly linked forward to Marius, Sulla, and Caesar as one continuous conflict.
- [[entities/sulla]]'s march on Rome (88 BC) is identified as the first time a Roman general led an army into the city itself; the book states Sulla's proscriptions as dictator may have killed "as many as nine thousand people," attributing to him the (likely apocryphal) line "I have executed everybody I can think of. Those I have forgotten about, I will execute at some time in the future."
- Caesar's assassination narrative follows Plutarch/Suetonius-derived tradition (Calpurnia's dream, Cimber's petition as distraction, Casca's first blow, "more than sixty senators" attacking), and the book explicitly credits Caesar — unlike Sulla — with pardoning former enemies including [[entities/pompey]]'s supporters, framing this clemency as a (failed) attempt to end the Optimates-Populares cycle of vengeance.
- Octavian's defeat of Antony and Cleopatra at Actium (31 BC) and his acceptance of the titles *Princeps* and *Augustus* in 27 BC are presented as the formal end of "the fighting that had begun with Tiberius Gracchus more than a hundred years earlier."

## Structure / Scope

The volume covers Egypt, Mesopotamia, the Levant/Israel, Assyria, Babylon, Persia, Greece, and Rome across 35 chapters, with Roman material concentrated in Chapters 22–27 (The Etruscans; The City of Seven Hills; The Punic Wars; Greece and Rome Collide; Marius and Sulla; The Rise and Fall of Julius Caesar), followed by chapters on Christ's birth and the early Church. Pedagogically the book alternates expository narration with invented first-person dramatized vignettes (e.g., a fictional Etruscan funeral through the eyes of a girl named Vellia, a dramatized Sabine-women episode) explicitly labeled as imaginative reconstructions rather than attested fact — the text itself flags these: "This meeting could never really have taken place" and "We do not know if Etruscan funerals happened exactly in this manner."

## Significance

As a confessional homeschool textbook rather than a scholarly monograph, this book's significance in the wiki is primarily as a contrast case: it shares narrative beats with academic treatments like [[references/watts-mortal-republic]] (the Gracchi-to-Augustus arc as one continuous crisis of the Republic) but frames the same events teleologically, as preparation for the Incarnation, rather than as a case study in republican institutional failure. Its explicit anti-secular, anti-Gibbon framing (stated in the foreword) distinguishes its historiographical stance sharply from the other four books in this batch.^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- The book's dramatized vignettes (Etruscan funeral, Sabine women abduction-and-reconciliation, Caesar's assassination dialogue) blend attested ancient tradition with invented dialogue; where the line falls between inherited legend (e.g., Livy/Plutarch) and the author's own narrative invention is not always sourced in-text.^[ambiguous]
- The claim that Sulla killed "as many as nine thousand people" during the proscriptions is stated without a specific ancient source citation in the sampled text; ancient figures for Sulla's proscription victims vary across sources.^[ambiguous]
