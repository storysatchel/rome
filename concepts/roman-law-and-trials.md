# Roman Law and Trials

The Late Roman Republic's criminal and civil judicial system is reconstructed almost entirely through literary testimony rather than surviving court records, with Alexander's catalog of 391 known trials (149–50 BC) serving as the primary evidentiary backbone here.

## Key Ideas

- The permanent-standing-court system (*quaestio perpetua*) began with the *lex Calpurnia* of 149 BC, which established the first such court (for extortion, *repetundae*); Alexander's catalog of trials runs from this founding statute down to 50 BC, on the eve of Caesar's crossing of the Rubicon.
- For each trial Alexander records up to eleven data categories — date, charge, defendant, advocates, prosecutors, presiding magistrate, jurors, witnesses, other parties, and verdict — reflecting how dense and procedurally formalized Late Republican prosecution had become by the 2nd–1st centuries BC.
- *Repetundae* (extortion) trials are frequently datable to the year immediately following a defendant's provincial command, since provincial misconduct was the most common trigger for prosecution upon a governor's return to Rome.
- Standing-court (*quaestio perpetua*) trials are methodologically distinct from two other tribunal types: trials before the full citizen assembly (*iudicia populi*) and the religious *quaestio apud pontifices*, reserved for offenses like the Vestal Virgins' incestum charges — showing that Roman "law" operated across civil, criminal, and sacral jurisdictions rather than a single unified court system. See [[concepts/roman-religion-and-augury]] for the religious dimension of Roman public life these sacral trials intersected with.
- The precise governing statute for many prosecutions is genuinely disputed in the ancient and modern record — e.g., whether C. Papirius Carbo's 119 BC trial fell under the *lex Acilia de repetundis* or the *lex Sempronia ne quis iudicio circumveniatur* — indicating that Roman statutory law in this period was often applied through contested interpretation rather than settled code. ^[ambiguous]

## Open Questions

- Many trial dates rest on inference from magistrates' known tenures rather than direct evidence — the underlying chronology of Late Republican prosecution is less certain than the catalog's tabular precision suggests.
- The later, more famous prosecutions of the 90s–50s BC (Clodius, Milo, Catiline's associates) are catalogued in Alexander's work but were not closely examined for this page, which drew primarily on the introduction and earliest entries (cases #1–~45). ^[ambiguous]
