# A Place at the Altar: Priestesses in Republican Rome

Meghan J. DiLuzio's *A Place at the Altar: Priestesses in Republican Rome* (published via Project MUSE, Princeton University Press) surveys the full range of female religious office in the Roman Republic — not only the [[entities/vestal-virgins]] but also the *flaminica Dialis* (wife of the flamen Dialis), priestly couples more broadly, and the Salian virgins and other *sacerdotes*/*ministrae* — arguing that these priestesses held essential, publicly visible religious authority rather than merely domestic or symbolic roles.^[inferred from title, table of contents, and recovered chapter content]

## Core Argument / Thesis

Based on the recovered table of contents (Introduction; Ch.1 The Flamen and Flaminica Dialis; Ch.2 Priestly Couples; Ch.3 Salian Virgins, Sacerdotes, and Ministrae; Ch.4 The Vestal Virgins; Ch.5 The Costume of the Vestal Virgins; Ch.6 The Ritual Activities of the Vestal Virgins; Ch.7 The Vestal Virgins in Roman Politics; Conclusion), the book's overall thesis — inferred from its structure and from the recovered Ch.6–7 argument — is that Republican priestesses occupied genuine public religious offices integrated into, not segregated from, Roman state religion, and that at least the Vestals became increasingly capable political actors over the course of the Republic. ^[inferred]

## Key Claims and Evidence

- Vestals played a prominent ritual role in state *lustratio* (purification) ceremonies, e.g. Tacitus's account of the AD 70 lustration of the Capitol after Vespasian's forces destroyed the Temple of Jupiter, in which the Vestals sprinkled the site with water drawn from fountains and streams before a praetor and pontifex performed a *suovetaurilia* sacrifice.
- Religious knowledge within the Vestal order was transmitted through direct apprenticeship rather than formal institutionalized education: Valerius Maximus describes a young Vestal as the "pupil" (*discipula*) of the chief Vestal Aemilia, and Dionysius of Halicarnassus reports a Vestal's thirty-year career divided into three stages (learning, performing, and teaching duties), a structure DiLuzio connects to Seneca's *De Otio* 2.1–2 description of the Vestal order's division of responsibility by seniority.
- The Vestals were assigned ritual duties connected to the Augustan regime, including incense offerings marking the anniversary of Augustus's election as pontifex maximus, and an annual sacrifice at the altar of Fortuna Redux (dedicated 19 BC) on the Augustalia (October 12); the Vestals were later placed in charge of the cult of the deified Livia under Claudius.
- Political interventions by individual Vestals recur as a theme in Ch.7: Tarpeia (legendary, Regal-period) is read as the archetypal failure of the Vestal's required loyalty to the Roman community over personal/family ties; Claudia (daughter of the consul of 143 BC Ap. Claudius Pulcher) used her person as a Vestal to physically block a tribune from obstructing her father's unsanctioned triumph over the Salassi, an episode ancient authors treat as legitimate largely because of her status; Claudia the Vestal is also invoked by Cicero in the *Pro Caelio* as a model of filial *pietas* contrasted with Clodia.
- Licinia, daughter of a popularis-aligned figure, dedicated a shrine to Bona Dea on the Aventine without the people's authorization; the urban praetor and senate challenged the dedication, and the pontifical college ruled (quoted by Cicero in *De Domo Sua* 136) that an unauthorized dedication on public land was not valid *sacrum*. DiLuzio reads the shrine's Aventine location — tied to Remus's auspices in the plebeian-cause tradition — as evidence Licinia's act was deliberately calculated to signal sympathy with the popularis cause.
- Licinia (likely the same or a related Vestal) also gave her seat at the gladiatorial games to M. Licinius Murena, a gesture Cicero's *Pro Murena* frames as an act of *pietas* toward a relative rather than an improper political favor, though DiLuzio reads it as simultaneously a political act.
- During the Catiline conspiracy (63 BC), the Vestals (including Fabia, cf. [[references/wildfang-romes-vestal-virgins]]) interpreted a dramatic flame/fire omen during a sacrifice and sent word via Terentia (Cicero's wife) urging Cicero to act against the conspirators; the senate's subsequent vote to execute the conspirators followed this episode, with DiLuzio reading the Vestals as having actively orchestrated a portent to influence the political crisis, leveraging their religious prestige to shape the course of events.
- Julius Caesar was appointed *flamen Dialis* by Marius and Cinna in late 87 or early 86 BC; the Vestals (together with other advocates) later successfully interceded with Sulla to spare Caesar's life during the proscriptions — the same episode treated in [[references/wildfang-romes-vestal-virgins]], where Suetonius's account places the Vestals' appeal in the most emphatic position. Both books agree this episode is a landmark instance of collective Vestal political action, though neither recovered DiLuzio passage states explicitly whether she, like Wildfang, frames it as evidence of a historical trend toward greater Vestal political "sophistication" — this specific interpretive framing is attested only in Wildfang. ^[ambiguous]
- During the chaotic triumviral period (40s BC), the Vestals were entrusted with safekeeping important legal documents, valuables, and money (including, per Plutarch/Cassius Dio, Mark Antony's will, later seized by Octavian) — DiLuzio situates Octavian's seizure of the will within the broader pattern of triumviral-era violations of the temple's sanctity and notes the Vestals removed sacred objects from the Temple of Vesta amid the proscriptions' instability.

## Structure / Scope

Covers the full Republican period into the early Principate (explicitly extending at least through events of the AD 70s, per the Tacitus lustration example), organized into eight chapters plus introduction and conclusion moving from the flamen/flaminica Dialis and priestly couples generally (Ch.1–3) to a sustained focus on the Vestals specifically (Ch.4–7: order, costume, ritual activity, and politics). Only Chapters 6–7 and the Bibliography were directly readable in this ingest; the book's treatment of the flaminica Dialis, priestly couples, and Salian virgins (Ch.1–3) is known only by chapter title. ^[ambiguous — due to file corruption, not stated in the source]

## Significance

Based on the recovered chapters, DiLuzio's significance lies in treating individual, named Vestals (Tarpeia, Claudia, Licinia, Fabia) as political agents in their own right rather than as passive religious symbols — a stance that converges with, but was developed independently in a separate chapter from, [[references/wildfang-romes-vestal-virgins]]'s argument that Vestals grew markedly more assertive and "sophisticated" political actors over the course of the Republic. Where Wildfang centers the 73 BC Fabia/Catiline and Licinia/Crassus incestum acquittals as her key evidence, DiLuzio's recovered chapter instead foregrounds the 63 BC Catilinarian omen episode and the Licinia/Bona Dea shrine dispute as her central case studies — complementary rather than overlapping episodes involving some of the same individuals. ^[inferred]
