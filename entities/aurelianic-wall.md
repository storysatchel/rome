# Aurelianic Wall

The Aurelianic Wall is the approximately nineteen-kilometre defensive circuit built around imperial Rome in the AD 270s. Constructed when renewed frontier pressures made the capital’s protection a concern, it continued to serve as part of Rome’s defences into the nineteenth century.

## Defending a large circuit

During the medieval period, a shrinking population without a standing defensive force struggled to man the walls. The development of gunpowder artillery further altered Rome’s defensive geography. Attackers could assault the western papal defences on the Gianicolo, the ancient Janiculum, and use the commanding ground to dominate the city with their guns. The account associates this approach with the attacks of 1527 and 1849.

## The final defence

Papal troops last defended the Aurelianic Wall in 1870. The Italian royal army inflicted some bombardment damage, but resistance at the breach beside Porta Pia was only token. The wall’s long military afterlife thus ended under conditions very different from those for which an ancient defensive circuit had been designed.

## Construction and inherited fabric

The wall was built of brick-faced concrete, incorporating earlier structures along its route and recycling large quantities of brick. Its approximately nineteen-kilometre circuit enclosed the enlarged imperial city, including extensive garden areas that remained open for much of its later history. The construction thus both altered the city’s boundary and helped preserve earlier buildings within the new defences.

The project followed a long interval in which Rome relied principally on provincial armies for protection rather than a maintained urban circuit. Renewed pressure on the northern frontiers in the AD 270s made a defensive enclosure appear necessary again. The wall’s later history should be read against this original response to changed imperial security, rather than beginning only with its medieval difficulties.
