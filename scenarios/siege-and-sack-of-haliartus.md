# The Siege and Sack of Haliartus

In 171 BC, the opening year of the Third Macedonian War against Perseus, the praetor Gaius Lucretius commanded the Roman fleet based at [[entities/cephallenia|Cephallenia]]. He directed operations against the Boeotian city of [[entities/haliartus|Haliartus]], which changed hands between two separate sieges before Lucretius himself took and sacked it, selling its population into slavery. The brutality of this and similar episodes elsewhere in Greece provoked a wave of allied complaints to the senate, and in 170 BC two tribunes of the plebs successfully prosecuted Lucretius, securing his conviction, exile, and a heavy fine.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Gaius Lucretius** ([[entities/gaius-lucretius-praetor171]]) — goal: a decisive early victory in Greece to open the war against Perseus and generate plunder/prestige; resources: command of the Roman fleet, 10,000 marines, 2,000 troops supplied by an allied king under Athenaeus.
- **Haliartus** ([[entities/haliartus]]) — goal: survival as a city; resources: Boeotian troops allied to Rome, city walls — ultimately insufficient against the combined siege force.
- **Publius Lentulus** (unnamed entity, Roman legate) — initiated the first siege of Haliartus with allied Boeotian troops, but was ordered by Lucretius's deputy (acting "in the name of the praetor") to abandon it.
- **Tribunes of the plebs, 170 BC** ([[entities/tribunes-of-the-plebs]]) — goal: hold a Roman commander accountable for atrocities against allies and restore Rome's reputation in Greece; resources: tribunician prosecutorial power before the popular assembly. The trial itself, including the charges and the tribunes' names, is covered in detail at [[scenarios/the-trial-of-gaius-lucretius]].

## What Happened

Lucretius, commanding the fleet at [[entities/cephallenia|Cephallenia]], sent his brother Marcus to sail to [[entities/chalcis|Chalcis]]. Meanwhile his legate Publius Lentulus had begun besieging [[entities/haliartus|Haliartus]] with a contingent of allied Boeotian troops; when Lucretius's deputy arrived at Chalcis, he ordered Lentulus, in the praetor's name, to abandon the siege. "The end of this siege gave way to a new one": Lucretius surrounded Haliartus himself with 10,000 marines and 2,000 of an allied king's troops under a commander named Athenaeus, took the city, and sold the population into slavery.

The pattern repeated across Roman operations in Greece that year: friendly cities were attacked, their leading citizens killed or driven into exile, the rest sold as slaves; other cities were subjected to extortionate demands for money and grain and forced to billet sailors, with attendant abuses. Allied delegations traveled to Rome to complain. The senate responded with conciliatory gestures — kind words, gifts, assurances the abuses lacked senatorial sanction — and a new rule that future requisitions from allies required senate authorization. ^[inferred, connecting Rosenstein's general account of the backlash to the specific Haliartus episode from Sage]

More concretely, in 170 BC two tribunes of the plebs brought Lucretius, the praetor who had commanded the fleet the previous year, to trial for his abuses against the allies in Greece. He was found guilty and condemned to exile and a substantial fine. His successor in the fleet command was separately censured by the senate for similar transgressions.

## Outcome

Haliartus was destroyed as a functioning city and its population enslaved. Gaius Lucretius, despite holding praetorian command, was convicted and driven into exile — a rare case of a sitting-era Roman magistrate facing real legal consequences for wartime atrocities against allies rather than citizens. The episode contributed to a broader senatorial policy shift requiring senate authorization for allied requisitions, and to a Roman diplomatic effort to repair relations with Greek states damaged by the war's opening year.
