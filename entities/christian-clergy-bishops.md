# Christian clergy and bishops

Christian clergy at Oxyrhynchus in Egypt included bishops, presbyters, deacons, readers, and monastics during the third and fourth centuries. Their uneven appearance in papyri and literary sources exposes both the practical work of church leaders and the documentary biases that favour bishops.

## Bishops in the documentary record

Christians first appear in the documentary evidence for Oxyrhynchus in the mid-third century. Its earliest known Christian cleric is papas Sotas, bishop of the growing congregation during the third quarter of that century.

## Wealth and visibility

The prominence of bishops must be read alongside a broader bias in written evidence. Wealthier people tend to generate more documents because owning and managing property requires written transactions. The relative scarcity of lower clergy in the papyri therefore does not measure their actual numbers or importance. The documents still offer occasional glimpses of those less visible ranks.

## Recovering the lower ranks

The study assembles evidence for the period from the mid-third through the end of the fourth century, but no surviving document lists a whole church staff. A cautious count accepts twenty-nine reasonably historical individuals, including at least eleven bishops, four presbyters, four nuns, perhaps five monks, four readers, and a deacon. These are attested persons across a period, not the simultaneous staffing of a church.

Literary narratives add little about lower clergy, while papyri preserve their ordinary activities. Some purported figures known from hagiography are excluded or treated cautiously, illustrating why a longer list is not necessarily a more reliable one.

## A working bishop

Sotas’s dossier consists of six papyrus letters plus an entry in a bishop list surviving in Ethiopic translation. It shows networking with other church leaders, teaching, fundraising, and possibly copying scripture. A letter places him at Antioch, but the suggested connection with a synod about Paul of Samosata remains conjectural. The letters illuminate practical work without making the best-documented bishop automatically the most historically important.
