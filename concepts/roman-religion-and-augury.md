# Roman Religion and Augury

Roman public religion centered on divination through augury — reading bird-signs, sky-signs, and other prodigies to validate or block proposed public acts — and operated through both formal priestly colleges and dedicated religious tribunals.

## Key Ideas

- Driediger-Murphy distinguishes two forms of public augury: permanent-effect signs that "inaugurated" people or places (*auguria*), and action-specific signs pertaining to a single proposed act (*auspicia*), the latter arriving either spontaneously (*auspicia oblativa*) or by ritual solicitation (*auspicia impetrativa*).
- Jupiter was consulted via augurs or magistrates before nearly every major act of Roman public life — creating priests, electing magistrates, holding assemblies, passing legislation, crossing the *pomerium*, crossing rivers with an army, or joining battle — making augury load-bearing infrastructure for the entire Republican political system, not a peripheral ritual.
- Citing Cicero's *De Legibus* 2.31, Driediger-Murphy documents the augurs' formal institutional powers: the right to adjourn or nullify assemblies, invalidate laws, and compel a consul's abdication — powers Cicero, himself an augur from 53/52 BC, openly celebrates rather than criticizes as a political tool.
- Driediger-Murphy argues against the dominant modern scholarly "consensus" (associated with Linderski, Scheid, and Rüpke) that Roman magistrates and augurs instrumentally "created" divine signs to manage political outcomes; she contends Cicero's testimony shows Romans believed augury genuinely expressed and responded to Jupiter's actual will, not elite manipulation dressed as religion.
- Alexander's trial catalog shows Roman religious authority extending into the judicial sphere: the *quaestio apud pontifices* — a religious tribunal distinct from the standing criminal courts — tried the Vestal Virgins for incestum in 114 BC, demonstrating that priestly bodies exercised genuine judicial, not merely advisory, power. See [[concepts/roman-law-and-trials]] for how this sacral jurisdiction related to the broader court system.
- McCall offers a concrete case study of augury's entanglement with elite family rivalry: in 215 BC, a thunderclap heard during consul-elect Marcellus's inauguration led the augurs to rule his election religiously flawed, forcing his resignation in favor of [[entities/clan-fabius|Fabius Maximus]] (Cunctator) — a fellow augur and, that same year, a newly made pontiff — who had a long-standing rivalry with Marcellus over the Fabii's traditional claim to the cavalry-patron god Honos (asserted since 304 BC and reasserted by Fabius's own 233 BC Temple of [[entities/temple-of-honos|Honos]] dedication, which Marcellus's 222 BC joint Honos-and-[[entities/temple-of-virtus|Virtus]] vow at Clastidium appeared to encroach on). McCall declines to resolve whether this reflects genuine religious belief or political manipulation, noting the two were "not mutually exclusive" for Roman aristocrats. ^[inferred]
- Whether Driediger-Murphy's close reading of Cicero's augural theory — an elite, philosophically self-conscious figure — accurately reflects the beliefs of ordinary Roman magistrates practicing augury day-to-day is a tension her book navigates but does not fully resolve. ^[ambiguous]

## Open Questions

- The degree to which augury functioned as genuine religious belief versus a legitimating political instrument remains actively contested between the "functionalist" scholarly consensus and Driediger-Murphy's revisionist reading — this page notes the disagreement rather than adjudicating it. ^[ambiguous]
- Detailed mechanics of specific augural procedures (*tripudium*, *obnuntiatio*) and the Bibulus affair of 59 BC, though central to Driediger-Murphy's argument, were not closely examined for this synthesis and remain open for deeper treatment.
