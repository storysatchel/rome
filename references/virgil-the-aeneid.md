# The Aeneid

This is Robert Fitzgerald's verse translation (Random House, 1981-1983) of Virgil's *Aeneid*, the 12-book Latin epic composed roughly 29-19 BCE under the patronage of Augustus, left unfinished at Virgil's death. The poem narrates the Trojan prince Aeneas's flight from the fall of Troy, his wanderings across the Mediterranean, and his war to establish a Trojan foothold in Latium — the legendary prehistory of Rome.

## Core Philosophy / Narrative

The poem's engine is the conflict between Aeneas's personal desire and his *fatum* (fate/duty) to found the Roman people: the text opens with Juno's enduring hatred of the Trojans — rooted in the Judgment of Paris, the honor paid to Ganymede, and her foreknowledge that "generations born of Trojan blood / Would one day overthrow her Tyrian walls" — driving her to provoke the storm that opens Book I and scatters Aeneas's fleet. Book IV stages the Dido tragedy: the Carthaginian queen's consuming passion ("a wound / Or inward fire eating her away") for Aeneas, encouraged by her sister Anna, ends when Aeneas abandons her at the gods' command to continue toward Italy — read traditionally as Rome's foundational sacrifice of private feeling to public destiny ^[inferred]. Divine machinery drives the plot throughout: Neptune personally calms the sea for Aeneas's fleet while exacting "one life given for many" (the helmsman Palinurus, lulled overboard by Somnus), and Books VI through XII escalate toward war in Italy. The epic closes with the duel between Aeneas and Turnus: Aeneas, on the verge of mercy, kills Turnus in a final rage upon recognizing Pallas's swordbelt worn as a trophy — an ending the poem does not soften or resolve morally ^[ambiguous].

## Key Claims and Evidence

- The poem opens in medias res with Juno's wrath and the storm off Sicily, explicitly framing the entire Trojan ordeal as the cost of founding Rome: "so hard and huge / A task it was to found the Roman people."
- Jupiter, late in Book XII, forecasts the cultural settlement underlying Roman identity: Juno extracts a promise that the Trojans will be absorbed into the Latins — "Let there be Alban kings for generations, / And let Italian valor be the strength / Of Rome in after times... let her [Troy's] name lie fallen" — explicitly folding Trojan ancestry into a renamed, Latin-speaking Roman future. [[concepts/roman-identity-and-cultural-memory]]
- Dido's death and the abandonment narrative in Book IV were read in antiquity (and by this translation's framing) as an etiological myth for the historical enmity between Rome and Carthage ^[inferred].
- Divine intervention is continuous and personal rather than symbolic: Neptune, Venus, Juno, and Jupiter directly alter the plot's physical outcomes (calming seas, deflecting weapons, arming combatants), reflecting an epic worldview in which human agency operates inside, not independent of, divine will.
- The poem ends without closure or an authorial verdict on Aeneas's final act of killing a suppliant Turnus, a structural choice much debated by later critics ^[ambiguous].
- Fitzgerald's translator's note credits Harvard colleagues Mason Hammond and Wendell Clausen with reviewing drafts, situating this as a mid-20th-century American literary rather than purely philological translation.

## Structure / Scope

Twelve books in dactylic-hexameter-derived verse (rendered here in blank verse), following a roughly Odyssean first half (Books I-VI: wanderings, the storm, Carthage/Dido, the funeral games in Sicily, the descent to the underworld) and an Iliadic second half (Books VII-XII: the war in Latium against Turnus and the Rutulians, ending in single combat). The translation includes a contents page titling each book thematically (e.g., "A Fateful Haven," "The Passion of the Queen," "The World Below," "The Fortunes of War"). Geographically the action spans Troy, Carthage, Sicily, Cumae, and Latium (central Italy).

## Significance

As Rome's national epic, commissioned-in-spirit under Augustus, the poem functions as a foundation myth binding the Julian dynasty (via Aeneas's son Ascanius/Iulus) to divine ancestry (Aeneas as son of Venus) and linking Rome's imperial present to a Trojan, rather than purely Latin, origin ^[inferred]. This positions the text as a primary source for [[concepts/roman-identity-and-cultural-memory]] and for Roman ideas of *pietas* (duty to gods, family, and country) as a cardinal virtue, embodied in Aeneas's epithet "pious Aeneas" throughout the tradition ^[inferred].

## Open Questions

- Whether the poem's ending (killing Turnus despite his plea) endorses or critiques the cost of Roman imperial destiny is unresolved in the text itself and remains a central crux of Virgil scholarship. ^[ambiguous]
- The sample read here does not cover Books II, III, VII-XI in detail (the fall of Troy, the Sicilian games, and the bulk of the Italian war), so claims about those books' specific content are not made here.
