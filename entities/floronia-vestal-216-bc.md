# Floronia (Vestal Virgin, d. 216 BC)

Floronia was a Vestal Virgin convicted of *incestum* in 216 BC, in the aftermath of the Roman defeat at [[entities/cannae|Cannae]], together with her colleague [[entities/opimia-vestal-216-bc|Opimia]]. She is the only one of the two whose partner is named: [[entities/lucius-cantilius-pontifical-scribe|L. Cantilius]], a pontifical scribe.

## Conviction

Livy (22.57.2–3) records that the Romans, already terrified by the scale of the slaughter and by other prodigies, were further alarmed because two Vestals that year, Opimia and Floronia, had been found guilty of unchastity (*stupri compertae*). One of them was put to death under the earth at the [[entities/colline-gate|Colline Gate]], "as is the custom", and the other took her own life. Cantilius, "one of the pontifical scribes, whom they now call the lesser pontifices", had committed the offence with Floronia. He was beaten with rods in the [[entities/comitium|Comitium]] by the Pontifex Maximus until he died under the blows. Beating the lover to death was the customary punishment in Vestal cases.

## Death

Livy's Latin, *altera … altera* ("the one … the other"), does not say which Vestal was buried and which killed herself. The two works in the corpus that name both women leave it open, and three others describe the case without naming either. Floronia therefore either died by the customary penalty or took her own life before it. The customary penalty was burial alive in an underground chamber in the [[entities/campus-sceleratus|campus sceleratus]] by the Colline Gate. A modern list of known Vestals records both women simply as "immolated 216 bce".

## Setting

The convictions were treated as part of the religious crisis that followed Cannae. The Romans looked to their own conduct for the cause of the disaster. The Vestals' unchastity was counted among prodigies that had gone unrecognised, and the whole affair, which Livy calls a *nefas*, was treated as a prodigy in its own right. The senate had the Sibylline Books consulted, sent Q. Fabius Pictor to Delphi, and ordered a Gallic and a Greek couple to be buried alive in the [[entities/forum-boarium|Forum Boarium]]. The lex Oppia of 215, which restrained women's dress and behaviour, followed the next year.

The case is also read as aimed at Roman women more broadly. Matrons had filled the streets with public mourning and had gathered at the senate's debate over the Cannae captives. On this reading, the condemnation of the two Vestals served as a symbolic correction of that conduct. Neither reading rests on evidence about Floronia's own actions: Livy records the conviction and her partner's name, but no accusation.
