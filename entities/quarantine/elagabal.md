# Elagabal

Sol Invictus Elagabal was a local sun god of Emesa in Syria, worshipped in the form of a conical black stone, or *baitylos*. The emperor conventionally called Elagabalus had served as the god’s high priest before taking power in 218 and brought the sacred stone to Rome.

## Supremacy at Rome

At the end of 220, the emperor placed Elagabal at the head of the Roman pantheon and adopted the title *sacerdos amplissimus dei invicti Solis Elagabali*. Imperial coins presented the stone in a four-horse chariot as the emperor’s divine protector, while Roman cult objects were gathered in the Palatine temple. The god was also married to the Punic goddess Urania.

After Elagabalus was killed in 222, his successor Alexander restored Jupiter’s primacy and returned the stone to Emesa. The god’s brief supremacy in the capital should be distinguished from the longer history of his local cult.

## Civic responses and the limits of religious unification

Martijn Icks challenges interpretations that make the reforms an early form of empire-wide monotheism or a precursor to Christianity. A divine marriage is incompatible with strict monotheism, and the surviving evidence supplies neither a developed theology nor a promise of an afterlife.

Provincial responses were nevertheless real and varied. The priestly title appears in documents from Numidia and Asisium but is absent from some inscriptions of 221–222; absence alone cannot prove resistance. Several cities minted the stone in a chariot, including Alexandria and cities in Asia Minor, Syria, and Judaea. Such evidence demonstrates awareness and selective adoption, not by itself a universal programme of conversion.
