# The Roman Republic, Vol. 3

## Coverage Note
Volume 3 of William Emerton Heitland's comprehensive history (*The Roman Republic*, Cambridge University Press, 1909; 576 pp.), delivering an exhaustive political and military account of the final half-century of the Republic from the death of Sulla (78 BC) to the collapse of the Liberators at Philippi (42 BC) and the transition to the Principate.

## Sampled Claims and Political Deconstruction
- **The Inherent Weakness of Sulla's Settlement:** Demonstrates that Sulla's reactionary constitution—which restored court juries exclusively to senators and hobbled the tribunate—could not survive the venality and administrative incompetence of the senatorial aristocracy. The consulship of Pompey and Crassus in 70 BC immediately restored full tribunician powers.
- **The Imperium Revolution:** Reconstructs the extraordinary commands granted to Pompey under the *Lex Gabinia* (67 BC) and *Lex Manilia* (66 BC), arguing that delegating sweeping supra-provincial imperium fatally subordinated regular republican magistrates to military dynasts.
- **The First Triumvirate and the Breakdown of Law:** Details how Caesar, Pompey, and Crassus formed a private alliance in 60 BC to monopolize legislation and provinces, leading to Caesar's radical consulship of 59 BC, Clodius's urban gang warfare, the Conference of Luca (56 BC), and the inevitable armed showdown between Caesar and the Senate in 49 BC.
- **Dictatorship, Assassination, and Proscription:** Traces Caesar's military victory in the Civil War, his autocratic reforms as perpetual dictator, his assassination by Brutus and Cassius on the Ides of March 44 BC, and the subsequent rise of the Second Triumvirate, marked by the blood of the proscriptions and the death of Cicero.
