# Vesta

Vesta was associated with the hearth fire both of Roman households and of her public shrine in the Forum. Ancient sources linked the preservation of her cult and fire to the preservation of Rome itself. Her public attendants, the [[entities/vestal-virgins|Vestal Virgins]], were priestesses of a state cult, not simply a household family tending a domestic hearth.

## Shrine, fire, and festival

The Vestals lived beside the *aedes Vestae* and tended its fire. Their recurring duties can be read as a combination of purification and preparation or storage of sacred substances; this is an interpretation of several rites, not a complete ancient definition of the goddess. On 1 March they replaced the shrine's laurel and renewed its fire.

From 7 to 15 June the shrine was opened to Rome's women, with Vesta's festival, the Vestalia, on 9 June. Ovid connects the celebration with bakers, garlanded donkeys, mills and bread, but supplies only hints about the Vestals' actual rites. Servius describes their preparation of *mola salsa* from spelt and salt on three occasions, including the Vestalia. Accounts of 15 June associate the shrine's cleaning with removal of its sweepings.

## Household, state, and interpretation

Vesta's connections with household storage are not identical to those of the [[entities/penates|Penates]]. At Lavinium, late sources describe magistrates sacrificing to both; Servius asks whether Vesta belongs among the Penates or is their companion. Servius and Macrobius differ on whether the magistrates performed the rite on leaving or assuming office, so the timing should not be treated as settled.

Some ancient authors identify Vesta with earth; modern scholars have argued from such texts and archaeological evidence for a chthonic dimension. The significance of the hearth fire is also contested: myths of impregnation by a hearth need not refer to Vesta, and the fire-drill may signify antiquity rather than sexual symbolism. These are interpretations rather than independently established meanings.
