# Quindecimviri Sacris Faciundis

The *Quindecimviri Sacris Faciundis* (The Fifteen Men for Performing Sacred Rites) constituted one of the four prestigious major priestly colleges (*quattuor amplissima collegia*) of ancient Rome, alongside the Pontiffs, the Augurs, and the Epulones. The college's primary constitutional and religious role was the guardianship, consultation, and exegesis of the prophetic *Libri Sibyllini* (Sibylline Books), and the regulation, supervision, and ritual naturalization of non-Roman, foreign (*peregrina*), and Graeco-Roman cults admitted into the city under the *ritus Graecus* (Greek rite).

## Origins and Numerical Expansion

According to Roman foundation lore, the Cumaean Sibyl offered nine books of prophecies to Rome's last king, Tarquinius Superbus. After he refused the exorbitant price, she burned six books before Tarquin purchased the remaining three at the original cost. 

The college charged with their custody underwent significant structural expansions reflecting Roman social struggles:
- **`Duoviri Sacris Faciundis`:** During the regal and early Republican era, the board consisted of two patricians appointed for life.
- **`Decemviri Sacris Faciundis`:** In 367 BCE, under the Licinio-Sextian legislation (*Leges Liciniae Sextiae*), the college was expanded to ten members, five of whom were legally required to be plebeians, marking a milestone in plebeian political emancipation.
- **`Quindecimviri Sacris Faciundis`:** Lucius Cornelius Sulla increased the college to fifteen members in 81 BCE during his constitutional overhaul. Julius Caesar briefly added a sixteenth member in 44 BCE, but the title *Quindecimviri* remained standard throughout the Principate.

The college held its original archives within a stone chest beneath the floor of the [[entities/temple-of-jupiter-capitolinus|Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus]] on the Capitoline. Following the catastrophic fire of 83 BCE that consumed the temple and the original books, senatorial envoys scoured Eryx, Samos, Ilium, and mainland Greece to collect replacement Sibylline verses. In 12 BCE, Augustus, having assumed the office of Pontifex Maximus and serving as a member of the Quindecimviri, purged over two thousand spurious prophetic scrolls from the city and transferred the authentic Sibylline Books into gilded cases beneath the pedestal of the cult statue in the newly constructed Temple of Apollo on the Palatine.

## Custodianship of the Sibylline Books

The *Libri Sibyllini* were written in Greek hexameter verses, frequently acrostic in structure. They were never open to public inspection; unauthorized perusal was punished with extreme severity (in the early Republic, a custodian named Marcus Atilius was sewn into a leather sack and hurled into the sea for transcribing parts of the text).

The Quindecimviri could only open and consult the books upon a formal decree (*senatus consultum*) from the Senate. Invocations of the books occurred almost exclusively during severe national crises:
- Unprecedented military disasters (such as the Battle of Cannae in 216 BCE).
- Persistent plagues, famines, and agricultural failures.
- Dire prodigies that pontifical expiations failed to appease (e.g., rains of blood, multiple hermaphroditic births, terrifying lightning strikes).

Upon consultation, the Quindecimviri did not issue straightforward historical predictions; rather, they delivered an official report (*responsum*) prescribing the necessary rituals to restore the *pax deorum*. These remedies generally involved:
- **`Lectisternia`:** Public ritual banquets where couches (*pulvinaria*) were laid out for pairs of Greek and Roman deities, whose effigies were "fed" sacred meals in the streets.
- **`Supplicationes`:** Solemn public processions where citizens of all ranks, crowned with laurel, visited every temple in the city to placate the gods.
- **Human Propitiation:** In rare, extreme emergencies during the Gallic invasion of 225 BCE and the Second Punic War (216 BCE), the Sibylline Books were interpreted to command the burial alive of two Greeks and two Gauls (one male, one female of each nation) in the [[entities/forum-boarium|Forum Boarium]].

## Regulation of Foreign Cults (*Peregrina Sacra*)

The Quindecimviri functioned as the state's primary instrument for supervising and absorbing foreign religions. While Roman traditionalists feared the corrupting influence of unbridled foreign superstitions (*superstitiones*), the Quindecimviri institutionalized foreign deities by adapting them into the *ritus Graecus*—where worshippers sacrificed bareheaded (*aperto capite*), accompanied by music and Greek hymns, rather than with veiled heads (*capite velato*) in the Roman fashion.

Key foreign cult importations managed by the college included:
1. **The Aventine Triad (496 BCE):** Introducing Ceres, Liber, and Libera (equated with Demeter, Dionysus, and Persephone) during a critical grain shortage, anchoring plebeian sacred autonomy on the Aventine Hill.
2. **Aesculapius (293 BCE):** Following a devastating epidemic, the Quindecimviri dispatched a delegation to Epidaurus in Greece, returning with the sacred serpent of Asklepios and establishing his healing sanctuary on the Tiber Island.
3. **Magna Mater / Cybele (204 BCE):** During Hannibal's invasion of Italy, the Sibylline Books decreed that foreign invaders could be expelled only if the Great Mother of Ida was brought to Rome. The college supervised the transfer of the black meteoric stone from Pessinus in Anatolia to Rome. Recognizing the ecstatic, castrated Anatolian priesthood (*Galli*) as a potential threat to Roman aristocratic decorum, the Quindecimviri established strict boundaries: Roman citizens were forbidden by law from serving as Galli or participating in ecstatic self-flagellation, while the college exercised direct administrative surveillance over the foreign priests within the temple precinct on the Palatine.
4. **Isis and Serapis:** As eastern cults proliferated throughout the late Republic and early Empire, the Quindecimviri were tasked with monitoring foreign shrines in the Campus Martius and regulating foreign sodalities to prevent civic disturbances.

## The *Ludi Saeculares* (Centennial Games)

The Quindecimviri were the designated ceremonial directors of the *Ludi Saeculares*, the grand century-cycle games celebrating the close of one *saeculum* (the longest natural human lifespan, traditionally fixed at 100 or 110 years) and the birth of the next.

Under Augustus in 17 BCE, the Quindecimviri—with Augustus and Marcus Vipsanius Agrippa acting as leading masters of the college—orchestrated the revived *Ludi Saeculares*. The college commissioned Horace to compose the famous choral hymn, the *Carmen Saeculare*, which was sung on the Palatine and Capitoline Hills by a chorus of twenty-seven boys and twenty-seven girls of living parents (*patrimi et matrimi*). The official inscribed acts of the festival (*Acta Ludorum Saecularium*), discovered epigraphically in the Campus Martius, detail the exhaustive administrative operations of the Quindecimviri in distributing ritual purifications (sulfur and bitumen) and conducting nocturnal sacrifices to the Fates (*Moerae*), Ilithyia, and Mother Earth (*Terra Mater*).

Subsequent celebrations of the games—under Claudius (47 CE), Domitian (88 CE), and Septimius Severus (204 CE)—were similarly planned and executed under the direct authority of the Quindecimviri, maintaining unbroken ritual continuity into the Severan period.
