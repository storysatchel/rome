# Tribune of the Plebs

The tribunate of the plebs (*tribunus plebis*) was a Roman magistracy — ultimately ten officeholders strong by the late third century BC, per Gargola — that presided over the *concilium plebis* and, over the course of the Republic, became the dominant engine of Roman legislation. Unlike the consulship or praetorship, it was not part of the regular *cursus honorum* of imperium-holding magistrates, but it carried the power to summon citizens and bring binding measures to a vote.

## Key Ideas

- By the late third century BC, Rome's magistracies included two consuls, a growing number of praetors (one at first, rising to six by 197 BC), curule and plebeian aediles, quaestors, irregular boards, and ten tribunes of the plebs. Per Gargola, officials holding the *ius agendi cum populo* could summon citizens either to *contiones* (speeches/announcements) or to *comitia*/*concilia* (voting assemblies); tribal assemblies — convened as the *concilium plebis* under a tribune — typically handled legislation by the mid-Republic onward.
- The tribunate presided specifically over the *concilium plebis*, traditionally established after the first *secessio* of 494 BC. Per Sandberg, the precise relationship between the *concilium plebis* and the broader *comitia tributa* (the full citizen body voting by tribe) is disputed in modern scholarship — some treat them as wholly distinct bodies, others as effectively identical — and Sandberg flags this as a problem of "greatest relevance" unresolved even within his own specialized study. ^[ambiguous]
- A decision of the full *populus* was technically a *lex*, while a decision of the *concilium plebis* under a tribune was a *plebiscitum* — a distinct and originally narrower category. After the *lex Hortensia* of 287 BC gave plebiscites the same binding force on all citizens (the *exaequatio legibus*), the two terms became interchangeable in common usage, though some inscriptions preserve the older technical distinction.
- Sandberg's study of Roman legislative practice (367–88 BC) traces a rising dominance of tribunician legislation over consular legislation across the middle and late Republic — tribunes, rather than consuls, increasingly became the magistrates who actually brought binding measures (*rogationes*) before the voting citizenry. ^[inferred: Sandberg frames this as part of a broader shift toward popular constitutional practice, though the underlying evidentiary base he works from is explicitly thin]
- The formal assent of the senate (*patrum auctoritas*), once apparently required for decisions of the assemblies (including those passed under tribunician initiative) to take legal effect, became over time a mere formality — part of what Sandberg reads as a longer trajectory from aristocratic toward more genuinely popular constitutional practice. ^[inferred]
- A bill (*rogatio*) underwent a formal *promulgatio* (public posting, typically weeks ahead) and public debate across a series of *contiones* before being put to a binary up-or-down vote (*uti rogas* or *antiquo*) in a reconstituted *comitia* — a procedural template Sandberg's study applies equally to tribunician *rogationes* and those of other magistrates.

## Open Questions

- Whether the *concilium plebis* and *comitia tributa* were institutionally distinct bodies or effectively the same assembly under different procedural names remains disputed and is not resolved by either citing source. ^[ambiguous]
- Sandberg concedes the evidentiary base for reconstructing the mechanics of tribunician legislation is "extremely limited," and frames his own conclusions as showing existing consensus views are problematic rather than proving a definitive alternative — so the precise chronology of the tribunate's rise to legislative dominance remains provisional. ^[ambiguous]
