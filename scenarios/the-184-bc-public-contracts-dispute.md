# The 184 BC Public Contracts Dispute — Titus Flamininus Overturns Cato's Censorial Auctions

As censors in 184 BC, Cato and L. Valerius Flaccus auctioned a great number of public contracts — for new public works, maintenance of existing properties, and collection of rents and imposts (*vectigalia*) — driving construction contract prices down and rental/tax contract prices up to squeeze maximum value for the state. The successful bidders, finding their margins dangerously narrow, complained; Titus Flamininus took up their cause and had the Senate annul the contracts, forcing a second auction.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Cato the Elder** ([[entities/cato-the-elder]]) — goal: maximize state revenue and minimize state expenditure on public works; resources: censorial authority to set contract terms.
- **Lucius Valerius Flaccus** ([[entities/lucius-valerius-flaccus]]) — goal: support Cato's fiscal program as co-censor; resources: shared censorial authority.
- **Titus Quinctius Flamininus** ([[entities/titus-quinctius-flamininus]]) — goal: reverse the censors' terms (and, per Plutarch, avenge his brother Lucius's expulsion from the Senate); resources: immense senatorial prestige, ability to carry a Senate majority.
- **The successful public contractors** — goal: renegotiate contract terms seen as too narrow to guarantee profit without risking default; resources: collective political lobbying through Flamininus.
- **M. Caelius, tribune of the plebs** — goal: unclear, apparently incited by Flamininus; resources: tribunician power to propose a fine against Cato.

## What Happened

After the first auction, contractors who had won the public works contracts at Cato and Flaccus's deliberately tight terms began complaining that their margins were too narrow to guarantee fulfillment without risking default. Titus Flamininus took up their case and persuaded the Senate to order the contracts annulled and reissued. Plutarch suggests Titus was motivated at least partly by anger at his brother Lucius's humiliating expulsion from the Senate during the same censorship (see [[scenarios/the-184-bc-senatorial-purge-lucius-flamininus-and-manilius]]), though his ability to win over a Senate that had already approved vast public works expenditure suggests a more substantive economic argument carried weight too. ^[extracted] During the same dispute, Flamininus also incited the tribune M. Caelius to attempt a move imposing a heavy fine on Cato, prompting Cato's speech *Si se M. Caelius tribunus plebis appellasset*. ^[extracted]

Obliged to restart the auction, Cato and Flaccus excluded all the previously successful bidders and reassigned the contracts on terms only slightly more favorable to the new contractors than before — effectively conceding little real ground while technically complying with the Senate's order. ^[inferred]

## Outcome

The contracts were annulled and re-auctioned under marginally better terms for contractors, but Cato retained the upper hand in substance. The tribunician move to fine Cato was dropped, apparently before reaching formal proceedings; the fine, in any case, was never paid.

## Open Questions

- Whether the Senate's motive was genuinely fiscal prudence (fear of contractor defaults) or primarily personal payback against Cato on Titus Flamininus's behalf remains disputed. ^[ambiguous]
- The full substance of the charge implied against Cato in the Caelius affair is not preserved.
