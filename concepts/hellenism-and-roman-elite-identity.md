# Hellenism and Roman Elite Identity

Astin and Gruen independently converge on the view that the Roman elite's relationship to Greek ("Hellenic") culture in the 3rd–2nd centuries BC was not the contradictory, anxious "love-hate" posture of older scholarship, but a confident, deliberate process through which Romans appropriated and adapted Greek culture to construct their own distinct identity. ^[inferred]

## Key Ideas

- **Cato as the test case, not the exception.** Astin's biography of Cato the Elder argues the man was both hostile to Greek philosophy and medicine and simultaneously demonstrably well-read in Greek literature — and treats this as "broad continuity and consistency" rather than psychological paradox: a coherent, calculated stance. Gruen's later, more extended treatment reaches the same conclusion independently, reinterpreting Cato as "a subtle and sophisticated thinker." Cato's gibes against Isocrates' pupils, his branding of Socrates a "babbler" undermining law and tradition, and his retelling of Hippocrates refusing to treat "barbarian" enemies of Greece are read by Gruen as a "deliberate design" to assert Latin cultural superiority, continuous with his sumptuary legislation (*lex Oppia* repeal) and his speech *de signis et tabulis* against private display of captured art — a single consistent program, not isolated outbursts.
- **Rejection of the "factional" model.** Gruen explicitly rejects the older scholarly framing of a "Scipionic circle"/philhellene party opposed to a "Catonian"/anti-Hellenic party: he finds no consistent factional alignment on Hellenism in the mid-2nd century evidence, and judges both the "philhellene vs. anti-Hellene" factional model and the "collective inferiority complex" explanation inadequate.
- **Genuine absorption, not mere affectation.** Gruen's closing case study is the satirist Lucilius, who mocks philhellenic *affectation* (e.g., Albucius's pretentious Greek mannerisms) but shows no anti-Greek bias himself — quoting Homer, Archilochus, Euripides, Plato, and Xenophon, and alluding to Epicurean atomism and Academic philosophy. Gruen reads this as evidence of genuine cultural absorption by the nobility, with satire targeting pretension rather than Hellenism as such.
- Astin's resolution (1978) anticipates and is closely paralleled by Gruen's argument (1992); Gruen's treatment converges with and extends Astin's but pushes further toward reading Cato as a sophisticated strategist of Roman cultural self-definition rather than merely an internally consistent conservative. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- Whether Cato's "deliberate design" reading fully accounts for the apparent tension between his private erudition and public anti-Hellenic rhetoric, or whether some genuine ambivalence remains underneath the "consistency" framing both scholars favor, is not fully settled by either source — both scholars argue against paradox, but neither claims to have produced a complete psychological account.
