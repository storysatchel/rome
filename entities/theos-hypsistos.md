# Theos Hypsistos

*Theos Hypsistos*, the “Highest God,” is a Greek divine designation found in inscriptions of the Roman eastern Mediterranean and adjoining regions. Worshippers also addressed *Zeus Hypsistos*, “Zeus Most High.” The similarity of these names raises a historical question rather than settling it: in some places the two designations occur together, but elsewhere Zeus appears in a distinctive, recognisably Olympian setting. They should not automatically be treated as names of one uniformly organised religion.

## Zeus, sanctuaries and images

In Macedonia, sanctuaries of Zeus Hypsistos have been identified at Dion and outside Beroia. At Dion, excavators found fourteen eagle reliefs; elsewhere in the Macedonian evidence there are statues and figured reliefs of Zeus. These remains fit readily within Greek Olympian worship. In the evidence surveyed for Macedonia and Thessaly, the deity is named Zeus rather than Theos Hypsistos: the figure is neither anonymous nor without an image. Zeus is the Greek counterpart of [[entities/jupiter|Jupiter]], but that comparison does not establish that a particular dedication to the Highest God was addressed to Jupiter.

The relationship is closer at some other sites. Dedications to Zeus Hypsistos and Theos Hypsistos occur at sanctuaries including Athens and Nicomedia, and eagles appear with both designations. Their proximity makes identification plausible in those settings, not inevitable everywhere. An eagle can signal a religious association without by itself proving precisely how an individual worshipper understood the god.

## A god who sees and protects

An altar dedication from Amastris represents the Highest God as unseen yet able to observe wrongdoing, and asks for protection from a danger to mortals. The inscription says the altar was erected on the command of Apollo. A possible connection to the epidemic under Marcus Aurelius is a scholarly suggestion, not a date secured by the wording alone. An inscription from Alexandria, as reported in modern scholarship, has a dead young woman invoke the all-seeing Highest God alongside Helios and Nemesis to avenge her death. These examples show the title used in appeals for protection and justice, including one invocation that names other divine powers.

At Oinoanda an inscribed oracle presents a god beyond containment by a single name, associates him with fire and all-seeing aether, and directs prayer toward the rising sun. Its relationship to Highest God worship is an interpretation supported by parallels, rather than an identity established simply by the oracle itself. Lamps and fire appear in the scholarly reconstruction of cult practice; they are evidence of ritual media, not proof that all communities used an identical rite.

## One god, or a shared language of exaltation?

The inscriptions invite different readings of religious supremacy. Stephen Mitchell reconstructs a widespread Highest God cult with shared features and local variations, while distinguishing the Macedonian Zeus material. He sees a particularly strong theological significance in *hypsistos*. Angelos Chaniotis instead warns that common language can conceal diverse local conceptions; he disputes identifying every dedication with a single type of worshipper or calling the practice monotheistic. A dedication at Kollyda to a local patron goddess as *Thea Hypsiste* illustrates why a related superlative need not everywhere designate the same divine person.

A title asserting supreme power does not on its own show whether a worshipper denied the existence of other gods, honoured one god above others, or employed a familiar formula in a particular setting. Similarly, a dedication naming a single deity does not by itself exclude the worship of others. The evidence allows both comparison and disagreement over the appropriate categories.

## Jewish connections and later testimony

Some Highest God dedications have been interpreted in Jewish contexts. Two manumission documents from Gorgippia, treated as Jewish in the scholarship consulted, combine dedication to Theos Hypsistos with an oath invoking Zeus, Earth and Sun; the significance of the oath formula requires interpretation. Mitchell proposes substantial overlap between non-Jewish worshippers of the Highest God and the *theosebeis* or “God-fearers” associated with Jewish communities. That is a reconstruction of religious contact, not proof that every person using the title belonged to a synagogue or shared the same beliefs.

Later Christian authors provide another kind of evidence, filtered through their own descriptions of other groups. Cyril of Alexandria reported people in Palestine and Phoenicia who worshipped the Highest God while acknowledging other heavenly and earthly powers. The extent to which such reports identify a continuous, unified movement across regions remains disputed.
