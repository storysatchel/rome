# Septemviri Epulonum

The *Septemviri Epulonum* (The Seven Men of the Sacred Feasts), originally the *Tresviri Epulones*, formed the fourth of Rome’s major priestly colleges (*quattuor amplissima collegia*), alongside the Pontiffs, Augurs, and Quindecimviri. Established in 196 BCE during the Middle Republic, the college was created to relieve the overworked College of Pontiffs of the logistical and ceremonial burdens of organizing the great state sacrificial banquets (*epula*), most notably the *Epulum Jovis* (Feast of Jupiter) celebrated during the *Ludi Romani* and the *Ludi Plebeii*.

## Historical Foundation and Expansion

Throughout the early and middle Republic, the preparation of public sacrificial banquets was the duty of the [[entities/collegium-pontificum|College of Pontiffs]]. However, as Rome expanded across the Mediterranean following the Second Punic War, the number, scale, and duration of civic festivals and games (*ludi*) multiplied dramatically, straining the pontiffs' administrative capacity.

- **Establishment in 196 BCE:** On the motion of the tribune of the plebs Gaius Licinius Lucullus, the popular assembly passed a law creating the *Tresviri Epulones* (a three-man board of banquet directors). Licinius Lucullus himself was elected as one of the inaugural three epulones, alongside Publius Manlius and Publius Porcius Laeca (Livy 33.42). From its inception, the office was open to plebeians.
- **Sullan and Caesarian Expansions:** Sulla expanded the board to seven members in 81 BCE, from which point the college was designated the *Septemviri Epulonum*. Julius Caesar temporarily increased the membership to ten in 44 BCE to accommodate loyal supporters seeking patrician-grade priestly honors, but under Augustus and the early Principate the number stabilized back at seven (retaining the archaic title *septemviri* even when emperors occasionally added supernumerary members).
- **Insignia and Status:** By senatorial decree, the epulones were granted the right to wear the *toga praetexta* (purple-bordered toga) during the performance of their duties, placing them on an equal ceremonial plane with the senior curule magistrates and fellow pontiffs.

## Ritual Duties: The *Epulum Jovis*

The crowning responsibility of the Septemviri Epulonum was the staging of the *Epulum Jovis*, held annually on the Ides of September (September 13) during the *Ludi Romani*, and on the Ides of November (November 13) during the *Ludi Plebeii*.

The ritual unfolded in the [[entities/temple-of-jupiter-capitolinus|Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus]] on the Capitoline:
1. **The Divine Banquet:** Within the temple cella, the Epulones arranged elaborate banqueting couches (*pulvinaria*). An effigy of Jupiter Optimus Maximus—with his face traditionally painted with red minium—was laid in a reclining posture upon a luxury couch, conforming to standard Roman male banqueting etiquette. Adjacent to Jupiter, statues of Juno and Minerva were positioned seated upright on chairs (*sellae*), reflecting ancient Roman gender conventions for respectable matrons.
2. **The Sacred Offering:** A sumptuous multi-course meal was ritually served to the Capitoline Triad. While the gods symbolically consumed the spiritual essence of the banquet through burning incense and libations poured upon altars, the Epulones, sitting alongside the consuls and senators invited to feast in the temple precinct, consumed the consecrated meat and food.
3. **Public Feasting (*Visceratio*):** Following the private aristocratic meal within the temple precinct, the banquets spilled out onto the Capitoline square and into the Forum. The Epulones coordinated with the curule and plebeian aediles to manage the distribution of sacrificial meat (*visceratio*) and wine to the Roman plebs, transforming the sacrifice into a vast civic communion reinforcing the social hierarchy.

## Logistics, Sumptuary Law, and Civic Politics

The staging of public epula required massive culinary logistics, procurement of cattle, sheep, and pigs, and management of specialized guilds of cooks, cupbearers, bakers, and temporary service slaves. 

The Epulones served as the vital administrative nexus between civic magistrates (the consuls and aediles financing the games), commercial suppliers, and the strict religious rules governing sacrificial foods. Because public feasts were prime opportunities for aristocratic candidates to curry electoral favor with the voting tribes through lavish displays of edible wealth, the Epulones frequently operated at the center of sumptuary regulation debates (such as the *Lex Orchia*, *Lex Fannia*, and *Lex Didia*), which attempted to limit banquet costs and guest numbers while exempting official state-mandated *epula publica*.

In the Late Republic, ambitious politicians sought election to the Septemviri Epulonum as an indispensable rung in building political stature. Membership provided high visibility during public spectacles, directly connecting the magistrate's name with public munificence (*munificentia*) and popular gratification.

## Imperial Transformation

Under the Principate, the Septemviri Epulonum formed part of the elite *quattuor amplissima collegia* that marked the apex of a successful senatorial career (*cursus honorum*). Inscriptions throughout Rome and the western provinces record senators boasting of holding the title of *Septemvir Epulonum* alongside consular and proconsular commands.

The college took on additional responsibilities for organizing imperial anniversary banquets, celebrating the birthdays (*natalicia*) and accessions of the reigning princeps and deceased deified emperors (*divi*). Augustus, Tiberius, and their successors regularly included themselves as members of the college, ensuring that public feasting reinforced dynastic loyalty alongside the traditional veneration of Jupiter Capitolinus.
