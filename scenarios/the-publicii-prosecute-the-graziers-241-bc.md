# The Publicii Prosecute the Graziers (241 BC)

According to Ovid, a long-unpunished custom had grown up of herders grazing their animals on public pastures (*ager publicus*) beyond the legal limits on herd size established under the Licinian legislation. "The masses lacked champions to protect public land, and the lazy alone grazed privately," Ovid writes — until the plebeian aediles of 241 BC, known as the Publicii, finally brought the practice before the courts.

## Parties & Stakes

- **The Publicii** ([[entities/publicii-aediles]]) — goal: enforce the legal limit on livestock grazed on public land and restore popular control over *ager publicus*; resources: aedilician authority to prosecute and fine.
- **The graziers/cattle breeders** (unnamed) — goal: continue exploiting public pasture beyond the legal animal limit without penalty; resources: existing herds and years of unchallenged customary practice.

## What Happened

Ovid records that "this licence was arraigned before the Publicii, aediles of the plebs. Before that men's courage had failed them. The people recovered their rights, the guilty were fined." ^[extracted] Roselaar connects this to a broader pattern of sporadic enforcement of grazing limits on *ager publicus*, noting similar fines on cattle breeders recorded for 296, 293, 196, and 193 BC, though the specific legal basis for those later fines is less certain. ^[extracted] The precise maximum herd size in force at the time (likely derived from the Licinian-era limit of 500 iugera of land and some number of grazing animals) is not stated by Ovid.

## Outcome

The guilty graziers were fined, and Ovid frames the episode as a popular vindication — "the people recovered their rights" — after a period in which illegal grazing had gone unchallenged for lack of aedilician initiative.

## Open Questions

- The names of the two Publicii aediles and the identities or number of graziers fined are not preserved.
- The precise cumulative animal limit being enforced (versus simple unauthorized grazing on land not legally occupied) is debated by modern scholars. ^[ambiguous]
