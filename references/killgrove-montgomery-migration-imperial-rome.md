# All Roads Lead to Rome: Exploring Human Migration through Biochemistry of Skeletons

## Coverage Note
Groundbreaking scientific study by Kristina Killgrove and Janet Montgomery (*PLOS ONE* 11.2, 2016, e0147985; 30 pp.) providing the first multi-isotopic biochemical evidence of geographic migration and dietary integration among common inhabitants of imperial Rome (1st–3rd centuries AD).

## Sampled Claims and Bioarchaeological Data
- **Isotopic Profiling of Imperial Cemeteries:** Analyzed strontium (^87Sr/^86Sr), oxygen (δ^18O), and carbon (δ^13C) isotopes in tooth enamel and bone collagen from 105 individuals buried at Castellaccio Europarco (suburban lower-class cemetery) and Casal Bertone (urban working-class cemetery near a large fullonica).
- **Quantifying Non-Local Immigrants:** Identified non-local individuals whose enamel strontium ratios deviated from the local Rome geological baseline (0.7088–0.7102). Strontium alone detected at least 4% to 8% foreign-born migrants, while oxygen isotopes revealed that up to 30% of the individuals had spent their early childhood outside central Italy (in alpine, northern European, or arid North African/Near Eastern regions).
- **Demographic Profiles of Migrants:** Demonstrates that non-local migrants at Casal Bertone were overwhelmingly adult males and older children/adolescents, reflecting voluntary labor migration and the extensive involuntary trafficking of enslaved persons to the imperial capital.
- **Rapid Dietary Homogenization:** Carbon and nitrogen isotope analyses revealed that once immigrants settled in Rome, their diets rapidly converged with the local urban population, heavily dominated by state-subsidized C3 Mediterranean wheat and legumes, with modest marine protein consumption.
