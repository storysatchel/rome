# Roman Household Staff

Cato's and Varro's agricultural handbooks give the most detailed surviving prescriptions for how a working Roman farm was to be staffed: specific roles, ratios, duties, and rations for the enslaved *familia rustica* under the direction of an overseer and housekeeper. For the household as a broader social/legal unit, see [[concepts/roman-household]].

## Key Ideas

- **Staffing ratios tied to farm size.** Cato specifies that an olive farm of 240 *jugera* (~160 acres) should be stocked with thirteen hands — an overseer, a housekeeper, five laborers, three ox-drivers, a swineherd, an ass-driver, and a shepherd — plus draft animals (*De Agri Cultura* X), giving a concrete picture of labor allocation on a mid-sized estate.
- **The overseer (*vilicus*).** The overseer's duties are laid out as a quasi-moral code: maintain discipline, settle disputes, prevent theft by ensuring no one goes hungry, keep no hangers-on, consult no soothsayers or astrologers, and work alongside the hands rather than merely supervise (*De Agri Cultura* V). The *pater familias*'s periodic inspection tours, cross-examining the overseer on work completed versus excuses offered (bad weather, sick slaves), are described as the core mechanism of estate accountability (*De Agri Cultura* II).
- **The housekeeper (*vilica*).** Paired with the overseer, the housekeeper is responsible for household religious observance on behalf of the *familia*, keeping the hearth clean, making preserves, and explicitly instructed not to gossip, receive visitors, or attend parties (*De Agri Cultura* CXLIII) — reflecting gendered and status-bound expectations of domestic order on a working estate. This is treated as a key primary-source data point for household staffing structure generally.
- **Rationing as a management tool.** Detailed rationing schedules for the *familia rustica* specify meal, bread, wine, and salt allowances by role and season (*De Agri Cultura* LVI–LIX), illustrating the material economy of a slave-staffed farm and functioning as a concrete, quantified complement to the overseer/housekeeper's disciplinary duties.
- Varro's parallel but distinct emphasis — agriculture's purpose as both profit (*fructus*) and pleasure (*voluptas*), with Book III devoted substantially to pleasure-oriented installations (his own aviary, fishponds, game preserves at Casinum) — suggests that by the late Republic, elite estate "staffing" needs had expanded beyond Cato's austere production model to include staff serving aristocratic leisure, though the source does not detail specific leisure-staff roles the way it does for Cato's production staff. ^[inferred]

## Open Questions

- The source itself flags that the extent to which Cato's and Varro's idealized staffing ratios and duty codes reflect universal practice versus the authors' own particular estates is not addressed within the text and remains debated among agrarian historians. ^[ambiguous]
- Neither treatise addresses the lived experience or interiority of the enslaved *familia rustica* whose labor the staffing system depends on; their presence is visible only through management instructions and ration tables.
