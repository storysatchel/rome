# Introduction: Intelligence Ancient and Modern

## Coverage Note
Introductory theoretical chapter of Rose Mary Sheldon's authoritative work (*Intelligence Activities in Ancient Rome*, Routledge, 2005, pp. 1–7) establishing the methodological vocabulary, operational definitions, and historical necessity of intelligence gathering across Roman history.

## Sampled Claims and Methodological Framework
- **The Imperative of Ancient Intelligence:** Challenges the widespread historiographical fallacy that because the Romans lacked a centralized intelligence agency (analogous to the CIA or MI6), they operated without intelligence. Sheldon demonstrates that maintaining an empire spanning three continents over a millennium was structurally impossible without systematic information collection.
- **Taxonomy of Roman Intelligence Activities:** Establishes clear categories of Roman intelligence operations:
  1. Tactical reconnaissance in the field (*exploratio*).
  2. Strategic and political intelligence gathered via diplomatic envoys (*legati*), allied client kings, and merchant caravans.
  3. Counter-espionage and covert political disruption in foreign states.
  4. Internal security and domestic political surveillance of Roman citizens.
- **Historical Trajectory:** Outlines the systemic evolution from informal, ad hoc aristocratic scouting networks in the Republic to centralized military and bureaucratic intelligence bodies (*frumentarii*, *agentes in rebus*) in the imperial autocracy.
