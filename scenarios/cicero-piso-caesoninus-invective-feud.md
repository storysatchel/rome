# The Cicero–Piso Caesoninus Invective Feud

L. Calpurnius Piso Caesoninus, consul in 58 BC (the year Cicero was driven into exile) and father-in-law of Julius Caesar, had supported P. Clodius Pulcher in engineering Cicero's banishment. Cicero, after his recall, secured Piso's early removal from his governorship of [[entities/macedonia|Macedonia]] as a form of political revenge. The feud escalated into open, sustained public invective.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Cicero** ([[entities/cicero]]) — goal: publicly discredit Piso and justify his own earlier exile and later revenge against him; resources: oratorical fame, the senate floor, written circulation of speeches.
- **L. Calpurnius Piso Caesoninus** ([[entities/lucius-calpurnius-piso-caesoninus]]) — goal: defend his record as governor and consul, and reclaim the narrative of 58 BC; resources: a reputation for moderation and gravity, connections through his daughter's marriage to Caesar.

## What Happened

In a senate speech of 55 BC, Piso defended himself against Cicero's accusations, arguing that Cicero's exile had been his own fault, not Piso's doing — and mockingly cited lines from a self-congratulatory poem Cicero had written about his own consulship ("cedant arma togae," "let arms yield to the toga"), joking that it was the poem's vanity, not Piso's actions, that had provoked public backlash against Cicero. Cicero answered with his ferocious invective *In Pisonem*, a detailed character assassination. Piso did not let the matter rest: in the summer of 54 BC he circulated a written pamphlet responding to Cicero's attack. Cicero's brother Quintus urged a further reply, but Cicero chose instead to claim rhetorical victory by note that Roman schoolchildren were already memorizing the *In Pisonem* — effectively letting Piso have the last public word while declaring himself the literary winner.

## Outcome

Piso was never prosecuted for misconduct in Macedonia, which strengthened his public standing relative to Cicero's accusations. He went on to be elected censor in 50 BC, suggesting the feud did lasting damage to neither man's career, even as it produced one of Cicero's most quoted invectives and proved that Piso — dismissed by Cicero as a mediocre speaker — could hold his own in a public war of words.

## Open Questions

- The content of Piso's 54 BC pamphlet is entirely lost; only Cicero's side of the final exchange survives directly.
