# The 66 BC Ambitus Convictions That Unseated Two Consuls-Elect

In 66 BC, both men elected consul for the following year — P. Autronius Paetus and P. Cornelius Sulla (nephew of the dictator) — were convicted of electoral bribery (*ambitus*) under the *lex Calpurnia* before they could even take office, and were replaced by the men their prosecutors represented (Alexander, cases #200–201).

## Parties & Stakes

- **P. Autronius Paetus** ([[entities/publius-autronius-paetus]]), cos. des. 65 — convicted; lost the consulship before taking it up.
- **P. Cornelius Sulla** ([[entities/publius-cornelius-sulla-cos-desig-65]]), cos. des. 65 — convicted on the same charge, same year.
- **L. Aurelius Cotta** ([[entities/lucius-aurelius-cotta-cos-65]]), cos. 65, cens. 64 — prosecuted and effectively replaced Autronius Paetus as consul.
- **L. Manlius Torquatus** ([[entities/lucius-manlius-torquatus-cos-65]]), cos. 65 — prosecuted Sulla (with his son, also named L. Manlius Torquatus, as subordinate prosecutor/*subscriptor*), and replaced him as consul.

## What Happened

Autronius and Sulla had won the consular elections for 65 BC, but their defeated rivals, Cotta and the elder Torquatus, brought bribery prosecutions against the winners under the standing *lex Calpurnia de ambitu*. Both prosecutions succeeded: Autronius was prosecuted by Cotta and convicted; Sulla was prosecuted by the elder Torquatus (assisted by his own son as junior prosecutor) and likewise convicted. The convictions came early enough in the year that the elections were effectively redone in substance — Cotta and Torquatus, the men who had brought the prosecutions, assumed the consulships that had briefly belonged to their rivals.

## Outcome

Rome entered 65 BC with Cotta and Torquatus as consuls instead of Autronius and Sulla. Ancient sources (Sallust, Asconius, Suetonius, Cassius Dio) connect the disgraced Autronius's subsequent bitterness directly to his later involvement in conspiratorial violence alongside Catiline — the so-called "First Catilinarian Conspiracy" rumor of an aborted plot to assassinate the new consuls on the day they took office is explicitly tied by ancient sources to resentment from this very prosecution.

## Open Questions

- The precise division of labor between the elder and younger Torquatus as prosecutor and subordinate prosecutor in Sulla's trial is disputed among ancient and modern sources Alexander cites, hinging on an ambiguous phrase in Cicero's *Pro Sulla*. ^[ambiguous]
