# Caesar Judges Sulla's Hitmen: The 64 BC Proscription-Murder Trials

In 64 BC — nearly two decades after Sulla's proscriptions had legally shielded those who killed the men on his death-lists — [[entities/julius-caesar]], presiding as *iudex quaestionis* of the murder court, revived old charges against surviving killers of the proscribed, convicting two minor figures while his own political ally Catiline escaped on a related charge (Alexander, cases #215–217).

## Parties & Stakes

- **L. Bellienus** ([[entities/lucius-bellienus]]), pr. 105 — convicted of murdering Q. Lucretius Afella (a Sullan partisan killed for defying Sulla's own authority, not technically a victim of the formal proscription lists).
- **L. Luscius** ([[entities/lucius-luscius-centurion]]), a centurion — convicted of three separate murders of men formally proscribed under Sulla.
- **L. Sergius Catilina** ([[entities/lucius-sergius-catilina]]), pr. 68 — tried on the same charge (killing of the proscribed) shortly after the other two, and acquitted, with consular *laudatores* speaking in his support.
- **C. Iulius Caesar** ([[entities/julius-caesar]]) — presided over all three cases as *iudex quaestionis*, in a legal maneuver that effectively reopened Sulla's proscriptions as prosecutable crimes two decades after the fact (some modern scholars argue Caesar in fact acted as *accusator* rather than presiding judge in these cases).

## What Happened

Caesar's political project in these years included rehabilitating the memory and the surviving victims' families of Sulla's proscriptions, which by 64 BC had passed from live political controversy into contested historical memory. As *iudex quaestionis* for the standing murder court (*lex Cornelia de sicariis et veneficis*), Caesar heard a cluster of cases against men who had killed individuals under color of Sulla's now-defunct proscription authority, timed deliberately to land just before that year's consular elections. Bellienus and Luscius, both relatively obscure figures without consular rank or major political protection, were convicted. Catiline — tried on essentially the same legal theory for his own killings during the proscriptions — was acquitted shortly afterward, with an array of consulars speaking in his defense, underscoring how political connection, not the underlying legal theory, determined outcomes in these revived cases.

## Outcome

Two minor Sullan killers were convicted decades after their crimes, establishing that the legal shield Sulla's dictatorial legislation had provided was not permanently immune to later prosecution. Catiline's acquittal on the same charge, by contrast, demonstrated that the same prosecutorial theory produced very different results depending on the defendant's political standing — a contrast ancient sources (Suetonius, Cassius Dio) explicitly drew out as evidence of Caesar's selective, politically calculated use of his judicial position.

## Open Questions

- Whether Caesar's formal role was presiding *iudex quaestionis* or, as some modern scholars argue, an *accusator* (prosecutor) is disputed in the sources Alexander cites. ^[ambiguous]
