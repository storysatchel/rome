# Roman Military Organization (Republic)

Scoped to the internal structure, recruitment, and bureaucracy of the Roman army itself, as distinct from [[concepts/roman-provincial-administration]], which covers how Rome governed and extracted resources from conquered territory once the army had secured it.

## Key Ideas

- Drogula's contribution to the Armstrong/Fronda volume argues early Roman military command was not a unified regal prerogative transferred intact to the consulship in 509 BC; instead, individual *gentes* (clans) commanded private warbands and conducted independent raiding and defense through the regal period and early Republic, with the *imperium* concept only gradually crystallizing — evidenced by the Fabii's private war against Veii and the *Lapis Satricanus* inscription.
- Sage's sourcebook traces the army's evolution through three stages: the archaic/Servian army organized around the clan (*gens*) as a fighting unit with a hoplite phalanx introduced via Etruscan influence; the manipular army developed in the 4th–3rd centuries BC; and the late-Republic army centered on the cohort as the primary tactical unit.
- Recruitment (the *dilectus*, or levy) depended on the census: Pearson documents that it required written rosters drawn from census *tabulae iuniorum*, legion assignment lists, and oath records to function at the scale Polybius describes — a bureaucratic apparatus underpinning what might otherwise look like an informal, ad hoc levy system.
- Military pay is dated by Sage, following Livy and Diodorus Siculus, to the final war against Veii (406 BC) — introduced not as pay in a modern salaried sense but as compensation enabling the lowest-qualifying census classes to serve, functioning primarily as a manpower-expansion mechanism rather than a plebeian political concession.
- Rosenstein directly challenges the older scholarly view that war-driven conscription dispossessed small Roman and Italian farmers to the benefit of senatorial slave-staffed estates; he argues marriage-timing flexibility, small-scale slaveholding, and women's agricultural labor allowed small farms to survive sustained military service without systemic collapse, despite estimating 34–40% of men serving between 200–168 BC may never have returned.
- Armstrong/Fronda's volume flags a live methodological debate (Gauthier vs. Cadiou) over whether the "Marian reforms" constituted genuine institutional change or merely ad hoc wartime measures — a question the volume's contributors themselves do not resolve. ^[ambiguous]
- Polybius's account of the manipular legion (Histories Book 6) has a documented blind spot, per Sage: he never mentions the cohort as a tactical unit, likely because it was drafted before his firsthand exposure to cohort-based siege warfare in Spain (151/0 BC); archaeological evidence from the Numantia-area camps broadly confirms but also complicates his model. ^[ambiguous]

## Open Questions

- Whether the "professionalization" of the late-Republic army and the emergence of "client armies" loyal to individual generals was a sharp break or a gradual continuation of earlier manpower practices remains genuinely contested across these sources, per the Armstrong/Fronda editors' own acknowledgment.
- The precise administrative mechanics of Mid-Republican record-keeping (legion rosters, pay accounts) are reconstructed by Pearson without any surviving primary documents, so claims about specific document formats remain inferential. ^[ambiguous]
