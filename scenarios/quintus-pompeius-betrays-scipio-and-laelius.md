# Quintus Pompeius's Betrayal of Scipio and Laelius

When Gaius Laelius intended to stand as a candidate for the consulship (ultimately won in 140 BC), his close friend Scipio Aemilianus took it upon himself to clear the path. Scipio directly asked Quintus Pompeius, a "new man" with his own consular ambitions, whether he too planned to run that year.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Scipio Aemilianus** ([[entities/scipio-aemilianus]]) — goal: secure the consulship for his close friend Laelius by removing a potential rival candidate; resources: his enormous personal prestige, which made his direct question to Pompeius hard to brush off.
- **Gaius Laelius** ([[entities/gaius-laelius]]) — goal: win the consulship without a contested race against a friend of his best friend; resources: Scipio's backing.
- **Quintus Pompeius** ([[entities/quintus-pompeius-cos-141bc]]) — goal: secretly pursue his own consular ambitions while avoiding open conflict with Scipio; resources: willingness to give a false assurance.

## What Happened

Pompeius told Scipio he had no intention of standing that year, and further promised to use his own influence to help Laelius's candidacy instead. Scipio, trusting the assurance, presumably reduced his own direct support efforts or assumed the field was effectively cleared. Pompeius then broke his word entirely: he put himself forward as a candidate for the consulship and was elected — defeating or sidelining Laelius in the process.

## Outcome

Scipio was outraged at the deception and permanently withdrew his friendship from Pompeius as a direct result, a rupture significant enough that Cicero cites it generations later in *De Amicitia* as the textbook example of how betrayal in a matter of ambition destroys even a long-standing friendship. The episode is explicitly contrasted in the dialogue with Scipio's separate falling-out with Metellus, which (unlike the Pompeius affair) was merely a political disagreement that never curdled into personal hatred.

## Open Questions

- The exact year and office context (whether this was the consulship of 141 BC specifically) rests on a later commentator's footnote rather than Cicero's own text, which only alludes to the episode in passing.
