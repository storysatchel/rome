# The Matricide Trial of Publicius Malleolus (101 BC)

In 101 BC, a Roman named Publicius Malleolus was tried before the popular assembly (*iudicium populi*) for killing his own mother, and became one of the small number of Republican-era Romans definitely known to have suffered the archaic and gruesome *poena cullei* — execution by being sewn into a leather sack and thrown into the sea (Alexander, case #75).

## Parties & Stakes

- **Publicius Malleolus** ([[entities/publicius-malleolus]]) — defendant, tried and convicted of matricide; executed.
- The Roman People, acting through the *comitia* — as the body trying capital cases of this kind in this period, rather than a standing jury court.

## What Happened

Ancient sources (the *Rhetorica ad Herennium*, Livy's epitome, and Orosius) record only the bare fact: Malleolus was convicted for having killed his mother and suffered the traditional punishment for parricide, which by this period extended to matricide — being sealed inside a sack (originally said to also contain a dog, a cock, a viper, and an ape, though the sources for this trial do not specify the sack's contents) and drowned. No details of his motive, trial proceedings, advocates, or the evidence against him survive.

## Outcome

Malleolus was convicted and executed by *poena cullei* — one of the clearest attested applications of this punishment in the historical (rather than legendary) Republican record, and a reminder that even as standing extortion and other *quaestiones* were becoming the Republic's normal mode of criminal justice, the assembly still occasionally tried and punished capital domestic crimes by older, ritualized methods.

## Open Questions

- Nothing is known of Malleolus's family, his accusers, or the specific evidence that secured his conviction; the case survives purely as a citation for the punishment's historicity. ^[ambiguous]
