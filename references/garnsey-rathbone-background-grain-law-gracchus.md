# The Background to the Grain Law of Gaius Gracchus

## Coverage Note
Landmark economic and historical study by Peter Garnsey and Dominic Rathbone (*The Journal of Roman Studies* 75, 1985, pp. 20–25) investigating the structural context and immediate precipitants of Gaius Gracchus's *Lex Sempronia Frumentaria* of 123 BC.

## Sampled Claims and Economic Analysis
- **Rejection of the Structural Famine Myth:** Challenges the traditional historiographical assumption that Gaius Gracchus's grain law was prompted by chronic, irreversible Italian agricultural collapse or general Mediterranean famine.
- **Specific External Shocks (130s–120s BC):** Demonstrates that Rome's grain market was destabilized by severe, acute supply disruptions:
  1. The First Sicilian Slave War (135–132 BC), which devastated agricultural estates in Sicily, Rome's primary overseas grain supplier.
  2. Severe locust plagues in North Africa in 125 BC, which annihilated cereal harvests and precipitated massive famine across Numidia and coastal Africa.
- **Institutional Market Stabilization:** Argues that the *Lex Sempronia Frumentaria*—which guaranteed Roman citizens a regular monthly allocation of wheat at a fixed subsidized price of 6 1/3 asses per *modius*—was not a demoralizing welfare dole, but an advanced public food security policy designed to eliminate speculative grain-hoarding by private merchants and protect the plebs urbana against extreme harvest price shocks.
