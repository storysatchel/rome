# The Secret Wager Over Fake Witnesses to a Lex Curiata

Cicero's letters preserve a remarkable private "compact" from 54 BC concerning the *lex curiata de imperio* — the formal curiate-assembly ratification that, by custom, confirmed the *imperium* of elected magistrates but could be dispensed with in practice. Appius Claudius Pulcher (consul of 54 BC) and his colleague in the consulship struck a secret bargain with two candidates for the following year's consulship: Memmius and Domitius.

## Parties & Stakes

- **Appius Claudius Pulcher, consul of 54 BC** ([[entities/appius-claudius-pulcher]]) and his unnamed colleague — goal: secure financial guarantees and political cover from the incoming consuls regarding a legally murky lex curiata; resources: outgoing consular authority and the leverage of controlling procedural formalities the incoming consuls would need.
- **Gaius Memmius** ([[entities/gaius-memmius-cos-candidate54]]) and **Domitius** (likely Cn. Domitius Calvinus) ([[entities/gnaeus-domitius-calvinus]]) — candidates for the consulship of 53 BC; goal: secure their election and protect themselves from later challenges over the legality of their predecessors' acts; resources: wealth sufficient to post a cash forfeiture, and political ambition driving the deal.

## What Happened

The two pairs of men entered a private contractual arrangement, recorded by Cicero: if Memmius and Domitius were elected consul, they would be obligated to produce three augurs who would testify (falsely, since no such vote had actually occurred) that Appius and his colleague had properly proposed and carried a lex curiata during their own consulship. Should Memmius and Domitius fail to produce these witnesses after the election, they would forfeit a specified sum of money to Appius and his colleague. ^[extracted] The arrangement reveals that the lex curiata, while not strictly essential to holding elective office, was considered sufficiently advantageous — probably as a shield against future legal challenges to a promagistrate's acts — that outgoing consuls would go to considerable lengths, including suborning false augural testimony, to manufacture its appearance after the fact. ^[inferred]

## Outcome

The direct outcome of the wager (whether the witnesses were in fact produced, or the forfeiture paid) is not recorded in the surviving evidence; the episode's enduring significance lies in exposing how routinely the formal apparatus of Roman public law could be privately gamed by sitting magistrates for mutual political insurance.

## Open Questions

- Whether this scheme was ever carried out, exposed, or penalized remains uncertain. ^[ambiguous]
- The precise identity of Appius's consular colleague in the arrangement is not established here. ^[ambiguous]
