# Julius Canus

Julius Canus was a Roman Stoic philosopher executed under [[entities/caligula|Caligula]]. He is known almost exclusively through Seneca's *De Tranquillitate Animi* (14.4–9), where Seneca celebrates him as an exemplary figure of his own generation whose composure under arbitrary imperial power matched the legendary sages of the past.

## Altercation with Caligula

Seneca introduces Canus as a man of the first rank (*vir in primis magnus*). Following a long dispute with Caligula—the subject of which Seneca does not record—the emperor told him as he departed, "Do not flatter yourself with any silly hope; I have ordered your execution." Canus replied, "Thank you, most excellent prince" (*Gratias ago, optime princeps*). 

Seneca presents three possible readings of the response: a sharp reminder to Caligula that his cruelty had made death a kindness; a taunt directed at an emperor whose subjects were routinely forced to thank him for executing their children and seizing their estates; or sincere relief at being released from Caligula's tyranny.

## The ten-day interval

Canus waited ten days for the sentence to be carried out. When a centurion leading a column of condemned prisoners arrived to summon him, Canus was playing a board game (*latrunculi*, translated as checkers). He counted his pieces before rising and told his opponent, "See that after I am dead you do not falsely claim to have won." Turning to the centurion, he added, "You will bear witness that I am one piece ahead."

When his friends wept at his departure, Canus rebuked them: "Why are you sorrowful? You debate whether souls are immortal; I shall soon know." 

## Final walk and inquiry

On the march to the place of execution, near the tumulus where daily sacrifices were offered to Caesar, his philosophy teacher asked what he was thinking. Canus answered that he intended to observe, at the very moment of transition, whether the soul remained conscious of its departure from the body, and promised that if he learned anything he would return to visit his friends and report on the state of the soul. 

Seneca uses the episode as the climax of his fourteenth chapter to illustrate *tranquillitas*: philosophical inquiry pursued not merely up to death, but through death itself as an empirical test.

## Limits of evidence

Outside Seneca's brief moral portrait, nothing is preserved of Canus's life, philosophical writings, official career, or the specific political cause of his execution. The date of the death is bounded only by Caligula's reign (37–41 AD).
